# Code-Switching and Multilingual Spoken Language Understanding (Indian-Language Emphasis)

## Major established benchmarks/datasets for code-switched speech/text understanding (Indian languages)

### Takeaway
There is a reasonably mature ecosystem of named, citable code-switching resources for Hindi-English (text-level corpora like L3Cube-HingCorpus/HingLID and the GLUECoS benchmark; speech-level corpora like HiACC, HingCos, and the MUCS/Microsoft Research challenge data), plus the newer, very large multilingual IndicVoices corpus that includes code-switched speech across 22 languages. Coverage is strongly skewed toward Hindi-English ("Hinglish"); other Indian code-switched pairs (Bengali-English, Telugu-English, Kannada-English) have smaller, shared-task-scale resources rather than large pretraining-scale corpora.

### Cited Findings
- **GLUECoS** (Microsoft Research, ACL 2020, authors Khanuja, Dandapat, Srinivasan, Sitaram, Choudhury) is the first general-purpose evaluation benchmark for code-switched NLP, spanning 6 tasks / 11 datasets / 8 models across English-Hindi and English-Spanish: Language Identification, POS tagging, Named Entity Recognition, Sentiment Analysis, Question Answering, and Natural Language Inference (NLI). It also released the first code-switched NLI dataset in Hindi-English, built from Bollywood movie dialogues. — [GLUECoS (ACL Anthology)](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.329/), [GLUECoS search summary](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/GLUECoS:-An-Evaluation-Benchmark-for-Code-Switched-Khanuja-Dandapat/00cd2650a89734105fa0c0aba3bf07935b318290)
- GLUECoS found that multilingual models (e.g., multilingual BERT-style models) **fine-tuned on code-switched data** perform best on most tasks across both language pairs, i.e., generic multilingual pretraining plus targeted code-switch fine-tuning beats purpose-built code-switch architectures in their evaluation. — [GLUECoS](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.329/)
- GLUECoS is part of Microsoft Research's broader **Project Melange**, which is explicitly organized around code-switching research for low-resource/multilingual communities, including Indian languages. — [Project Melange — Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/project/melange/)
- **MUCS (Multilingual and Code-Switching ASR Challenge)**, organized by Microsoft Research with partners, covers 7 Indian languages (Hindi, Marathi, Odia, Tamil, Telugu, Gujarati, Bengali) plus two code-switched pairs (Hindi-English and Bengali-English), with ~600 hours of transcribed speech split across a multilingual-ASR subtask and a code-switching-ASR subtask. Baseline recipes achieved WER of 30.73% (multilingual subtask) and 32.45% (code-switching subtask) on test sets. The Hindi-English code-switched data was extracted from spoken technical tutorials, where the switching is largely driven by English technical terminology embedded in Hindi speech. — [MUCS 2021 (Microsoft Research)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/multilingual-and-code-switching-asr-challenges-for-low-resource-indian-languages/), [MUCS arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.00235)
- **L3Cube-HingCorpus** (WILDRE 2022, ACL Anthology) is the first large-scale real Hindi-English code-mixed corpus in Roman script: 52.93M sentences / 1.04B tokens scraped from Twitter. The same project released **HingBERT, HingMBERT, HingRoBERTa, HingGPT** (pretrained on this corpus) and **L3Cube-HingLID**, described as the largest code-mixed Hindi-English language-identification dataset, with a companion production-quality LID model (HingBERT-LID). All resources are openly released on GitHub (l3cube-pune/code-mixed-nlp). — [L3Cube-HingCorpus (WILDRE, ACL Anthology)](https://aclanthology.org/2022.wildre-1.2/), [GitHub repo](https://github.com/l3cube-pune/code-mixed-nlp)
- **HiACC** ("Hinglish Adult & Children Code-switched Corpus") is described as the first code-switched Hinglish *speech* dataset spanning both adult and child speakers: 3,318 audio segments from adults and 1,858 from children, totaling 5.24 hours of read and spontaneous speech. — [HiACC (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12329218/), [HiACC (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352340925006109)
- **HingCos** corpus: 9,251 Hindi-English code-switching utterances (~25 hours of speech), partitioned into train/dev/test sets of 7,015 / 1,152 / 2,136 sentences respectively. — [HingCos reference via search](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.08293)
- **IndicVoices** (AI4Bharat/IIT-Madras): a large natural, spontaneous speech dataset — cited figures vary by source/version: one description gives 7,348 hours from 16,237 speakers across 22 Indian languages including monolingual and code-switched speech; a fuller description gives 23.7K hours (8% read, 76% extempore, 15% conversational) from 51K speakers across 400+ districts and 22 languages, of which 11.2K hours are transcribed. It underlies **IndicASR**, described as the first ASR model covering all 22 Eighth-Schedule Indian languages. A follow-on, **IndicVoices-R**, was published at NeurIPS 2024 Datasets & Benchmarks track. — [IndicVoices (Hugging Face)](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ai4bharat/IndicVoices), [IndicVoices paper (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.01926), [IndicVoices-R (NeurIPS 2024)](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/7dfcaf4512bbf2a807a783b90afb6c09-Paper-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.pdf), [inc42 coverage](https://inc42.com/buzz/iit-madras-ai4bharat-unveils-indicvoices-offers-access-to-7300-hours-of-speech-datasets/)
- **CALCS (Computational Approaches to Linguistic Code-Switching)** workshop series (co-located with NAACL/ACL/EMNLP) is a standing venue specifically for code-switching NLP/speech work; it ran a shared task on **Machine Translation for English→Hinglish (Hindi-English code-mixed)** in 2021, and continues to publish ASR/LID/translation work for code-switched speech (e.g., 2023 papers on unified ASR+LID models and text-derived language-identity incorporation for end-to-end ASR). — [CALCS 2021 MT shared task](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.09625), [CALCS 2021 proceedings](https://aclanthology.org/2021.calcs-1.pdf), [CALCS 2023 proceedings](https://aclanthology.org/2023.calcs-1.pdf)
- **WILDRE (Workshop on Indian Language Data: Resources and Evaluation)**, co-located with LREC/LREC-COLING, is the main standing venue for Indian-language *data resource* papers, including multiple code-mixing corpora. The 7th WILDRE (LREC-COLING 2024) included **EmoMix-3L** (Bangla-English-Hindi code-mixed emotion detection dataset) and a shared task on code-mixed sentiment analysis for less-resourced Indo-Aryan languages; a later WILDRE edition (2026 proceedings listing) includes a **Bengali-English and Hindi-English Code-Mixed Speech Data with Disfluencies** paper, indicating the series' continued focus on speech-level code-mixing resources with realistic disfluency. — [WILDRE-7 proceedings index](http://www.lrec-conf.org/proceedings/lrec-coling-2024/wildre/index.html), [EmoMix-3L](https://aclanthology.org/2024.wildre-1.2/), [WILDRE code-mixed speech w/ disfluencies](https://aclanthology.org/2026.wildre-1.5/)
- **ICON (International Conference on Natural Language Processing)**, the main Indian NLP conference, has hosted a long-running series of code-mixing shared tasks: POS tagging for code-mixed social media text (ICON 2015, Telugu-English, 1,987 sentences), **SAIL_Code-Mixed** sentiment analysis shared task (ICON 2017, Hindi-English and Bengali-English), and word-level language identification for code-mixed Kannada-English (ICON 2022). — [SAIL_Code-Mixed overview](https://arxiv.org/html/1803.06745v1), [ICON 2022 word-level LID](https://aclanthology.org/2022.icon-wlli.5.pdf)
- A 2023 arXiv survey notes "extensive efforts should be made to develop large-scale code-switching corpora for Indian languages" despite the resources above — i.e., even specialists in the area regard current Indian-language code-switching data as still comparatively thin next to high-resource language-pair (e.g. Mandarin-English, Spanish-English) code-switching corpora. — [Multilingual and code-switching ASR challenges for low resource Indian languages (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.00235)

### Inferences
- The Hindi-English resource stack (GLUECoS text tasks + L3Cube text/LID corpora + HiACC/HingCos/MUCS speech corpora) is dense enough to be treated as a *reusable, citable benchmark suite* for a Hindi-English code-switching component; other Indian-language pairs are comparatively under-resourced and mostly exist as shared-task-scale datasets rather than benchmark suites.
- Licensing/availability is mixed: L3Cube resources are openly released on GitHub; MUCS/Microsoft Research challenge data was distributed for the challenge (terms likely tied to challenge participation — not independently verified here); IndicVoices is openly hosted on Hugging Face/GitHub. HiACC/HingCos availability terms were not directly confirmed in sources retrieved (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- Exact licensing terms (open vs. restricted/non-commercial) for HiACC, HingCos, and the MUCS challenge data were not confirmed from sources retrieved in this pass — flag for follow-up before assuming reuse rights.
- No Telugu-English, Tamil-English, or Malayalam-English *speech* (as opposed to text) code-switching corpus of MUCS/IndicVoices scale was found; only smaller text-based shared-task datasets surfaced.

## Architectural/modeling approaches for code-switched spoken language understanding

### Takeaway
The literature shows a clear directional shift away from **language-ID-then-route (cascade) pipelines** toward **end-to-end, unified multilingual models** that handle intra-sentential switches natively, because cascades compound latency/errors and cut words mid-switch at language boundaries; however, end-to-end code-switch ASR remains data-hungry and acoustically harder than monolingual ASR, and is explicitly still described as an active, unsolved research problem rather than a finished solution.

### Cited Findings
- Traditional approach: "many existing bilingual and multilingual ASR systems require a Language Identification (LID) model followed by monolingual ASR," but "LID-based cascade pipelines add compounding latency and cut words mid-switch." — [search synthesis citing multiple CALCS/arXiv papers on LID-routing vs end-to-end](https://aclanthology.org/2023.calcs-1.4.pdf)
- End-to-end multilingual architectures are reported to "handle intra-sentential switches natively without LID routing overhead, reducing WER by up to 55% at language boundaries," and "end-to-end ASR for code-switching has been found to perform better than traditional pipelined methods." — [Towards Code-switching ASR for End-to-end CTC Models (Microsoft Research)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/Towards_code_switched_ASR_for_End_to_End_CTC_models.pdf), [End-to-End Code-Switching ASR for Low-Resourced Language Pairs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.12681)
- More recent "unified" approaches combine ASR and LID in a single model rather than as separate stages — e.g., a 2023 CALCS paper on a "unified model for code-switching speech recognition and language identification based on a concatenated tokenizer," explicitly replacing the need for two separate monolingual models with one bilingual code-switched model. — [Unified Model for Code-Switching Speech Recognition and LID (CALCS 2023)](https://aclanthology.org/2023.calcs-1.7.pdf), [arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.08753)
- Some systems still use LID or code-switch detection as a pre/post-processing step, but this is explicitly flagged as adding "additional complexity ... and increas[ing] processing time" relative to end-to-end alternatives — i.e., the field treats cascades as the older, less-preferred design rather than best practice. — [search synthesis of ASR CS literature](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.08293)
- GLUECoS's own empirical finding (see benchmark section above) — that **generic multilingual models fine-tuned on code-switched data** beat task-specific architectures across most of its 6 tasks — supports a broader modeling takeaway for text/SLU (not just ASR): large multilingual pretraining + targeted code-switch fine-tuning is currently the most robust general-purpose recipe, rather than bespoke code-switch grammars or hand-built switching models. — [GLUECoS](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.329/)
- Newer architecture variants specifically target the switching problem rather than treating it as "more multilingual data": e.g., **Language-Routing Mixture of Experts** for multilingual/code-switching ASR, and methods that **align speech to languages** to improve code-switching recognition — both signal that the open research frontier is building switch-aware internal structure into end-to-end models (expert routing, explicit language-boundary alignment) rather than relying purely on scale. — [Language-Routing MoE for Multilingual and CS ASR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.05956), [Aligning Speech to Languages to Enhance CS ASR](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.05887v2)
- Despite these advances, the literature still frames code-switched ASR as unresolved: "code-switched speech remains a major challenge for ASR systems, and even powerful models like Whisper perform poorly on mixed-language input," with intrasentential code-switching singled out as "the biggest challenge to the development of language and acoustic models for ASR" due to structural complexity, and training-data scarcity remaining "a major challenge in building effective CS ASR systems for most languages." — [search synthesis of CS ASR surveys](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.07741), [ASRU 2019 Mandarin-English CS Challenge overview](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.05916)
- Accent/phonetic confusability is a documented acoustic-level failure mode specific to code-switching: "the accent of the primary language may bias the secondary language in bilingual code-switching speech, making the two languages auditorily similar," which degrades LID even before ASR is attempted. — [search synthesis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.05916)

### Inferences
- For a task-agnostic "conversational-integrity layer," the literature's direction of travel argues against building a hard upfront "detect language, then route to a monolingual pipeline" gate — that pattern is explicitly identified as the weaker, legacy design. A layer that assumes a single detected language per utterance (or per turn) before downstream processing would be reproducing a pattern the ASR/SLU literature has been moving away from.
- The MoE/language-routing and speech-to-language-alignment lines of work suggest the more defensible pattern is: keep a unified, switch-tolerant backbone, but allow internal, soft, continuously-updated language signals (not a one-shot hard decision) to inform processing — i.e., language identity as a running feature/context, not a routing switch.

### Gaps
- No single paper found that benchmarks LID-then-route vs. end-to-end head-to-head specifically on an *Indian-language* code-switched SLU/intent task (most direct head-to-head comparisons cited are from general CS-ASR literature, e.g. Mandarin-English/South African languages, not confirmed as Indian-language-specific experiments). This is an inference carried over from the broader CS-ASR literature rather than an Indian-language-specific finding.

## Regional lexical/dialectal vocabulary vs. code-switching: how the literature treats the boundary

### Takeaway
The literature treats dialect and code-switching as **related but conceptually distinct phenomena under a shared "code"/variety framework** — dialect variation is generally handled as its own strand (dialect identification/classification, dialect-specific language modeling) rather than folded into code-switching research, even though both ultimately require fine-grained identification of "which linguistic variety produced this token/utterance." In practice, Indian-language research treats some dialects (e.g., Bhojpuri, Maithili, Magahi) as data-resource problems closer to "low-resource sister languages" than as a code-switching problem, and separately there is very little direct literature specifically on "regional lexical variation within one standard language" (e.g., regional word-choice differences within standard Hindi) as its own named research area — it mostly surfaces implicitly inside dialect-ID and NER/resource papers for named dialects.

### Cited Findings
- Foundational definitional framing: "Code-switching is the process of shifting from one linguistic code (a language or dialect) to another... In contrast, dialect is the variation of the language with a different form that is very specific to the region where the code-switching style is spoken" — and critically, "practically all work on code-switching has been based on a strict identification between the notions of 'code' and 'linguistic variety,' be that a language, dialect, style, or prosodic register," meaning **dialects are treated theoretically as one type of "code" a speaker can switch between**, but this is a theoretical unification, not evidence that applied NLP systems merge the two problems. — [search synthesis of code-switching/dialect-ID literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code-switching)
- In applied NLP, dialect identification is usually pursued as its own task family (e.g., Arabic Dialect Identification literature, VarDial workshop series) rather than as a subtask of code-switching detection, even though the two are "interconnected" and "both requir[e] sophisticated language identification techniques." The Arabic case is explicitly noted as *complicated by* code-switching and shared vocabulary across dialects — i.e., dialect-ID and code-switch-ID interact and can confound each other, but are still studied as separate literatures. — [Arabic Dialect Identification in the Context of Bivalency and Code-Switching](https://aclanthology.org/L18-1573.pdf), [Multilingual Identification of English Code-Switching (VarDial 2024)](https://aclanthology.org/2024.vardial-1.14.pdf)
- For Indian languages specifically, **Bhojpuri, Maithili, and Magahi** are treated in the literature as low-resource languages/dialects in their own right, with dedicated resource-building papers (e.g., a Named Entity Recognition dataset and deep-learning baseline for all three, and a separate paper computing similarity estimates between them and baselines for three applications) — i.e., the research response to "dialect/regional variety" is to build dedicated monolingual(ish) resources and models for the variety, not to treat it as code-switching with Hindi. — [NER dataset/baseline for Bhojpuri, Maithili, Magahi (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.06451), [Linguistic resources & similarity estimates for Bhojpuri, Magahi, Maithili (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.13945)
- At the same time, officially/administratively, "Bhojpuri and Magahi are considered 'sub-languages' (or dialects) of Hindi... though Bhojpuri lacks official recognition as a scheduled language because it is classified by the government as a dialect of Hindi" — showing that even the language-vs-dialect boundary for these varieties is a contested classification question, not a settled linguistic fact, which directly affects how NLP resources are framed (as "Hindi dialect" work vs. "distinct low-resource language" work). — [Bhojpuri language (Wikipedia, citing linguistic/administrative sources)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhojpuri_language)
- There is direct work on **dialect classification as distinct from language/code-switch identification**: a paper titled "Dialect Classification for Hindi and Bhojpuri Languages" builds an end-to-end system to identify/classify Hindi vs. Bhojpuri dialects from speech signal information, explicitly exploring **intonational differences** between dialects as a identification feature — i.e., dialect-ID here is pursued via prosodic/acoustic cues, a different technical approach than token-level code-switch LID (which is lexical/textual). — [Dialect Classification for Hindi and Bhojpuri Languages (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-96-5754-4_6)
- Broader regional-dialect text resources exist outside Hindi too — e.g., **HinDialect** (a folk-song corpus spanning 26 Hindi-related dialects) and **VACASPATI** (115M-sentence Bengali literature corpus sampled across West Bengal and Bangladesh specifically to capture regional lexical differences) — both framed as dialect/regional-variation resources, separately from any code-switching corpus. — [search synthesis citing HinDialect/VACASPATI via BhashaSutra survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.18423)
- Dialectal variation is shown to directly degrade downstream NLU/slot-filling performance in other language families (German), which the literature uses as a general warning that "dialect-specific corpora and evaluation benchmarks are essential to avoid biasing models toward standard varieties" — a general principle invoked for South Asian languages too but with less direct empirical slot-filling evidence specifically for Indian-language dialects than for German. — [Improving Dialectal Slot and Intent Detection with Auxiliary Tasks: Bavarian case study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.03863)
- Lexical borrowing (loanwords) is studied as a third, related-but-distinct phenomenon from both code-switching and dialect variation: borrowing is a property of the *lexicon* (words absorbed into a language over time, driven by "need" for new concepts or "prestige" of the donor language) rather than a property of a given utterance's language mixture. A 2025 multilingual evaluation found that "general-purpose LLMs consistently fail to distinguish borrowed from native vocabulary" across 10 languages, which is a direct, documented failure mode relevant to any system that must decide whether a word is "foreign" (code-switch) or simply "borrowed/naturalized" vocabulary within the matrix language. — [Are Language Models Borrowing-Blind? (arXiv, 2025)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.26254), [Lexical borrowing concepts and issues](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/279973916_Lexical_borrowing_concepts_and_issues)

### Inferences
- For the "conversational-integrity layer," the literature supports a three-way conceptual split rather than a binary one: (1) **code-switching proper** — alternation between named languages within/across utterances, studied with token-level LID and CS-ASR techniques; (2) **dialect/regional-variety variation** — treated as its own identification problem (often prosodic/acoustic for spoken dialect-ID, or resource-building for text), closer to a "which variety of this language family" classification than a "which language is this word" classification; (3) **lexical borrowing/naturalized loanwords** — a lexicon-level phenomenon where a "foreign-origin" word is not actually a language switch at all, and current models (including LLMs) are documented to struggle at telling this apart from genuine code-switching or dialect-specific vocabulary.
- Because official/administrative language-vs-dialect classification (e.g., "Is Bhojpuri a dialect of Hindi or its own language?") is contested and policy-driven rather than purely linguistic, a conversational-integrity layer probably should not hard-code a single taxonomy of "language vs. dialect" for Indian varieties — the research literature itself does not have a single resolved answer, and treats it query-by-query (NER-resource papers treat Bhojpuri as a language; government/administrative framing treats it as a Hindi dialect).

### Gaps
- No paper was found that directly and empirically studies "regional lexical variation within standard Hindi" (i.e., different regions of Hindi-speaking India using different everyday words for the same concept, short of full dialect shift) as a named, dedicated SLU robustness problem. Most discoverable work frames regional variation either as (a) full-dialect-identification (Bhojpuri/Maithili/Magahi as distinct varieties) or (b) social-media-text dialect corpora (HinDialect, VACASPATI, BanglaDial) — not as an intent/slot-understanding robustness study for a standard-language voice assistant encountering regional word choice. This looks like a genuine literature gap rather than a search-coverage failure, and should be flagged explicitly to the report writer.

## ASR/SLU for regional Indian dialects (Bhojpuri, Maithili, etc.) through a standard-language (Hindi) recognizer

### Takeaway
There is a small but direct body of work benchmarking standard-language (Hindi) ASR/recognizers against regional-dialect speech (Bhojpuri, Maithili), consistently finding substantially higher error rates than clean standard Hindi, and the literature documents concrete transformation/failure patterns — closest-sound substitution, morphological/suffix substitution errors, and model hallucination/insertion loops — though most of this evidence is industry-blog/benchmark-level rather than deeply peer-reviewed linguistic analysis of *why* specific dialectal forms fail.

### Cited Findings
- Dialect-specific language modeling is shown to directly improve ASR: a paper titled "Improving Automatic Speech Recognition with Dialect-Specific Language Models" explicitly targets this gap, implying that a single standard-language (non-dialect-aware) LM is a documented source of error for dialectal input. — [Improving ASR with Dialect-Specific Language Models (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-48309-7_5)
- A benchmark evaluation of Indian-language ASR systems reports that the best-performing system tested ("Sarvam Audio") still exceeds a 20% WER threshold (considered the boundary of "practical usability") specifically on **Bhojpuri (20.9% WER) and Maithili (24.8% WER)**, while clean standard Hindi sits lower (roughly 8–18% WER depending on system), and Hindi-English code-switched telephony audio runs 18–28% WER on major global platforms — i.e., regional dialects (Bhojpuri/Maithili) are empirically *harder* for current systems than either clean standard Hindi or Hindi-English code-switching. — [search synthesis of Indian ASR benchmarking coverage](https://deepgram.com/learn/why-wer-fails-indian-languages-bridge-7-metric-framework)
- Documented failure modes for Indic ASR under non-standard input (code-switch and, by extension, dialectal mismatch) include: (1) **closest-sound substitution** — "the language model has no statistical basis for assigning [a non-standard word] a high probability. It will often substitute the closest-sounding [standard-language] word," or alternatively flags it as unrecognizable and produces a deletion error; (2) **morphological/suffix substitution** — "if your ASR correctly identifies the root morpheme but attaches the wrong case suffix, WER counts it as a full word substitution," the same penalty as a totally wrong word — relevant because dialectal Hindi-family varieties often diverge from standard Hindi primarily in suffixes/case-markers and function words rather than core vocabulary; (3) **hallucination/insertion loops** — some systems show very high insertion rates (one system cited at 39.15% in Hindi, 36.87% in English) attributed to the model "repeating... fluent but inaccurate text based on their internal language model rather than the audio when they are unsure," a risk likely amplified when the input dialect is out-of-distribution for the recognizer's training data. — [search synthesis of ASR failure-mode analysis](https://deepgram.com/learn/why-wer-fails-indian-languages-bridge-7-metric-framework)
- Classification-first alternative: rather than running dialectal speech through a standard recognizer and hoping for graceful degradation, some researchers instead build a **dialect classifier as a front-end** — the Hindi/Bhojpuri dialect classification system noted above uses **intonational (prosodic) differences** as the discriminating signal, implying that acoustic/prosodic cues can carry dialect identity even when lexical content is similar enough to confuse a text-driven recognizer. — [Dialect Classification for Hindi and Bhojpuri Languages (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-96-5754-4_6)
- Lower-resource-language framing: Bhojpuri, Maithili, and Magahi are treated in parallel NER/resource literature as needing their *own* NLP pipelines (tokenizers, NER, baselines) rather than being adequately served by a Hindi pipeline — reinforcing that the applied-research consensus is "build dedicated resources," not "rely on Hindi recognizer robustness." — [NER dataset/baseline for Bhojpuri, Maithili, Magahi](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.06451)

### Inferences
- The combination of (a) consistently higher WER for Bhojpuri/Maithili than for standard Hindi even on the *best* current systems, and (b) the specific failure-mode taxonomy above (closest-sound substitution, suffix-substitution penalties, hallucination loops) together suggest that a conversational-integrity layer handling Indian-dialect speech through a standard-Hindi (or similar) recognizer should expect **systematic, patterned** errors concentrated at (i) function words/case-suffixes, and (ii) low-confidence spans where the recognizer may hallucinate plausible-sounding standard-language text rather than failing cleanly — both of which argue for confidence-aware downstream handling rather than trusting transcript text at face value for dialectal speakers.

### Gaps
- The most specific numeric findings here (Sarvam Audio WER on Bhojpuri/Maithili, insertion-rate percentages) come from industry blog/benchmark sources (Deepgram, Sarvam, Gnani) rather than peer-reviewed papers; WebFetch access to these exact source pages was blocked by network policy in this research pass, so these figures are reported only via search-engine synthesis and could not be independently cross-checked against the primary blog text. Treat these numbers as plausible but not independently verified — recommend the report writer flag them as "industry-benchmark-sourced, unverified against primary text" if precision matters.
- No peer-reviewed paper was found giving a linguistically detailed account of *which specific Bhojpuri/Maithili lexical or phonological forms* get mis-transcribed by a Hindi ASR system (e.g., specific word pairs or sound shifts) — the failure-mode taxonomy found is general (applies to code-switching and OOV handling broadly) rather than Bhojpuri/Maithili-specific linguistic documentation.

## What is genuinely hard/unsolved vs. largely solved/commodity in code-switched SLU

### Takeaway
**Sentence-level / document-level monolingual language identification is considered a solved, commodity task** (near-100% accuracy with simple n-gram/stopword methods on clean monolingual text). By contrast, **token-level / intra-sentential code-switch LID, code-switch ASR (especially intrasentential), and genuinely robust code-switched SLU/NLU remain open, actively-researched problems** — current token-level LID systems reach only "mid-90s" accuracy at best and degrade further on accented/acoustic input, and code-switch ASR is explicitly still described in 2023-2025 literature as a major unsolved challenge even for strong general models like Whisper.

### Cited Findings
- "Identifying the language of a text as one of the given languages is considered to be a solved task, with simple n-gram approaches, character encoding detection or stop word lists leading to recognition accuracy of up to 100% on benchmark data sets" — but this applies only when "the input text is encoded in exactly one language." — [search synthesis of token-level LID literature](https://nejlt.ep.liu.se/article/download/5725/5326/28872)
- Once switching happens *within* a sentence (the norm for natural code-switched speech), the task becomes fundamentally different and harder: "code-switching reduces the minimum unit of detection to a token," and token-level LID research systems, while outperforming monolingual-trained identifiers, "reach accuracies in the mid-90s" — notably below the near-100% ceiling of monolingual LID, and this is before acoustic/accent confounds are introduced. General-purpose off-the-shelf libraries (Spacy, pycld2, Lingua) perform even worse at the token level, "primarily due to a lack of context, as most libraries work with sentences rather than individual words." — [search synthesis, Challenges of Computational Processing of Code-Switching (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.02213), [Subword-Level LID for Intra-Word Code-Switching](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334601504_Subword-Level_Language_Identification_for_Intra-Word_Code-Switching)
- Spoken (acoustic, not just textual) LID for code-switching is harder still: "achieving language identification in accented speech remains [a] challenge" because "the accent of the primary language may bias the secondary language in bilingual code-switching speech, making the two languages auditorily similar." — [search synthesis citing ASRU 2019 CS Challenge overview](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.05916)
- Code-switched ASR itself is repeatedly and explicitly flagged across multiple recent sources (2023–2025) as unsolved: "code-switched speech remains a major challenge for ASR systems, and even powerful models like Whisper perform poorly on mixed-language input"; intrasentential code-switching specifically "poses the biggest challenge to the development of language and acoustic models for ASR... due to its inherent structural complexity"; and "limited availability of large-scale CS training data... remains a major challenge in building effective CS ASR systems for most languages." — [Code-Switching in End-to-End ASR: A Systematic Literature Review (arXiv, 2025)](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.07741v1), [ASRU 2019 Mandarin-English CS Challenge](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.05916)
- Beyond ASR, a 2023 position paper ("Representativeness as a Forgotten Lesson for Multilingual and Code-switched Data Collection and Preparation") argues that **despite recent advances in massive multilingual language models, there has still not been much progress in building successful code-switching systems** — attributing this specifically to data representativeness problems (how CS data is collected and curated), not just model architecture — suggesting that the open problem is as much a *data methodology* problem as a modeling one. — [search synthesis citing this EMNLP Findings 2023 paper](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Sunayana-Sitaram)
- Loanword/borrowing disambiguation is a newly-documented, specific open problem adjacent to code-switching: general-purpose LLMs "consistently fail to distinguish borrowed from native vocabulary" even across widely-studied languages, as of a 2025 multilingual evaluation across 10 languages — implying that even state-of-the-art LLM-based systems cannot reliably tell "this is a code-switch" apart from "this is just a naturalized loanword in the matrix language," which is directly relevant to any system trying to decide whether to treat a word as a language-boundary event. — [Are Language Models Borrowing-Blind? (arXiv, 2025)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.26254)

### Inferences
- The "solved vs. open" line in this literature is not language-switching vs. no-switching, but rather **granularity and modality**: whole-utterance/whole-document LID on clean text is commodity technology; everything that requires deciding language identity *per token*, *per acoustic frame*, or *in the presence of accent/dialect/borrowing ambiguity* is still an active research area with meaningfully imperfect (mid-90s-or-lower accuracy, high WER) current performance. A conversational-integrity layer should therefore budget for ongoing uncertainty at language *boundaries within* an utterance and when distinguishing switches from borrowings/dialect forms, even if it can treat "which language is the dominant language of this whole utterance" as a comparatively reliable signal.
- The explicit framing that **data representativeness/collection methodology**, not just model scale, is identified as a root cause of stalled progress is a notable, non-obvious finding worth flagging to the broader report synthesis — it implies "more data" or "bigger multilingual model" alone is not expected (per this 2023 position paper) to resolve the hard cases, and that the problem is partly about *how* code-switched training data is gathered/curated.
