# Noise-Robust ASR and Conversational Acoustic-Degradation Benchmarks

## What are CHiME-4 and CHiME-5 specifically? What acoustic conditions do they cover, and what tasks/annotations do they provide? Why is CHiME-5 particularly relevant as a "realistic" acoustic reference?

### Takeaway
CHiME-4 is a single/multi-microphone tablet-device ASR task built on read WSJ0 sentences re-recorded/mixed with four everyday noise types (street, pedestrian area, cafe, bus) — i.e., controlled, single-speaker, read-speech noise robustness. CHiME-5 is a fundamentally different and more relevant benchmark for "realistic" degradation: it is a fully transcribed corpus of unscripted, multi-party, far-field conversational speech recorded in real homes, with naturally occurring overlap, reverberation, and genuine domestic noise (not synthetically injected), making it the closest existing analogue to messy real-world conversational audio.

### Cited Findings
- CHiME-4's scenario is ASR for a single- or multi-microphone tablet device used in everyday noisy environments, with 1/2/6-channel microphone array data and **real acoustic mixing** (talkers speaking in challenging noisy environments) — [chimechallenge.org via search](https://www.chimechallenge.org/challenges/chime4/overview)
- CHiME-4 covers four noisy recording environments: street (STR), pedestrian area (PED), cafe (CAF), bus (BUS); training used 1600 real utterances from 4 speakers in these environments plus 7138 simulated noisy utterances (WSJ0 read sentences + additive noise from the same four environments); dev set = 410 utterances × 4 environments × {real, simulated} = 3280 utterances; eval set = 2640 utterances — [arXiv building state-of-the-art distant ASR CHiME-4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.10109)
- CHiME-4 utterances are provided in continuous audio with ground-truth VAD annotations — [search result citing chime4 docs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.10109)
- CHiME-4's audio/annotations are distributed only via LDC as part of the CHiME-3 package (LDC2017S24); accessing it requires an LDC license, and it is built on WSJ0 (CSR-I), so a WSJ0 LDC license is effectively also required — [LDC catalog / chimechallenge download search](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/LDC2017S24)
- CHiME-5 targets "distant multi-microphone conversational ASR in real home environments," aiming to advance robust ASR at the intersection of speech/language processing, signal processing, and ML — [Barker et al., Interspeech 2018, CHiME-5 dataset/task/baselines paper](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2018/barker18_interspeech.pdf)
- The CHiME-5 dataset consists of 20 real dinner parties of 2–3 hours each, 4 participants per party wearing binaural microphones, each party in a different home, with 3 recording locations per home (kitchen, dining room, living room) — [arXiv 1803.10609, CHiME-5 dataset/task/baselines](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.10609)
- Recordings include natural multi-speaker conversation in reverberant, noisy environments and are fully transcribed (human reference transcripts exist, not just WER scoring against hidden text) — [arXiv 1803.10609](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.10609)
- Breakdown of overlap in CHiME-5 audio: 22% contains only noise, 51% one single active speaker, 20% two overlapping speakers, 5% three overlapping speakers, 2% four overlapping speakers — [arXiv 1803.10609](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.10609)
- CHiME-5 features real domestic background noises (kitchen appliances, air conditioning, movement, etc.), distinguishing it from synthetic noise-injection corpora like CHiME-4 — [arXiv 1803.10609](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.10609)
- As of January 1, 2024, CHiME-5 was re-issued under a CC BY-SA 4.0 license and is now free for both academic and commercial use (previously required a signed agreement), downloadable via myairbridge.com — [chimechallenge.org download page via search](https://www.chimechallenge.org/challenges/chime5/download)

### Inferences
- CHiME-4 and CHiME-5 represent two different points on a difficulty/realism axis: CHiME-4 is "clean scripted speech + environmental noise overlay" (closer to a synthetic noise-injection design even though some mixing is real-world captured), while CHiME-5 is "genuinely messy conversational speech recorded in situ" — this makes CHiME-5 the stronger reference for benchmarking a conversational-integrity layer against *realistic* degradation rather than additive-noise robustness alone.
- CHiME-5's full human transcripts plus documented overlap/noise statistics make it directly reusable: a system's output could be compared turn-by-turn against ground truth under known degrees of speaker overlap (0/1/2/3/4 speakers active), giving a natural "difficulty tier" ladder keyed to overlap count rather than to an arbitrary SNR label.
- CHiME-4's LDC/WSJ0 gating is a meaningful adoption cost; CHiME-5/6's post-2024 CC BY-SA 4.0 re-release removes that friction and is likely the more practical corpus to adapt for an external research/benchmark pipeline.

- Did not verify whether human transcripts for CHiME-4 include disfluency/false-start annotation beyond VAD segmentation (read speech from WSJ0, so disfluencies are likely minimal by construction).

## What came after CHiME-5 (CHiME-6, CHiME-7, CHiME-8)? What did later challenges add (diarization, more realistic overlap, different domains)?

### Takeaway
The CHiME series evolved from CHiME-5 (ASR-only on real home dinner parties) to CHiME-6 (added mandatory joint diarization+ASR track, denser microphone array), to CHiME-7/8 "DASR" (generalization across heterogeneous device geometries and multiple domains: dinner parties, lab dinner-party recordings, interview/telephone calls, and — by CHiME-8 — corporate/office meetings), reflecting a shift from single-domain realism toward cross-domain generalization and joint diarization-transcription evaluation.

### Cited Findings
- CHiME-6 re-uses the CHiME-5 real dinner-party recordings (4 participants, informal speech, low SNR) but adds distant speech captured by 6 Kinect array devices with 4 microphones each (24 array mics) plus the 8 binaural mics (2×4), for 32 microphones per session total — [search result synthesizing CHiME-6 docs](https://chimechallenge.github.io/chime6/track1_data.html)
- CHiME-6 Track 2 requires systems to produce both diarization and ASR output jointly (unlike CHiME-5, where oracle diarization segments could be used for the ASR task); the primary metric for this joint track is cpWER (concatenated-minimum-permutation/speaker-attributed WER), which counts both diarization/speaker-attribution errors and word-recognition errors — [arXiv 2004.09249, CHiME-6 Challenge paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.09249)
- CHiME-7 (ran Feb 7 – Jun 16, 2023) had two sub-tasks: (1) DASR — joint diarization + distant ASR across multiple, possibly heterogeneous, recording devices, evaluated on three diverse scenarios: CHiME-6 (real dinner parties), DiPCo (lab-recorded dinner-party scenario), and Mixer 6 (interview/telephone-call sessions) — explicitly targeting generalization across array geometries and use cases with no a-priori device information; (2) UDASE — unsupervised domain adaptation for conversational speech enhancement, learning to suppress noise using out-of-domain labeled + in-domain unlabeled data — [arXiv 2306.13734, CHiME-7 DASR paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.13734) and [arXiv 2307.03533, CHiME-7 UDASE task paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.03533)
- DiPCo (Dinner Party Corpus, Amazon): simulates a dinner-party scenario in an everyday home with 4 Amazon-employee volunteers per group having natural conversation around a dining table; recorded with one close-talk mic plus five far-field 7-microphone array devices at different room locations; 10 sessions, 15–45 minutes each, with human-labeled transcripts; released as a public research/benchmarking dataset specifically to advance noise-robust/distant speech processing and address the "cocktail party"/"dinner party" problem — [arXiv 1909.13447, DiPCo paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.13447) and [Amazon Science blog](https://www.amazon.science/blog/amazon-releases-new-public-data-set-to-help-address-cocktail-party-problem)
- Mixer 6 corpus: 2-speaker sessions at 16kHz, recorded by 10 different far-field devices plus 3 close-talk devices; each session has an interview segment (interviewer + subject), a telephone call, and a short prompt-reading segment; sessions ~15 minutes — [search result on CHiME-8/Mixer 6](https://www.isca-archive.org/chime_2024/cornell24_chime.pdf)
- CHiME-8 DASR (2024) carries forward CHiME-7 DASR, adding: (i) NOTSOFAR-1, a new office/corporate-meeting scenario domain, (ii) a manually corrected Mixer 6 development set, (iii) a new track permitting large language models, and (iv) a jury-award mechanism to encourage practical/innovative solutions — [arXiv 2407.16447, CHiME-8 DASR paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.16447)
- NOTSOFAR-1 (office meeting scenario used in CHiME-8 Task 2): 315 recordings of office meetings among 32 unique participants across 30 different meeting rooms, 4–8 speakers per meeting, most "jumpstarted"/guided by a professional actor around a given topic; captured by up to 7 commercially available far-field array devices (4 tabletop circular 7-mic devices + 3 linear array devices), with close-talk headset devices available for train/dev splits (low cross-talk) — [search result on NOTSOFAR-1/CHiME-8](https://www.isca-archive.org/chime_2024/cornell24_chime.pdf)
- As of the research date (Oct 2026), CHiME-8 (2024) is the most recent fully documented challenge found; no evidence of a CHiME-9 or later iteration was found in search results — [multiple search results, no CHiME-9 found]

### Inferences
- The progression CHiME-5 → 6 → 7 → 8 moves along two axes relevant to a "difficulty ladder": (a) task complexity (ASR-only → joint diarization+ASR with cpWER) and (b) domain/device generalization (single home-dinner domain → multiple domains: home dinner party, lab dinner party, interview/telephone call, and office meeting — each with different device geometries). This makes the CHiME-7/8 DASR scenario set (CHiME-6 + DiPCo + Mixer 6 [+ NOTSOFAR-1 in CHiME-8]) a ready-made multi-domain "hard tier" battery, each sub-corpus with human transcripts and documented recording conditions.
- Mixer 6's inclusion of an actual telephone-call segment per session is notable: it is one of relatively few modern "realistic overlap/far-field" benchmarks that also contains a telephony-channel segment, potentially bridging the CHiME line with the telephone-speech corpora (Fisher/CALLHOME) called out below.

### Gaps
- Could not determine exact licensing terms for DiPCo (appears on Zenodo and HuggingFace, suggesting open access) vs. Mixer 6 and NOTSOFAR-1 (likely LDC-gated or challenge-registration-gated, as Mixer 6 is historically an LDC corpus) — this needs explicit confirmation before assuming reuse rights.
- Could not confirm whether CHiME-8's "LLM-permitted track" published any standardized score comparing LLM-based systems to earlier conventional cascaded systems — potentially useful comparison data but not found in the search snippets retrieved.

## What is the AMI Meeting Corpus, and what conversational/acoustic phenomena does it capture?

### Takeaway
AMI is a ~100-hour multi-party meeting corpus recorded with synchronized close-talking and far-field microphones (plus video and whiteboard/slide capture), manually transcribed at the word level with rich discourse annotation, and documented to have a substantial natural-overlap proportion (~24.7%) — making it a strong, freely available source for far-field/natural-overlap "meeting-style" degradation, distinct from CHiME's domestic-noise focus.

### Cited Findings
- The AMI Meeting Corpus consists of 100 hours of meeting recordings, captured with close-talking and far-field microphones, individual and room-view video cameras, and output from a slide projector and an electronic whiteboard — [search summary of AMI Corpus Overview](https://groups.inf.ed.ac.uk/ami/corpus/overview.shtml)
- The corpus includes high-quality, manually produced orthographic transcription for each individual speaker with word-level timings, plus a wide range of other annotations covering linguistic phenomena (discourse properties such as named entities and dialogue acts, summaries, emotions) and non-linguistic behaviors (head and hand gestures) — [search summary of AMI overview / pre-announcement paper](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/bitstreams/f737e755-8723-469d-837e-038d69a61d8d/download)
- The AMI corpus has an approximate 24.7% overlap proportion, indicating that speaker overlap is systematically tracked/measurable in the corpus — [search synthesis citing joint speech/overlap detection benchmark paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.13012)
- The corpus was originally delivered to LDC, but the AMI Meeting Corpus and the related ICSI Corpus have since been released publicly under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International license (CC BY 4.0), allowing copy/distribute/display for any purpose provided the AMI project is credited — [groups.inf.ed.ac.uk AMI download page via search](https://groups.inf.ed.ac.uk/ami/download/)
- AMI signal and annotation downloads are provided directly (not gated behind a paid LDC license) at the official AMI corpus download page, with annotations split into a manual-annotations zip and an automatically-derived-data zip — [groups.inf.ed.ac.uk AMI download page via search](https://groups.inf.ed.ac.uk/ami/download/)

### Inferences
- AMI's free CC BY 4.0 license plus full manual transcripts makes it the lowest-friction "realistic multi-party meeting" benchmark of all those reviewed — more accessible than most CHiME tasks pre-2024, and (unlike CHiME-4) genuinely spontaneous/conversational rather than read speech.
- AMI is best suited as a "far-field, natural multi-party overlap, meeting-room acoustics" tier — a different acoustic regime from CHiME-5/6's domestic noise or CHiME-4's street/cafe environmental noise, so it is complementary rather than redundant with the CHiME line in a difficulty ladder.

### Gaps
- Could not find a specific SNR or reverberation-time (RT60) characterization for AMI's far-field channel from these searches — would need a direct read of the AMI technical/corpus papers for acoustic-condition parameters.

## Are there well-known spontaneous telephone-speech corpora beyond Switchboard relevant to acoustic/channel degradation (e.g., Fisher, CALLHOME)?

### Takeaway
Fisher and CALLHOME are the two major LDC-cataloged conversational telephone-speech corpora beyond Switchboard; both are narrowband (8kHz, telephone-network-limited) and carry real channel artifacts (noise, echo, distortion, handset/network variation), with Fisher being far larger (2000+ hours, 16,000+ conversations) and explicitly designed to overcome the narrow-vocabulary/small-speaker-count limitations of CALLHOME, while CALLHOME conversations are described as unusually natural/intimate given small numbers of speakers on long calls.

### Cited Findings
- The Fisher corpus consists of more than 16,000 English conversations collected as conversational telephone speech, totaling over 2,000 hours of two-channel English telephone conversation — [LDC/LREC Fisher corpus paper via search](https://www.ldc.upenn.edu/sites/www.ldc.upenn.edu/files/lrec2004-fisher-corpus.pdf)
- The Fisher collection protocol was created at LDC specifically to address robust-ASR developers' need for large, broad-vocabulary conversational telephone data, after CALLFRIEND/Switchboard-II (adapted from language/speaker-ID collection designs) proved limiting for ASR — [search synthesis of From Switchboard to Fisher paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221483849_From_Switchboard_to_Fisher_Telephone_collection_protocols_their_uses_and_yields)
- CALLHOME protocol/corpora, while designed to support ASR, feature small numbers of speakers making relatively long telephone calls with narrower vocabulary; CALLHOME conversations are described as "challengingly natural and intimate" — [search synthesis of same paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221483849_From_Switchboard_to_Fisher_Telephone_collection_protocols_their_uses_and_yields)
- Telephone speech channel degradation characteristics include low bandwidth, noise, echo, distortion, handset/network differences, and speaker variation; Fisher recordings are noted as significantly noisier than comparison datasets with variable channel characteristics — [search synthesis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221483849_From_Switchboard_to_Fisher_Telephone_collection_protocols_their_uses_and_yields)
- Fisher audio is delivered as 2-channel mu-law encoded, 8000 samples/second (narrowband, as captured from the public telephone network) — e.g., Fisher Spanish Speech, LDC2010S01 — [LDC catalog, Fisher Spanish Speech](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/LDC2010S01)
- The Fisher and CALLHOME Spanish-English corpora have also been packaged specifically for speech translation research, with transcripts available (e.g., the Fisher-CALLHOME Spanish-English speech translation corpus on GitHub) — [GitHub, fisher-callhome-corpus](https://github.com/joshua-decoder/fisher-callhome-corpus)

### Inferences
- Fisher/CALLHOME represent the "telephony channel effects" rung of an acoustic-difficulty ladder distinct from CHiME/AMI's far-field/overlap-driven degradation: their difficulty comes from narrowband encoding, codec/handset artifacts, and network noise rather than room acoustics or multi-speaker overlap — a useful, qualitatively different degradation type for a general voice-AI robustness ladder (clean → far-field/overlap → telephony-channel → combined).
- Both corpora are standard LDC-licensed (gated/paid) products, consistent with most classic LDC speech corpora; this is a real access cost versus CHiME-5/6 (now CC BY-SA 4.0) or AMI (CC BY 4.0).

### Gaps
- Could not confirm current LDC catalog numbers/pricing for Fisher English or CALLHOME English specifically (only Fisher Spanish's catalog page, LDC2010S01, was directly surfaced) — exact access terms for the English-language Fisher/CALLHOME sets should be verified directly on the LDC catalog before any reuse decision.
- Did not find explicit documentation of a standardized "difficulty tier" framework within Fisher/CALLHOME themselves (these are raw data collections, not benchmark challenges with graded conditions) — any tiering would need to be constructed by the reusing researcher (e.g., based on SNR estimation or call-quality metadata if present).

## Are there established multilingual/code-switching speech benchmarks with acoustic noise/degradation conditions (not just clean multilingual speech)? Any India-specific work?

### Takeaway
Most prominent multilingual/code-switching speech resources (CS-FLEURS, IITG-HingCoS, HiACC) are oriented toward linguistic/text coverage and read or lightly spontaneous speech rather than systematically graded acoustic degradation; a small number of India-specific corpora (IITG-HingCoS from IIT Guwahati, VITB-HEBiC) explicitly claim "natural, noise-rich" recording environments, but none of the sources found define formal noise/SNR difficulty tiers the way CHiME or Speech Robust Bench do. A separate, non-multilingual-specific benchmark, Speech Robust Bench (SRB, ICLR 2025), is the clearest example found of a graded, multi-severity-level acoustic/digital-degradation benchmark and is a strong candidate for the "difficulty ladder" framing itself, independent of language.

### Cited Findings
- IITG-HingCoS (IIT Guwahati) is a Hinglish (Hindi-English) code-switching database for ASR; it contains 25,988 code-switching sentences (~0.58 million words) and 9,251 Hindi-English code-switching utterances corresponding to about 25 hours of speech — [ScienceDirect, IITG-HingCoS paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167639318304217)
- HiACC (Hinglish Adult & Children Code-switched Corpus) is a benchmark Hinglish speech corpus aimed at improving ASR in resource-constrained settings; it comprises 3,318 audio segments from adults and 1,858 from children, totaling 5.24 hours of read and spontaneous speech — [ScienceDirect/PMC, HiACC paper](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12329218/)
- VITB-HEBiC is a Hindi-English code-switching corpus built from 137 speakers across 27 Indian states, explicitly recorded in "natural, noise-rich environments" for ASR development — [ScienceDirect, VITB-HEBiC paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0045790624009030)
- CS-FLEURS (Interspeech 2025) is a massively multilingual, code-switched speech dataset built from FLEURS-derived text, covering 52 languages and 113 unique code-switched language pairs, with human-read and synthetic (XTTS/MMS-TTS) speech; its test data splits into Read, XTTS1, XTTS2, and MMS subsets — [arXiv 2509.14161, CS-FLEURS paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14161)
- CS-FLEURS's "noise handling" is about suppressing unwanted synthesis/recording artifacts (using the denoised FLEURS-R base and filtering frequencies outside 80Hz–7000Hz to avoid buzzing/crackling), not about modeling realistic environmental noise conditions as a benchmark axis — [arXiv 2509.14161](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14161)
- Speech Robust Bench (SRB) is a robustness benchmark for ASR comprising 114 input perturbations across six categories: clean speech, social gatherings, speech variations, environmental effects, digital augmentations, and adversarial attacks, with multiple severity levels per perturbation designed to be applied in increasing order of difficulty — [arXiv 2403.07937, Speech Robust Bench paper (ICLR 2025)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.07937)
- SRB reports results using WER plus Word Error Rate Degradation (WERD) and a normalized version (NWERD) to quantify robustness degradation specifically, rather than only absolute WER — [arXiv 2403.07937](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.07937)

### Inferences
- There is a clear gap in the literature surfaced by this research: no benchmark found combines (a) multilingual/code-switching speech content with (b) a systematically graded acoustic-noise difficulty ladder in the way CHiME or SRB do for monolingual/English content. CS-FLEURS and the Indian code-switching corpora address linguistic coverage; CHiME/SRB address acoustic difficulty; a "Boli"-style conversational-integrity benchmark that wants both axes (language mixing AND graded acoustic degradation) would likely need to construct this combination itself, e.g. by applying SRB-style perturbations or CHiME-style real noise overlays on top of an existing Hindi/code-switched spontaneous-speech corpus (IITG-HingCoS, HiACC, or VITB-HEBiC), since none of the located India-specific corpora publish formal SNR/difficulty tiers despite claiming "noise-rich" conditions.
- SRB's severity-leveled, multi-category perturbation design (clean → social gatherings → environmental effects → digital/adversarial) is the most directly reusable *generic difficulty-ladder framework* found in this research, more so than any single CHiME year's specific setup, precisely because it is explicitly parameterized by severity and designed to be corpus-agnostic (applied as perturbations on top of arbitrary speech input) rather than tied to one fixed recording scenario.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm licensing/access terms for IITG-HingCoS, HiACC, or VITB-HEBiC (free download vs. request-based academic access) — none of the search snippets specified this explicitly; would need a direct visit to each paper's data-availability section.
- Could not confirm whether IITG-HingCoS, HiACC, or VITB-HEBiC provide formal train/dev/test splits with documented SNR or noise-type metadata (as opposed to just being collected "in noisy environments") — this level of detail was not present in available search snippets.
- No broader IIT/IISc-specific "noisy speech benchmark" survey paper (as opposed to individual code-switching corpora) was found; the search for "IIT IISc noisy speech corpus" returned only code-switching-focused results, not a general Indian-language noise-robustness benchmark. This should be flagged as an open gap for the broader synthesis.
- Could not verify SRB's exact licensing (though it is on GitHub, suggesting open/permissive use) — should be confirmed directly from its repository.
