# Clarification Requests and Uncertainty Handling in Task-Oriented Dialogue Systems

## 1. When should a system trigger a clarification request, and how should it phrase a targeted (vs. generic) one?

### Takeaway
There is a mature, decades-old literature establishing (a) principled, data-driven confidence-threshold methods for deciding whether to accept/confirm/reject or ask for clarification, and (b) a theory of clarification-request *form* (the "reprise question") showing that humans overwhelmingly prefer targeted, content-echoing clarifications over generic "please repeat" requests. Modern (2023–2026) NLP work reframes this as a two-subtask pipeline — clarification-need prediction (binary ambiguity classification) plus clarification-question generation/selection — but the underlying trigger logic (confidence/uncertainty crossing a threshold, or detection of multiple competing interpretations) is continuous with the classic work.

### Cited Findings
- Classic grounding-based dialogue management uses two thresholds (T1, T2) on an utterance/move confidence score: above T1 the system accepts outright; below T2 it treats the turn as a non-understanding and asks for clarification; between T1 and T2 it asks for confirmation (explicit or implicit grounding) rather than full clarification — a three-way accept/confirm/reject(clarify) decision driven purely by confidence score. — [On the use of confidence for statistical decision in dialogue strategies](https://aclanthology.org/W04-2321.pdf)
- Bohus & Rudnicky's empirical analysis of non-understanding errors in a spoken dialogue system (conference-room reservation task) catalogued ten recovery strategies and showed that **rejection threshold tuning is a principled, data-driven optimization problem**: the false-rejection rate (unnecessarily triggering clarification) can be lowered either by building better confidence annotators or by tuning the rejection threshold per-domain and per-dialogue-state, i.e., thresholds should not be fixed/global. — [An Investigation of Non-understanding Errors and Recovery Strategies](https://aclanthology.org/2005.sigdial-1.14.pdf); related: [Error Awareness and Recovery in Conversational Spoken Language Interfaces (Bohus PhD thesis)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Thesis_dbohus.pdf)
- David Schlangen's influential taxonomy ("Causes and Strategies for Requesting Clarification in Dialogue," SIGdial 2004) models clarification causes at multiple levels of communication (not just "didn't hear you" but failures at the level of reference, intention, or acceptability), and distinguishes **explicit confirmation** (an actual clarification request, e.g. "Did you say you want to leave from Potsdam?") from **implicit confirmation** (displaying understanding while moving the dialogue forward, e.g. "From Potsdam. To where?") as two different system strategies for handling the same uncertainty, with different conversational cost. — [Causes and Strategies for Requesting Clarification in Dialogue](https://aclanthology.org/W04-2325.pdf)
- Matthew Purver's PhD thesis ("The Theory and Use of Clarification Requests in Dialogue," 2004) found empirically that **88% of human clarification requests in his corpus were "reprise questions"** — targeted clarifications that echo/reformulate part of the interlocutor's own utterance (e.g., "Potsdam?" or "You said WHERE?") rather than generic repair requests — establishing reprise-form as the dominant, natural human strategy for targeted clarification. — [The Theory and Use of Clarification Requests in Dialogue (Purver thesis)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/2897205_The_Theory_and_Use_of_Clarification_Requests_in_Dialogue_Matthew_Purver)
- Rieser & Lemon's line of work (reinforcement-learning / supervised dialogue-strategy learning from Wizard-of-Oz data) showed that **choosing between clarification strategies (of form vs. of intent) and deciding whether to treat a candidate interpretation as correct can itself be learned** from features of spoken-language understanding (ASR confidence, parse features) rather than hand-coded; logistic-regression models for predicting the right clarification action outperformed earlier supervised baselines by roughly 2x on their task, and this was explicitly proposed as a cheaper way to bootstrap RL dialogue policies from small/no simulated data. — [Learning and Evaluation of Dialogue Strategies for New Applications (Rieser & Lemon)](https://aclanthology.org/J11-1006.pdf); [A framework for learning multimodal clarification strategies](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220029409_A_framework_for_learning_multimodal_clarification_strategies)
- Modern conversational-search/QA framing (post-2019) decomposes the problem into two formal subtasks: **clarification need prediction** (binary classification: is this query ambiguous enough to need clarification?) and **clarification question generation/selection** (retrieval from a question bank + reranking, e.g. BERT-based NeuQS; or RL-based generation maximizing a "clarification utility" function, e.g. QCM; or unified seq2seq models like UniPCQA that do need-prediction, question-generation, and answering jointly). — [A Survey on Asking Clarification Questions Datasets in Conversational Systems (Rahmani, Wang, Feng, Zhang, Yilmaz, Lipani; ACL 2023)](https://github.com/rahmanidashti/ACQSurvey); datasets Qulac, ClariQ, MIMICS, MANtIS, ClariT, ClarQ, CLAQUA cited therein.
- Dataset-level evidence that need-prediction and question-generation are now standard benchmarked subtasks: Qulac (2019, conversational search), ClariQ (2021), MIMICS (2020, large-scale search-engine clarification logs), CLAQUA (2019, KBQA), with a "three primary tasks" framing (need prediction, question ranking, user-satisfaction assessment) used across the field. — [ACQSurvey](https://github.com/rahmanidashti/ACQSurvey)
- Instruction-based LLM elicitation of clarifying questions ("ask the user up to three targeted clarifying questions... proceed once you've received answers") is reported anecdotally/practically to collapse multi-turn repair loops into one or two exchanges versus naive single-shot guessing, though this specific claim comes from a practitioner write-up rather than a peer-reviewed study. — [Local LLMs perform so much better when you teach them to ask before they answer](https://www.xda-developers.com/local-llm-clarifying-questions-system-prompt/) (practitioner source, not peer-reviewed — treat as weaker evidence)
- Recent benchmark/study framing explicitly treats the clarification decision as a **policy problem with multiple sub-decisions**, not a single binary trigger: "deciding whether to ask, what to ask, how to use the user's reply, and when to stop" — i.e., current literature increasingly models clarification as a multi-turn interaction policy (including budgeted/cost-sensitive stopping) rather than a one-shot gate. — [One More Turn, Less Regret: A Regret-Based Multi-Turn Benchmark for LLMs' Clarification Policies](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.21143)
- A 2024 SIGdial paper ("Analyzing and Enhancing Clarification Strategies for Conversational...") continues this targeted-vs-generic distinction in the current LLM-adjacent literature, though the agent could not fetch full text (site blocked); title and venue corroborated across searches. — [Analyzing and Enhancing Clarification Strategies](https://aclanthology.org/2024.sigdial-1.25.pdf) (title/venue confirmed; full content not retrievable in this session)

### Inferences
- The field has converged on a layered decision architecture that is directly reusable for a conversational-integrity layer: (1) a continuous confidence/uncertainty signal per hypothesis, (2) threshold bands mapping that signal to accept / implicit-confirm / explicit-clarify / reject, with thresholds tuned per-context rather than globally, and (3) a *separate* generation step that produces a targeted, reprise-style clarification referencing the specific ambiguous span rather than a generic repair prompt. This separation of "when" (threshold/classifier) from "how" (targeted generation referencing the ambiguous constituent) is a reusable pattern regardless of whether the underlying system is a classic SDS or an LLM.
- The shift from classic SDS to LLM-era framing is mostly about *how* the need-prediction classifier and question generator are implemented (hand-tuned thresholds vs. learned classifiers vs. prompted LLM judgment), not about the basic decision structure — suggesting the conversational-integrity layer can treat "detect need to clarify" and "generate targeted clarification" as two swappable modules.

- No direct, controlled comparison found (in retrievable sources) quantifying targeted/reprise clarifications vs. generic clarifications on task success or turn count for *voice* interfaces specifically (most quantitative evidence is text/chat-based conversational search).

## 2. Multiple-hypothesis / belief-state tracking (POMDP-based dialogue systems): what did this tradition conclude about tradeoffs vs. single-hypothesis systems?

### Takeaway
This is the most theoretically settled sub-literature: statistical/POMDP spoken dialogue systems (roughly 2005–2015, centrally associated with Steve Young, Jason Williams, Milica Gašić, and the Cambridge/Microsoft groups) established that maintaining a probability distribution over many candidate dialogue states ("belief state"), rather than committing to a single best guess, measurably improves robustness to ASR/NLU errors — at the cost of substantial implementation and scaling complexity, which is exactly why practical systems approximate the full belief state rather than track it exactly.

### Cited Findings
- The core POMDP-dialogue idea is that because user intent is hidden and ASR/NLU are unreliable, the dialogue manager should **never collapse to a single best hypothesis**; instead it maintains a full distribution (belief state) over possible dialogue states, updated via Bayesian inference after each turn ("belief monitoring"), and the system's next action is computed directly from this distribution rather than from a point estimate. — [POMDP-based Statistical Spoken Dialogue Systems: a Review (Young, Gašić, Thomson, Williams)](http://mi.eng.cam.ac.uk/~sjy/papers/ygtw13.pdf) (title/authors/venue — Proc. IEEE 2013 — confirmed via search; full text not retrievable in this session due to egress block)
- The explicit claimed benefit: "the belief state provides an explicit representation of uncertainty leading to systems that are much more robust to speech recognition errors" than systems that commit early to a single interpretation. — [POMDP-based Statistical Spoken Dialogue Systems: a Review](http://mi.eng.cam.ac.uk/~sjy/papers/ygtw13.pdf) (per search-engine summary of the paper; could not independently verify exact wording against full text)
- The explicit claimed *cost*: belief-state tracking is "simple in theory" but the belief state is continuous and extremely high-dimensional, making exact implementation intractable — motivating approximate representations such as the Hidden Information State (HIS) system's compressed/partitioned belief space, which was described as the first trainable, scalable, real-world POMDP-based spoken dialogue system. — [POMDP-based Statistical Spoken Dialogue Systems: a Review](http://mi.eng.cam.ac.uk/~sjy/papers/ygtw13.pdf); [The Hidden Information State Dialogue Manager: A Real-World POMDP-Based System](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220816814_The_Hidden_Information_State_Dialogue_Manager_A_Real-World_POMDP-Based_System)
- Williams, Poupart & Young formalized the dialogue POMDP's observation as a **composite** of a discrete dialogue-act hypothesis plus a continuous confidence score, explicitly modeling recognition confidence as part of the state-estimation problem rather than a side signal; they frame the prior (pre-POMDP) landscape as three largely separate techniques — (i) tracking parallel dialogue-state hypotheses, (ii) local use of confidence scores for accept/confirm/reject decisions, and (iii) automated planning — which the POMDP formulation unifies into one decision-theoretic framework. — [Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes with Continuous Observations for Dialogue Management](https://aclanthology.org/2005.sigdial-1.4/)
- The policy layer built on top of belief tracking is explicitly a **reinforcement-learning** problem: find the action-selection policy (mapping belief states to system actions) that maximizes expected discounted cumulative reward over the whole dialogue — i.e., the multi-hypothesis/uncertainty representation is valuable precisely because it is the state representation that RL policy optimization operates on, not an end in itself. — [POMDP-based Statistical Spoken Dialogue Systems: a Review](http://mi.eng.cam.ac.uk/~sjy/papers/ygtw13.pdf) (per search summary)
- The ASR/NLU-facing analogue of belief tracking is N-best-list processing: speech recognizers emit an N-best list of hypotheses with per-utterance, per-word, and per-slot confidence scores, and systems commonly prune the N-best list (e.g., to a max of ~4 entries, or discard entries below ~30% confidence) before using it for state update — a practical compression step between raw ASR output and a tractable "multiple hypotheses" representation. — [patent: Method and system for processing parallel context dependent speech recognition results](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9117453) and related patent literature on N-best confidence culling (industry/patent sources — note lower rigor than peer-reviewed papers, but corroborate the general practice described in academic sources on confidence thresholds)
- Later neural dialogue-state-tracking (DST) work directly tested the single- vs. multi-hypothesis tradeoff with ASR noise: modeling ASR ambiguity via **word confusion networks** (richer than a flattened 1-best transcript) and training DST models on ASR hypotheses rather than clean text improved tracking performance substantially (one reported jump from 28.2% to 33.5% accuracy when training on hypothesis-level/TTS-based noisy data vs. clean-text training), and challenge-series findings (DSTC) consistently found that **the best trackers incorporate low-level ASR features (N-best scores, word-confusion-network scores)** rather than discarding them after 1-best transcription. — [Modeling ASR Ambiguity for Dialogue State Tracking Using Word Confusion Networks](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.00768); general DSTC conclusion per search summary of [The Dialog State Tracking Challenge Series: A Review](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/4ba3/39bd571585fadb1fb1d14ef902b6784f574f.pdf)
- A specific neural follow-on literature ("Uncertainty Measures in Neural Belief Tracking and the Effects on Dialogue Policy Performance") explicitly reconnects modern neural DST to the POMDP tradition's core question — whether carrying explicit uncertainty estimates through belief tracking into the downstream policy improves dialogue performance versus collapsing to point estimates — confirming this is still an active research question in the neural era, not fully closed. — [Uncertainty Measures in Neural Belief Tracking and the Effects on Dialogue Policy Performance](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.04349) (title/topic confirmed via search; full text not retrieved)

### Inferences
- The POMDP/belief-state tradition's central, reusable conclusion for a conversational-integrity layer is a **two-part design pattern**: (1) uncertainty should be represented as a distribution (or at least an N-best set with scores), not collapsed early, because this measurably improves robustness to noisy input; but (2) exact/full distribution tracking is computationally intractable at scale, so real systems need a principled *compression* of the hypothesis space (summary belief states, partitioned/clustered hypotheses, pruned N-best lists) rather than either (a) full exact tracking or (b) naive single-hypothesis collapse.
- The fact that uncertainty-aware neural belief tracking vs. policy performance is still being actively studied in 2021+ (not just a solved 2013-era result) suggests this is **not fully settled** even for classic slot-filling dialogue — it is an ongoing empirical question how much uncertainty propagation actually helps once the policy layer is strong, which is directly relevant to deciding how much "multi-hypothesis" machinery a new conversational-integrity layer actually needs vs. how much is diminishing returns.

## 3. LLM-era uncertainty estimation, calibration, and abstention — and decisions to ask clarifying questions rather than guess

### Takeaway
A substantial and fast-growing (2024–2026) literature on LLM abstention/selective prediction exists, but it is largely *not yet* conversational/multi-turn in focus — most of it treats abstention as a single-shot "answer or decline" decision; a smaller but growing sub-literature specifically studies LLMs' tendency (and reluctance) to ask clarifying questions in dialogue, and finds a striking behavioral gap: LLMs can often *recognize* ambiguity when explicitly probed, but default to answering directly anyway rather than asking.

### Cited Findings
- A TACL 2025 survey ("Know Your Limits: A Survey of Abstention in Large Language Models," Bingbing Wen et al.) frames abstention as the general capability of declining to answer under insufficient evidence; it identifies that neither raw token-probability measures nor verbalized ("I am 80% confident") confidence are reliably well-calibrated, and that practitioners must combine multiple signal types (log-likelihood, calibration post-processing, entropy-based uncertainty metrics) because no single signal suffices. — [Know Your Limits: A Survey of Abstention in Large Language Models](https://aclanthology.org/2025.tacl-1.26.pdf)
- A core identified tension in this survey/line of work: **over-abstention reduces usefulness; under-abstention compromises safety/reliability** — i.e., abstention threshold-setting reproduces the same accept/clarify/reject tradeoff structure found decades earlier in SDS confidence-threshold work, just in LLM terms. — [Know Your Limits](https://aclanthology.org/2025.tacl-1.26.pdf)
- A specifically cautionary empirical finding: entropy-based uncertainty alone is reported as an **unsafe trigger** for abstention, because "abstention often fails precisely when a model is most uncertain" — i.e., the cases where you'd most want the system to hedge or ask are sometimes exactly where naive uncertainty signals are least reliable. — per search synthesis of recent abstention literature (specific primary source not independently verified in this session — flag as moderately-sourced claim)
- "Knowing but Not Showing: LLMs Recognize Ambiguity but Rarely Ask Clarifying Questions" (Jinyan Su & Claire Cardie, Cornell) is the most directly relevant 2025/2026 paper found: it evaluates models in three regimes — standard QA, explicit ambiguity judgment, and behavioral analysis (classifying responses as direct answer / refusal / clarifying question) — on ambiguous, unambiguous, and disambiguated questions, and finds that **models that correctly judge a query as ambiguous when explicitly asked nonetheless overwhelmingly default to producing a direct "best guess" answer rather than asking a clarifying question or refusing**, revealing a gap between ambiguity-recognition capability and clarification *behavior*. The paper explicitly argues that a single best-guess answer to an ambiguous query forces hidden, unstated assumptions about user intent that can propagate errors downstream. — [Knowing but Not Showing: LLMs Recognize Ambiguity but Rarely Ask Clarifying Questions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25284)
- A directly relevant human-vs-LLM behavioral comparison (Madge, Purver & Poesio, CRAC 2025) using a new corpus built from the Minecraft Dialogue Corpus found **only a weak relationship between (annotated) referential ambiguity and whether a clarification question actually gets produced**, and — notably — an asymmetry: *humans* rarely produce clarification requests for referential ambiguity but often do for task-level uncertainty, while *LLMs* show the reverse pattern (more CRs for referential ambiguity, fewer for task uncertainty). Correlation between human and LLM clarification behavior was found to be low. — [Referential ambiguity and clarification requests: comparing human and LLM behaviour](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.10445)
- "Modeling Future Conversation Turns to Teach LLMs to Ask Clarifying Questions" proposes assigning preference-learning labels by simulating how different candidate responses (direct answer vs. clarifying question) play out in *future* conversation turns, rather than labeling clarification-worthiness from the current turn alone; this future-turn-simulation approach reportedly yields about a **5% F1 improvement** (measured against answer sets reflecting different valid interpretations of an ambiguous query) over baselines that don't model future turns. — [Modeling Future Conversation Turns to Teach LLMs to Ask Clarifying Questions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13788)
- "Asking the Right Question at the Right Time: Human and Model Uncertainty Guidance to Ask Clarification Questions" explicitly studies using *model* uncertainty signals (as opposed to only surface ambiguity features) to decide the right moment to trigger a clarifying question, aligning model-internal uncertainty with the human timing of clarification requests. — [Asking the Right Question at the Right Time](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.06509) (title/topic confirmed; full content not independently retrieved in this session)
- Task-oriented-dialogue-specific abstention evaluation: SAGE (State-Grounded, Abstention-Aware Evaluation) proposes a **turn-level abstention signal** derived from the minimum decision margin across all the criteria decided in a turn, producing a ranked list of turns whose lowest-confidence tail can be declined/escalated — i.e., abstention is computed compositionally across sub-decisions within a turn rather than as one global confidence score, which maps closely onto a slot-by-slot or intent-by-intent confidence design. — [SAGE: State-Grounded, Abstention-Aware Evaluation of Task-Oriented Dialogue Agents](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.00434)
- "Act or Clarify? Modeling Sensitivity to Uncertainty and Cost in Communication" explicitly frames the act-vs-clarify decision as a **cost-sensitivity** problem — the decision to clarify should depend not just on raw ambiguity/uncertainty but on the cost of acting wrongly vs. the cost of asking — echoing the classic grounding-cost framing (Clark) and giving a formal decision-theoretic hook. — [Act or Clarify? Modeling Sensitivity to Uncertainty and Cost in Communication](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.02843) (title/framing confirmed via search; full text not retrieved)

### Inferences
- The most load-bearing finding for a "conversational-integrity layer" design is the ambiguity-awareness vs. clarification-behavior gap documented in "Knowing but Not Showing" and the human/LLM asymmetry in Madge et al.: **an LLM's internal ambiguity/uncertainty signal is not sufficient by itself to produce good clarification behavior** — there needs to be an explicit policy layer (akin to the classic dialogue-manager action-selection step) that converts a recognized-ambiguity signal into an actual clarify-vs-answer decision, because left to its own devices the model tends to answer anyway. This directly supports building an explicit, separate decision/policy component rather than relying on prompting an LLM to "just know" when to ask.
- The repeated finding that entropy/uncertainty-alone triggers are unreliable, combined with the classic SDS finding that confidence thresholds must be tuned per-domain/per-state, suggests that for voice specifically, a **single global confidence threshold is very unlikely to be the right design** — multiple, context-conditioned signals (ASR confidence + NLU/intent confidence + model-internal uncertainty + cost-of-error estimate) likely need to be combined, consistent with SAGE's compositional/turn-level approach and the classic three-technique unification Williams & Young described for POMDPs.

## 4. How is "ambiguity" operationalized/measured — are there established taxonomies?

### Takeaway
Yes — there is a well-established linguistic-level taxonomy (lexical / referential (coreferential) / scopal / syntactic-structural ambiguity) that long predates dialogue-systems work, and the dialogue/LLM literature has recently layered dialogue-specific and task-oriented taxonomies on top of it (e.g., CLAMBER's input-process-output-derived 3-dimension/8-category taxonomy, and broader "linguistic ambiguity vs. contextual ambiguity" splits); no single taxonomy is yet universally adopted across the field, but convergent structure is visible.

### Cited Findings
- Base linguistic taxonomy used across NLP: **lexical ambiguity** (a single word with multiple meanings), **referential/coreferential ambiguity** (multiple possible referents for a pronoun/NP — Winograd schemas are the canonical probe for this), and **scopal ambiguity** (relative scope of quantifiers/operators, e.g. "every"/"some" determining which part of a sentence they apply to). — [Disambiguation in Conversational Question Answering in the Era of LLM: A Survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.12543v1); corroborated by [A Taxonomy of Ambiguity Types for NLP](https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/a-taxonomy-of-ambiguity-types-for-nlp) (full primary text not retrievable — themoonlight.io and arxiv blocked — relying on search-indexed summary)
- A broader two-way split proposed for dialogue/LLM settings: **Linguistic Ambiguity** (lexical, structural, or semantic indeterminacy intrinsic to the utterance) vs. **Contextual Ambiguity** (under-specified or shifting reference to entities, locations, time, or discourse context — i.e., ambiguity that depends on dialogue history/situational grounding, not just the sentence in isolation). Current LLMs were found to identify certain semantic ambiguities (lexical, referential) and contextual ambiguities reasonably well, but **struggle with systematic disambiguation** (i.e., recognizing ≠ resolving). — [Disambiguation in Conversational Question Answering in the Era of LLM: A Survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.12543v1)
- CLAMBER (ACL 2024; Zhang, Qin, Deng, Huang, Lei, Liu, Jin, Liang et al.) proposes a taxonomy built from an input-process-output framework for evaluating collaborative systems, consolidating **input-understanding** and **task-completion** perspectives into (per the retrievable summary) multiple top-level dimensions further broken into **eight fine-grained categories**, including an "Epistemic Misalignment" dimension (ambiguity from conflict between the LLM's internal knowledge and the user's query — e.g. an "Unfamiliar"-entity subtype) and an "Aleatoric Output" dimension (ambiguity from missing details needed for a complete/accurate answer). The benchmark itself comprises ~12K data points spanning these ambiguity categories, and found that current LLMs still struggle to achieve strong performance on both identifying and clarifying ambiguity. — [CLAMBER: A Benchmark of Identifying and Clarifying Ambiguous Information Needs in Large Language Models](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.578/)
- Schlangen's clarification-cause taxonomy is itself effectively an ambiguity/non-understanding taxonomy organized by **which level of communication failed** (acoustic/lexical non-understanding vs. reference failure vs. intention/acceptability failure), rather than by surface linguistic category — a complementary, process-oriented way of structuring ambiguity types that maps directly onto *what kind of clarification is needed* at each level. — [Causes and Strategies for Requesting Clarification in Dialogue](https://aclanthology.org/W04-2325.pdf)
- "Missing information" as a distinct ambiguity type (rather than a mis-parse) is explicitly named by the task-oriented clarification-question literature's framing of clarification-need prediction (is the query *under-specified*, not just lexically/referentially ambiguous), and by CLAMBER's "Aleatoric Output" category (ambiguity from lack of essential details). — [CLAMBER](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.578/); [ACQSurvey](https://github.com/rahmanidashti/ACQSurvey)

### Inferences
- For a benchmark/evaluation of a voice conversational-integrity layer, a usable minimal taxonomy synthesizing the above would be: **(1) lexical**, **(2) referential/coreferential**, **(3) scopal/structural**, **(4) missing-information / under-specification**, **(5) contextual/discourse** (reference resolution dependent on dialogue history), and, specific to voice, an implicit **(6) acoustic/ASR-hypothesis ambiguity** (multiple competing transcriptions/N-best hypotheses) that sits "below" the linguistic taxonomy and is largely absent from the text-centric taxonomies surveyed above — this is the most direct conceptual gap between existing taxonomies (built for text/chat LLMs) and a voice-specific integrity layer, and is worth flagging explicitly in synthesis as something the existing literature does not fully cover.
- The CLAMBER epistemic-misalignment category (ambiguity from LLM-knowledge/user-query conflict) is conceptually distinct from classical linguistic ambiguity and closer to the LLM-abstention literature's "known-unknowns" framing — suggesting ambiguity taxonomies and abstention/calibration taxonomies are converging into a shared conceptual space in the 2024+ literature, which is useful for unifying the clarification and abstention mechanisms in one integrity layer rather than treating them as separate subsystems.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve full text of "A Taxonomy of Ambiguity Types for NLP" (arxiv/themoonlight blocked) to confirm its complete category list beyond lexical/referential/scopal — the report synthesis above is based on secondary summary and should be treated as partial.
- No taxonomy found in the retrievable literature that explicitly incorporates **ASR/acoustic-level ambiguity** (competing phonetic hypotheses, disfluency-induced ambiguity) as a first-class category alongside linguistic/contextual ambiguity — this appears to be a genuine gap between the (largely text/chat-oriented) ambiguity-taxonomy literature and the needs of a voice-specific conversational-integrity layer.

## 5. What is settled vs. genuinely open — especially for spoken (not just text) dialogue?

### Takeaway
Settled/reusable practice: confidence-threshold-based accept/confirm/clarify/reject decision structures, targeted reprise-style clarification phrasing, and representing uncertainty as a distribution/N-best set rather than collapsing early all have strong, decades-deep empirical support. Genuinely open: how to combine LLM-internal uncertainty with classic ASR/NLU confidence signals, how to convert ambiguity-*recognition* into reliable clarification-*behavior*, how much of the full-belief-state machinery is still worth its complexity cost in the neural/LLM era, and — especially — almost everything specific to spoken (as opposed to text) LLM-era dialogue, which the literature search suggests is comparatively under-studied relative to text/chat settings.

### Cited Findings
- Settled: confidence-threshold-based three-way decisions (accept / confirm / clarify-or-reject) are established since the mid-2000s and still structurally underlie both classic SDS confidence-threshold work and modern LLM abstention-threshold framing (same accept/decline/ask tradeoff, reproduced under new vocabulary). — [On the use of confidence for statistical decision in dialogue strategies](https://aclanthology.org/W04-2321.pdf); [Know Your Limits: A Survey of Abstention in Large Language Models](https://aclanthology.org/2025.tacl-1.26.pdf)
- Settled: targeted, reprise-style clarification (echoing the ambiguous constituent) is the dominant and preferred human strategy (88% of human CRs in Purver's corpus) and is treated as the gold-standard *form* for a clarification question across both classic and modern literature, as opposed to generic "please repeat" requests. — [Purver thesis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/2897205_The_Theory_and_Use_of_Clarification_Requests_in_Dialogue_Matthew_Purver)
- Open (explicitly flagged by recent survey/benchmark papers): **the inability to determine when to ask for clarification** is named directly as one of the core limiting challenges for current clarification-capable systems, especially for latent/indirect ambiguity and adversarial or non-cooperative user behavior; empirical ablation work finds that explicit ambiguity-tracking, planning, and user-behavior-forecasting modules are *all* needed together for robust clarification — no single mechanism suffices. — per search synthesis of recent clarification-benchmark literature (e.g., multi-turn clarification policy papers); specific primary citation for the ablation claim not independently verified in this session — flag as moderately-sourced
- Open: multi-turn clarification improves accuracy but can increase user friction/interaction cost, and current mitigations (budgeted turns, cost-sensitive stopping, single-turn-only clarification) are described as trade-offs rather than solved problems. — per search synthesis, corroborated conceptually by [Act or Clarify? Modeling Sensitivity to Uncertainty and Cost in Communication](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.02843)
- Open: evaluation/data validity — much current clarification research relies on LLM-simulated users, scripted ambiguities, or gold-oracle answers, which the field itself flags as potentially diverging from real-world dialogue dynamics and real error modes — a significant caveat for any benchmark built on existing datasets. — per search synthesis of recent benchmark papers (e.g., regret-based multi-turn clarification benchmark)
- Open and directly load-bearing for this project: the behavioral gap between ambiguity-recognition and clarification-action ("Knowing but Not Showing") and the human/LLM divergence in *which* ambiguity types trigger clarification (Madge et al., referential vs. task-uncertainty asymmetry) show that **naively prompting an LLM to "ask when unsure" is not a solved, reliable mechanism** — it requires explicit behavioral shaping (RL/self-play, future-turn-simulation preference learning) to close, and even then is evaluated mostly in text settings. — [Knowing but Not Showing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25284); [Referential ambiguity and clarification requests: comparing human and LLM behaviour](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.10445); [Modeling Future Conversation Turns to Teach LLMs to Ask Clarifying Questions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13788)
- Open, specific to spoken dialogue: almost all 2023–2026 clarification/ambiguity/abstention papers surfaced in this research are evaluated on **text or chat-based conversational QA/search**, not spoken/voice interaction with real ASR noise; the classic belief-state/N-best literature (which *was* voice-native) is 10–20 years old and largely predates both LLMs and modern neural ASR. The one explicitly spoken-dialogue-flavored recent benchmark found, "C3: A Bilingual Benchmark for Spoken Dialogue Models Exploring Challenges in Complex Conversations," signals the field recognizes this gap and is beginning to address it, but it was not possible to retrieve full method/results in this session. — [C3: A Bilingual Benchmark for Spoken Dialogue Models](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.22968) (title/existence confirmed; content not retrieved — egress blocked)
- Open: whether/how much explicit uncertainty propagation through belief tracking still helps once the downstream policy is a strong neural/LLM-based model is an active research question, not a closed 2013-era result (per the continued existence of papers like "Uncertainty Measures in Neural Belief Tracking and the Effects on Dialogue Policy Performance" in 2021). — [Uncertainty Measures in Neural Belief Tracking](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.04349)

### Inferences
- The single highest-value, best-evidenced design primitives to carry into a task-agnostic conversational-integrity layer are: (1) never collapse to a single hypothesis before the policy/action-selection step — carry at least an N-best set with scores; (2) use context-conditioned (not global) thresholds to route between accept / implicit-confirm / explicit-clarify / escalate-or-abstain; (3) when clarifying, generate a targeted, reprise-style question tied to the specific ambiguous span/hypothesis, not a generic repair prompt; and (4) do not rely on a model's bare internal uncertainty or ambiguity-awareness to reliably produce clarifying behavior — that conversion needs an explicit, separately-trained or separately-engineered policy layer, because current evidence shows recognition and behavior are decoupled in LLMs.
- The field's clearest unresolved frontier — and the one most directly relevant to differentiating this project's contribution — is **spoken, noisy, LLM-era clarification/uncertainty handling**: the belief-state tradition solved this for statistical slot-filling SDS, and the LLM-era literature solved (partially) ambiguity/clarification for text, but the intersection (LLM-era voice agents reasoning over ASR uncertainty *and* semantic/task ambiguity *and* deciding when to ask) is thin in the retrievable literature, which is consistent with this being a genuinely open, synthesis-worthy problem for the broader research effort.
