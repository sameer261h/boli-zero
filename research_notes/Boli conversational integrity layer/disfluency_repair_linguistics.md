## What is the Shriberg/Levelt-style reparandum / interruption-point / interregnum / repair framework? What are the main disfluency types?

### Takeaway
The field's standard structural model (Shriberg 1994, building on Levelt 1983) decomposes a self-interrupted utterance into up to four parts — **reparandum** (the disfluent/to-be-replaced material), the **interruption point** (a boundary, not a word), an optional **interregnum** (filler/editing material), and the **repair** (the fluent continuation) — and this structural skeleton is widely treated as settled, citable science, not a novel contribution to reuse. Levelt additionally supplies a functional typology of *why* a repair happens (error vs. appropriateness vs. different-information repair) and a formal well-formedness constraint on how repairs are grammatically licensed.

### Cited Findings
- Shriberg (1994) formalized the four-part disfluency structure: **Reparandum** (the disfluent part to be replaced/ignored, usually short — 2-3 words), **Interruption Point** (not a word itself, but the moment speech is interrupted and repair begins, typically right after the last word spoken before the break), **Interregnum** (optional repair cues/fillers bridging the disfluency and the repair), and **Repair** (the fluent continuation such that removing the first two regions leaves a fluent utterance). Not all four parts need be present in a given disfluency instance. — [SRI/Shriberg: Phonetic Consequences of Speech Disfluency](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/phonetic_consequences_of_speech_disfluency.pdf) (via search synthesis)
- The Switchboard annotation formalizes this with bracket notation: the reparandum begins with `[`, ends with `+`, the optional edit phase/interregnum follows, and the repair follows after the interruption marker and ends with `]`. — [ResearchGate figure, Switchboard repair notation](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-reparandum-begins-with-a-left-square-bracket-and-ends-with-a-The-repair_fig2_220147952)
- Levelt (1983), based on a corpus of spontaneous Dutch speech, proposed that speakers monitor their own speech via the same comprehension system used to monitor others' speech (including an "inner loop" that inspects the speech plan before articulation), and that self-repair proceeds in three phases: (i) monitoring/interrupting speech when trouble is detected, (ii) hesitating/pausing (silent or filled pauses), (iii) producing the repair. — [Levelt 1983 "Monitoring and self-repair in speech," Cognition 14:41-104](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_64752_4/component/file_64753/1983_levelt_monitoring.pdf); corroborated by [Self-Monitoring and Self-Repair in Spontaneous Speech (Neliti)](https://media.neliti.com/media/publications/144784-EN-self-monitoring-and-self-repair-in-spont.pdf)
- Levelt's functional typology of self-repairs has **four types**: *error repair* (correcting an actual mistake; introduces information crucial to correct interpretation), *appropriateness repair* (the original was not erroneous, but the speaker refines/elaborates/clarifies it), *different-information repair* (speaker changes their mind mid-utterance and restarts with new/different content), and (per one secondary source) *same-information repair*. — [search synthesis citing Levelt 1983, via TESL-EJ/secondary literature discussion](http://tesl-ej.org/ej49/a1.html)
- Levelt's repairs are claimed to obey a **well-formedness rule**: concatenating (a) the pre-interruption speech completed to be well-formed, (b) "and", and (c) the post-interruption repair text, must itself be syntactically well-formed — except for repairs that are themselves syntactically/phonologically ill-formed. This is presented as a structural constraint governing how listeners (and models) can parse repairs. — [search synthesis of Levelt 1983 well-formedness rule, cross-referenced via "Speech Repairs, Intonational Boundaries and Discourse Markers" (Hindle-style formalization)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cmp-lg/9712009)
- Levelt's model (conceptualization → formulation → articulation → self-monitoring) remains described as "the most widely-adopted theory in self-repair studies," i.e., settled foundational science that subsequent disfluency work (including Shriberg's and the Switchboard annotation) builds on rather than displaces. — [search synthesis, Levelt framework overview](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:897160/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- Main disfluency types recognized across this literature: **repetitions** (word/phrase repeated verbatim), **substitutions/corrections** (reparandum replaced by different words — the classic "repair"), **restarts/abandoned utterances** (speaker abandons the current structure and begins a syntactically new one), **deletions** (reparandum simply dropped with no replacement), filled pauses ("uh," "um"), and explicit editing terms/discourse markers ("I mean," "you know," "well") that often occupy the interregnum slot. — [search synthesis of Switchboard annotation categories](https://web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/calhoun.pdf); word-level labels in the corpus are **fluent / reparandum / repair**, with reparandum "encompassing a variety of disfluencies such as filled pauses, false starts, repetitions, and substitutions." — [GitHub: NXT-Switchboard-Disfluency-Parser](https://github.com/nihal111/NXT-Switchboard-Disfluency-Parser)

### Inferences
- The reparandum/IP/interregnum/repair skeleton is a **language-agnostic structural grammar of self-interruption** (it describes timing/discourse structure, not language-specific lexical content), which is likely why it has been successfully ported to French, Dutch, and (per later findings below) Indian-language corpora. The *interregnum fillers* and *editing terms* themselves, however, are lexically language- and register-specific (e.g., "I mean," "you know" vs. Hindi/Indian-language equivalents) and would need re-specification per language.
- Levelt's functional typology (error / appropriateness / different-information repair) is a useful lens for a "conversational-integrity layer" because it separates disfluencies that signal *actual correction of wrong content* (error repair — high-value signal: the final repair supersedes the reparandum) from disfluencies that signal *stylistic refinement* (appropriateness repair — both reparandum and repair may carry usable content) and from topic *changes of mind* (different-information repair — reparandum may be a now-abandoned intent that should not be silently merged with the repair).

## How has the Switchboard Corpus been annotated for disfluencies and repairs? What generalizes vs. what is English/telephone-specific?

### Takeaway
Switchboard's disfluency annotation (part of the Penn Treebank-3 release and later re-expressed in NXT-format XML) is the de facto reference scheme for English disfluency research: it marks reparandum/interregnum/repair spans with bracket notation over a corpus of ~3M words of spontaneous two-stranger telephone conversation, and treats disfluency as pervasive but still a minority phenomenon (roughly 5-6% of tokens annotated as disfluent, with repetitions being the single largest category at over 50% of disfluency instances). The scheme's structural machinery (bracket spans, reparandum/repair distinction) is reusable across languages; its *lexical* editing-term inventory and some of its genre assumptions (telephone-only audio, no visual grounding, strangers negotiating an assigned topic) are English/telephone-specific and listed explicitly below.

### Cited Findings
- The Switchboard Corpus is "over 3 million words of spontaneous telephone speech conversations... English telephone speech between two strangers who were given specific topics." — [search synthesis on Switchboard disfluency rate](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/disfluencies_in_switchboard.pdf)
- A subset of one million words from Switchboard was annotated for syntactic structure *and* disfluencies as part of the Penn Treebank project; annotation "involved tagging hesitations, stutters, word fragments, restarts, and a limited class of discourse markers." This is released as **Treebank-3, LDC catalog LDC99T42** (1999). — [LDC Catalog: Treebank-3](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/LDC99T42)
- A richer, consolidated multi-layer re-annotation was released as the **NXT-format Switchboard Corpus**, LDC catalog **LDC2009T26** (Nov. 2009), which brings together in NITE XML Toolkit (NXT) format the multiple annotation layers (syntax, disfluency, dialogue acts, prosody, etc.) performed on a transcript subset of Switchboard-1 Release 2 (the base audio/transcripts, LDC97S62). — [LDC Catalog: NXT Switchboard Annotations](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/LDC2009T26); [The NXT-format Switchboard Corpus paper](https://web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/calhoun.pdf)
- Annotation scheme mechanics: a repair = reparandum (replaced words) + optional edit term ("uh," "you know") + repair. The reparandum begins with `[` and ends at a point marked (commonly `+` or `?` depending on version); the repair follows the edit phase after the interruption marker and ends with `]`. The guideline explicitly defines "editing terms" as items "having some semantic content" (distinguishing them from pure filled pauses). — [search synthesis of Switchboard annotation framework](https://www.semdial.org/anthology/Z15-Tian_semdial_0020.pdf); bracket notation detail — [ResearchGate figure](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-reparandum-begins-with-a-left-square-bracket-and-ends-with-a-The-repair_fig2_220147952)
- Prevalence statistics: only **5.9% of tokens** in Switchboard are annotated as disfluent, and **more than 50% of disfluency instances are repetitions** (the single largest category), per analyses of the corpus. — [search synthesis, "Disfluencies in Switchboard" and related statistics](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/disfluencies_in_switchboard.pdf)
- Access/licensing: Switchboard-1 Release 2 (base audio) is **LDC97S62**; Treebank-3 is **LDC99T42**; NXT Switchboard Annotations is **LDC2009T26** — all are **LDC catalog items requiring an LDC membership/license fee**, not freely open-access, though academic licensing is standard practice (subject to LDC's non-commercial/member terms). A separately re-annotated/corrected mapping of the disfluency annotations is available as an open GitHub resource. — [LDC Catalog: LDC97S62](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/LDC97S62); [LDC Catalog: LDC99T42](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/LDC99T42); [LDC Catalog: LDC2009T26](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/LDC2009T26); [GitHub: switchboard_corrected_reannotated](https://github.com/vickyzayats/switchboard_corrected_reannotated)

### Inferences
- **Likely to generalize across languages**: the bracket-span structural machinery (reparandum/interruption-point/interregnum/repair), the fluent/reparandum/repair token-labeling scheme, the repetition/substitution/restart/deletion disfluency taxonomy, and the basic observation that disfluency is a real but minority phenomenon (single-digit-to-low-double-digit percentage of tokens) in conversational speech generally.
- **Likely English/telephone-specific**: the specific lexical inventory of editing terms/discourse markers ("I mean," "you know," "well" as having "semantic content" vs. "uh/um" as pure filler) is tied to English lexical choices and would not transfer directly to Hindi, Tamil, Kannada, etc. without re-specification; the audio-only, two-stranger, assigned-topic, landline-telephone register of Switchboard also means the corpus under-represents disfluency patterns from video/face-to-face grounding cues, code-switching, multi-party overlap, and task-oriented (not just social) talk — all relevant to a voice-AI product context.
- The dominance of repetitions (>50%) as "settled" empirical science specifically describes this register/language; whether code-switched or dialectal Indian-language speech shows the same repetition-dominant distribution is an open empirical question (see WILDRE section below, which does not report a comparable prevalence breakdown in what I could retrieve).

### Gaps
- I could not access the actual Switchboard disfluency annotation guideline/manual text (Meteer et al. 1995 "Dysfluency Annotation Stylebook for the Switchboard Corpus" is the canonical primary source but its hosting domain was not reachable this session), so annotation mechanics above are reconstructed from secondary paraphrase, not the primary guideline.

## What does "Towards Disfluency Annotated Corpora for Indian Languages" (Kochar et al., WILDRE 2024) contribute?

### Takeaway
Kochar, Mujadia, Mishra & Sharma (WILDRE 2024, co-located with LREC-COLING 2024) is the first systematic attempt to extend disfluency annotation to **six Indian languages** (reportedly including Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Telugu, Kannada, and a sixth), explicitly noting that—unlike English—no comprehensive disfluency-annotation guidelines previously existed for these languages; it contributes (a) a holistic annotation scheme adapted to Indian-language-specific linguistic phenomena (including code-mixing/code-switching with English), and (b) an algorithmic method for synthetically generating disfluent training data from clean text. This looks like foundational/novel infrastructure work (not yet "settled science") — it is establishing baseline annotation practice rather than confirming an existing consensus.

### Cited Findings
- The paper is Kochar, Mujadia, Vasantlal, Mishra, Sharma, "Towards Disfluency Annotated Corpora for Indian Languages," in *Proceedings of the 7th Workshop on Indian Language Data: Resources and Evaluation (WILDRE)*, LREC-COLING 2024, published 25 May 2024. — [ACL Anthology: 2024.wildre-1.1](https://aclanthology.org/2024.wildre-1.1/) (full PDF at [2024.wildre-1.1.pdf](https://aclanthology.org/2024.wildre-1.1.pdf), not directly fetchable this session but indexed/confirmed via search)
- Motivation: "individuals often engage in thinking and self-correction during speech production, and these instances of interruption or correction are commonly referred to as disfluencies"; the paper "presents comprehensive research on disfluencies in Indian languages, involving annotation of real-world conversation transcripts and analysis of linguistic nuances inherent to Indian languages that need to be considered during annotation." — [search synthesis of abstract](https://aclanthology.org/2024.wildre-1.1/)
- Explicit gap claim: "unlike English, datasets comprising six Indian languages lack comprehensive guidelines regarding disfluency behavior," so the authors "followed a holistic approach for identifying disfluency instances and other details requiring special attention when dealing with Indian languages." — [search synthesis of abstract](https://aclanthology.org/2024.wildre-1.1/)
- Synthetic data generation: the paper "introduces a robust algorithm for the synthetic generation of disfluent data" that (per search synthesis) "takes clean text and injects disfluencies based on learned probabilities and linguistic rules, creating realistic disfluent samples by considering factors like sentence length, the probability of injecting multiple disfluencies, and the specific characteristics of different disfluency types such as partial word repairs, phrase repairs, and code-mixed repairs." — [search synthesis](https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/work/towards-disfluency-annotated-corpora-for-indian-languages/wrk_b5b113f3fcadf6c71a363cfd14cb2db5)
- A related/follow-on resource from (likely) overlapping authorship reports **Kappa agreement scores of 92 (Hindi) and 89 (Bengali)** for disfluency annotation across what is described as 6 Indian languages (Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Telugu, Kannada, and others), and explicitly addresses **code-mixed Indian-language-English data** as part of the annotation challenge. — [search synthesis, cross-referencing WILDRE disfluency annotation work](https://aclanthology.org/2024.wildre-1.1.pdf)
- A closely related, apparently expanded journal version exists: "Disfluency annotated corpora for Indian English in technical domains," *Language Resources and Evaluation* (Springer), DOI 10.1007/s10579-024-09781-5 — suggesting this research program extended beyond the WILDRE paper into Indian-English technical-domain speech specifically. — [Springer article listing](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10579-024-09781-5) (abstract not directly retrievable this session)
- A further related/adjacent 2026 WILDRE paper, "Bengali-English and Hindi-English Code Mixed Speech Data with Disfluencies," and a separate "BEHE-CMDisfl" synthetic corpus integrate disfluency phenomena (filled pauses, repetitions, restarts) specifically into Bengali-English and Hindi-English **code-mixed** utterances, with text generated via LLM prompting "to encourage controlled switching and varied disfluency patterns." — [ACL Anthology: 2026.wildre-1.5](https://aclanthology.org/2026.wildre-1.5/) (search synthesis)

### Inferences
- The explicit framing ("unlike English, datasets... lack comprehensive guidelines") positions this as genuinely novel foundational work for Indian languages — a research team building a voice-AI "conversational-integrity layer" for Indian-language/dialect speech should treat this scheme as the closest available starting point, but should expect to extend rather than simply adopt it, since it appears to be an initial/first-generation annotation effort rather than a mature, multiply-validated standard like Switchboard's.
- The explicit inclusion of "code-mixed repairs" as a named disfluency subtype (alongside partial-word and phrase repairs) in the synthetic-generation algorithm is directly relevant to the target system's code-switching requirement — this suggests the Indian-language disfluency literature already treats code-switching not as a confound to disfluency annotation but as an integrated disfluency dimension (switching languages mid-repair), which is a finding Switchboard's English-only scheme cannot offer.
- The high inter-annotator Kappa scores (89-92) reported for Hindi/Bengali in the broader research program suggest the core reparandum/repair distinction is learnable and reliable even for annotators working in Indian languages — supporting the inference that the Shriberg/Levelt structural skeleton (not just its English lexical instantiation) does generalize.

## Does disfluent material (false starts, corrections, retractions, hedges) carry useful signal rather than being noise to strip?

### Takeaway
There are two partly conflicting strands in the literature: (1) an NLP/engineering strand showing disfluencies measurably *degrade* downstream task performance (intent detection, slot filling, MT, QA) when left in ASR transcripts, which is the standard justification for disfluency *removal/correction* pipelines; and (2) a psycholinguistic/pragmatics strand showing disfluencies and hedges are not random noise but **systematically correlated with speaker uncertainty, reduced commitment, and lower feeling-of-knowing**, i.e., they carry real epistemic signal that listeners (and, by extension, a downstream reasoning system) could use — even though that same signal causes human listeners to rate disfluent speakers as less knowledgeable. These two strands are not actually contradictory once reframed: current systems strip disfluencies because their architectures can't *use* the signal, not because the signal is absent.

### Cited Findings
- "Disfluencies not only hurt the readability of ASR transcripts, but also erode model performance on downstream tasks, such as machine translation and question answering." — [search synthesis citing "Mind the Pause: Disfluency-Aware Objective Tuning for Multilingual Speech Correction with LLMs"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.12242)
- "Experimental results show that disfluencies negatively affect the performances of downstream intent detection and slot filling tasks in dialogue systems," and disfluencies "can trigger a chain of errors impacting all the modules of a dialogue system: natural language understanding, dialogue state tracking, and response generation." Existing dialogue datasets contain only a marginal number of disfluent utterances, so systems "may... critically fail when exposed to disfluent utterances" at deployment. — [search synthesis citing "From Disfluency Detection to Intent Detection and Slot Filling"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.08359) and ["Disfluency Generation for More Robust Dialogue Systems"](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.728.pdf)
- On the uncertainty-signal side: "Disfluencies are temporary pauses, errors, or filler sounds that interrupt the flow of language that can signal that a speaker is hesitant or experiencing difficulty planning their next words." In a trivia-recall study, "people trying to recall answers... produced more disfluencies, longer latencies, more rising intonation, and more expressions of doubt when they reported having a low feeling of knowing about an answer" — i.e., disfluency rate tracks a speaker's own metacognitive uncertainty, not just production difficulty. — [search synthesis of hedging/disfluency-uncertainty literature](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8579j2fh/qt8579j2fh.pdf)
- "Hedging is a behavior wherein speakers or writers attempt to distance themselves from the proposition they are communicating," and "whether speakers strengthen or hedge their statements is shaped by how much commitment and accountability they are willing to assume, balancing persuasive intent with the potential social consequences of being wrong." "Plausibility shields signal a lack of commitment to the content of a message." — [Prokofieva & Hirschberg, "Hedging and Speaker Commitment"](http://www.cs.columbia.edu/~prokofieva/Prokofieva_Hirschberg_Paper.pdf)
- Listener-side cost of the same signal: "speakers who use 'ums,' 'ahs,' and corrections are consistently rated as less knowledgeable than those who speak fluently" — i.e., the same disfluency that carries genuine epistemic signal is also socially penalized, which is a tension relevant to any system that might *surface* disfluency-derived uncertainty signals to end users or to other agents. — [search synthesis, hedging/disfluency perception literature]
- Separately, there is active work on going the *other* direction — deliberately generating/injecting disfluencies into training data or dialogue system output ("Disfluency Generation for More Robust Dialogue Systems"; Kochar et al.'s synthetic generator above) specifically because models trained only on fluent data fail on real disfluent input — an implicit acknowledgment that disfluency is not just noise but a distributional feature of real conversational input that systems must be able to parse, even if the end *goal* is still often to strip it for downstream processing. — [search synthesis](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.728.pdf)

### Inferences
- The dominant current NLP engineering paradigm treats disfluency as "noise to detect-and-remove" largely because downstream modules (slot-filling, intent classifiers) are trained on fluent text and cannot natively consume reparandum/repair structure as a *feature* — this is an architectural limitation of current pipelines, not proof that the reparandum carries no information. The psycholinguistic literature (uncertainty/commitment/hedging) argues the opposite: the *existence and location* of a self-repair is itself a signal about the speaker's confidence, prior intent, and what almost got said — which is exactly the kind of signal a "conversational-integrity layer" sitting above a disfluency-removal ASR/NLU stack could choose to preserve (e.g., tag an utterance with "speaker self-corrected a number/name here" as metadata) rather than discard.
- This supports a design argument for the broader synthesis: preserving reparandum content and repair-type (error vs. appropriateness vs. different-information, per Levelt) as structured metadata — rather than just outputting the cleaned repair string — lets a downstream reasoning layer treat "the user said X then corrected to Y" as a feature (e.g., for disambiguation, trust calibration, or flagging low-confidence slots) instead of silently losing that the correction ever happened.

### Gaps
- I found no direct academic source specifically proposing (as an established, citable finding) that voice-AI systems should *architecturally preserve* disfluency-derived uncertainty signal as first-class downstream-reasoning input, as opposed to either (a) stripping disfluency for cleaner NLU or (b) training on disfluency-augmented data purely for *robustness* (i.e., so the parser doesn't choke on disfluent input, not so it *uses* the disfluency as a signal). This looks like an open/novel design argument the broader Boli synthesis would be making, not a confirmed existing recommendation in the literature I could access this session.
- The hedging/commitment literature search results were general (cognitive-science, pragmatics) rather than specific to spoken dialogue system design; I did not find a paper explicitly bridging "hedge/disfluency as commitment signal" with "voice-agent downstream task reasoning" — that bridge appears to be the novel contribution space, not a settled citation.

## How do humans change their speech after an ASR or voice-agent misunderstanding?

### Takeaway
This is a well-established, decades-old (1996-2017+) experimental HCI/speech literature, centered on Sharon Oviatt and colleagues' work and later cross-linguistic/multilingual replications: after a recognition error, users systematically **hyperarticulate** (slow down, elongate segments and pauses, widen pitch excursions, suppress amplitude/F0 variability, shift to final falling contours) in a predictable, staged way (the **CHAM model** — two stages of increasing severity tied to error-rate history), and this adaptation is exactly the *wrong* thing acoustically for most ASR systems, which were trained on normal fluent speech and recognize hyperarticulated speech *worse* — producing a well-documented "hyperarticulation paradox" / **error spiral** where repair attempts increase rather than decrease recognition failure. This looks like settled, highly-cited, directly reusable science (not novel), with the caveat that it was established primarily on English-language systems circa the 1990s-2000s; the one multilingual replication found (Kraljevski et al. 2017, Interspeech) indicates the phenomenon recurs across languages but with language-specific detail.

### Cited Findings
- "When speaking to interactive systems, people sometimes hyperarticulate — or adopt a clarified form of speech that has been associated with increased recognition errors." Hyperarticulated speech is "slower and louder speech with wider pitch excursion and more internal silence." — [search synthesis of hyperarticulation literature](https://faculty.washington.edu/levow/papers/ICSLP_96/icslp96.htm)
- The "hyperarticulation paradox": "Current state-of-the-art speech recognition systems fail to handle hyperarticulated speech, with recognition performance degrading significantly... When a speech recognizer fails to recognize words, users switch to clearer articulation, but the recognizer then creates even more errors, prompting users to extend efforts toward a hyper-clear speaking mode, which leads to even more recognition errors." — [search synthesis, Oviatt/Levow hyperarticulation line of work](https://faculty.washington.edu/levow/papers/Speech_Communication_98/speechjournal.pdf)
- Specific durational/linguistic changes during error resolution: "During both low and high error rates, durational changes were pervasive, including elongation of the speech segment and large relative increases in the number and duration of pauses." Speakers "are more likely to clearly articulate constituents that were apparently misrecognized than those either before or after the troublesome constituents, and more likely to clearly articulate content words than function words" — i.e., hyperarticulation is *targeted* at the specific misrecognized span, not a blanket slow-down. — [Oviatt, Levow et al., "Predicting Hyperarticulate Speech During Human-Computer Error Resolution," Speech Communication 1998](https://faculty.washington.edu/levow/papers/Speech_Communication_98/speechjournal.pdf)
- The **CHAM model** (Computer-elicited Hyperarticulate Adaptation Model): correcting persistent recognition errors triggers three qualitatively different adaptations — (1) increased linguistic contrast via switching input modes/lexical content across repeated correction attempts, (2) increased hyperarticulation (lengthened segments/pauses, more final falling contours) when correcting with verbatim speech, (3) simultaneous suppression of linguistic variability in amplitude and F0. **Stage I** adaptation = moderate hyperarticulation (single-feature change: duration) under low system-error rate; **Stage II** = extreme, multi-feature hyperarticulation (duration + articulation + intonation + F0 + amplitude) under high system-error rate. — [Oviatt et al., CHAM model, ICSLP 1998](https://www.isca-archive.org/icslp_1998/oviatt98b_icslp.html); overview — [search synthesis](https://doi.org/10.1177/002383099804100409)
- "Hyperarticulation is a targeted and flexible adaptation rather than a generalized and stable mode of speaking, increasing after evidence of misrecognition and then decaying gradually over several turns in the absence of further misrecognitions." — [search synthesis, "Adapting speaking after evidence of misrecognition: Local and global hyperarticulation," Speech Communication 50(3), 2007](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1016/j.specom.2007.07.005)
- Multilingual replication: Kraljevski et al., "Hyperarticulation of Corrections in Multilingual Dialogue Systems," Interspeech 2017, studies hyperarticulation of corrections specifically across multiple languages in dialogue systems (title-level confirmation that the phenomenon has been examined beyond English; full-text detail not retrievable this session). — [Kraljevski et al. 2017, Interspeech](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2017/kraljevski17_interspeech.pdf)
- Error-spiral / repair-strategy findings from spoken dialogue systems research: "A system may persist in misunderstanding repeated spoken renditions of the same named entity, unless a substantially different tactic can be adopted to assure higher communicative success," and "a critical barrier to widespread deployment remains in the form of communication breakdown at strategic points in the dialogue, often when the user is trying to convey a critical piece of information that the system repeatedly misunderstands." There is "a well-known problem of falling into error spirals when a recognition error occurs in a spoken dialogue system," where the system's own repeat-request strategy (reusing the same wording) can perpetuate rather than resolve the spiral. — [search synthesis, SIGdial/spoken dialogue systems error-recovery literature](https://www.semdial.org/anthology/Z12-Henderson_semdial_0017.pdf)
- A concrete repair strategy noted as both common and risky: "Repetition was the primary repair strategy, with the system repeating its question until a successful utterance was obtained" — flagged in recent (intent-based vs. LLM-based) spoken dialogue system error/recovery analyses as a strategy that readily produces loops. — [search synthesis, "Error and Recovery Analysis of Intent-based and LLM-based SDS"](https://www.isca-archive.org/slate_2025/pepe25_slate.pdf)
- "When humans speak to each other, there is a collaborative process of recovery from non-understanding or misunderstanding" — contrasted implicitly with the more brittle system-initiated repeat-request strategies in human-computer dialogue, pointing to human-human conversational repair as a richer behavioral model dialogue systems could emulate rather than the degenerate "just repeat/re-ask" strategy. — [search synthesis, "Recovering from Non-Understanding Errors," SemDial](https://www.semdial.org/anthology/Z12-Henderson_semdial_0017.pdf)

### Inferences
- The CHAM model's staged structure (moderate adaptation under occasional error → extreme, multi-feature adaptation under repeated error) is directly transferable as a *design heuristic* for a conversational-integrity layer: a system could detect "user is in Stage II hyperarticulation" (slowed, exaggerated, falling-contour-heavy repeated input) as an explicit signal that the *previous* turn failed badly, independent of whatever the ASR itself reports as confidence — this is a behaviorally-grounded, settled-science signal that is currently underused in most production voice-AI error handling, which still mostly turns on raw ASR confidence scores rather than user-adaptation cues.
- The fact that hyperarticulation specifically *degrades* ASR accuracy (because ASR is trained on normal-register speech) implies that a repair/correction turn is systematically the *most* error-prone turn in a conversation — which argues for a conversational-integrity layer to treat "second attempt after an apparent misunderstanding" turns with heightened suspicion/fallback logic (e.g., route to clarification or multimodal confirmation) rather than assuming a retry will be easier to transcribe than the original attempt.
- "Repetition as primary repair strategy" being flagged as loop-prone in both classic (telephone IVR-era) and recent (LLM-based SDS) literature suggests this is a stable, cross-generation failure mode — not something newer LLM-based architectures have automatically solved — reinforcing that explicit repair-strategy design (varying wording/modality on retry, as in CHAM's "linguistic contrast" adaptation) remains necessary.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the full text of Kraljevski et al. 2017 (Interspeech, multilingual hyperarticulation of corrections) — only the title and a one-line thematic summary were available, so I cannot report which specific languages were tested, what cross-linguistic differences (if any) were found, or whether hyperarticulation strength/form varies by language typology (e.g., tonal vs. non-tonal, or languages with different prosodic marking of emphasis). This is the single most relevant gap for the "universal vs. English-specific" question in this domain, and the report-writer should flag CHAM/hyperarticulation as "established for English, cross-linguistic generality only partially confirmed."
- I found no literature (in this session's reachable results) specifically on hyperarticulation or error-spiral behavior in **regional-dialect** or **code-switched** speech to a voice agent, which is the actual target scenario for Boli's conversational-integrity layer — this is a clear, explicit gap: the CHAM/Oviatt line of work was conducted on (as far as retrieved) standard-dialect, single-language interactions, and whether dialect speakers hyperarticulate toward their own dialect norm or toward a perceived "standard"/prestige register when correcting a misrecognition is, as far as I could determine this session, an open question not addressed in the retrieved literature.
- I did not find CHI-venue (ACM CHI proper) papers distinct from the Oviatt/ICSLP/Speech-Communication line — the "CHI/Interspeech/SIGdial" venues named in the task's suggested sources were only partially covered (strong Interspeech/Speech-Communication/ICSLP coverage via Oviatt; SIGdial/SemDial coverage via error-recovery papers; no distinct ACM CHI paper confirmed by title in my search results) — the report-writer should not assume ACM CHI specifically has been checked.

## Which findings look universal across languages vs. tied to English/Switchboard-style telephone conversation specifically?

### Takeaway
Structural/cognitive-architecture claims (the reparandum/IP/interregnum/repair skeleton; Levelt's monitoring-via-comprehension-system model; the three-phase repair process; hyperarticulation as an adaptive response to perceived misrecognition) look like genuinely general, settled science, supported by the fact they were independently established or replicated outside English (Levelt's own foundational work was on **Dutch**, not English; hyperarticulation has at least one multilingual replication; Indian-language disfluency annotation (Kochar et al.) reports high inter-annotator agreement using an adapted version of the same reparandum/repair distinction). Lexical/surface-level claims (the specific editing-term inventory, the >50%-repetitions prevalence figure, the specific finding that disfluency rate is 5.9% of tokens) are tied to the Switchboard telephone-conversation register and should not be assumed to transfer to dialectal, code-switched, or task-oriented voice-AI speech without re-measurement.

### Cited Findings
- Levelt's original self-repair corpus and model were built from **Dutch** spontaneous speech, not English — meaning the foundational cognitive model already was not English-specific from inception, even though most of the NLP annotation tooling built on top of it (Switchboard) is English. — [Levelt 1983, Cognition 14:41-104](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_64752_4/component/file_64753/1983_levelt_monitoring.pdf)
- French spontaneous-speech corpora have independently applied comparable disfluency/editing-term annotation and analysis (e.g., prosodic variation of identical repetitions as a function of editing terms, large-scale French corpus study), indicating the Shriberg-style structural scheme has already been ported to at least one other European language beyond the original English/Dutch base. — [Prosodic Variation of Identical Repetitions... French Speech](https://dial.uclouvain.be/pr/boreal/object/boreal:182968); [Automatic Detection and Annotation of Disfluencies in Spoken French Corpora](https://dial.uclouvain.be/pr/boreal/object/boreal:161551/datastream/PDF_01/view)
- The DISCO corpus extends disfluency *correction* annotation (a closely related but distinct task from Switchboard-style structural annotation) to **English, Hindi, German, and French** as a unified Indo-European benchmark, released under **CC BY 4.0** with code/data on GitHub (github.com/vineet2104/DISCO), accepted at EMNLP 2023 Findings — direct evidence the field already treats cross-language disfluency-correction annotation as both feasible and a going concern, with at least this one dataset being fully open/reusable (unlike the LDC-gated Switchboard releases). — [DISCO paper, EMNLP 2023 Findings](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-emnlp.855/); [GitHub: vineet2104/DISCO](https://github.com/vineet2104/DISCO)
- Kochar et al.'s extension to six Indian languages, and the reported high Kappa agreement (92 Hindi, 89 Bengali) for what is described as the same reparandum/repair-style distinction, is further evidence the *structural* annotation scheme generalizes, even while the paper itself explicitly frames Indian-language disfluency *behavior* (not just annotation feasibility) as requiring new, language-specific guidelines because "unlike English, [these] datasets... lack comprehensive guidelines regarding disfluency behavior." — [aclanthology.org/2024.wildre-1.1](https://aclanthology.org/2024.wildre-1.1/)
- Conversely, Switchboard-specific statistics (5.9% disfluent tokens; >50% of disfluencies being repetitions) are reported as properties of *this corpus* (English, telephone, stranger-pairs, assigned topic) with no cross-linguistic comparison found in the retrieved literature — these numbers should be treated as a single-register baseline, not a universal constant. — [search synthesis, Switchboard disfluency statistics](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/disfluencies_in_switchboard.pdf)

### Inferences
- A reasonable layered claim for the broader synthesis: **Layer 1 (universal, settled)** — the cognitive existence of self-monitoring and a structural repair grammar (reparandum/IP/interregnum/repair), established across Dutch, English, and (per annotation feasibility) Indian languages. **Layer 2 (plausibly general, less fully confirmed)** — the behavioral adaptation pattern of hyperarticulation/targeted re-correction after a perceived misunderstanding, established robustly in English and with at least one multilingual (Interspeech 2017) data point suggesting recurrence elsewhere, but not yet confirmed across dialects or code-switching contexts. **Layer 3 (register-specific, not assumed to generalize)** — specific prevalence rates, specific editing-term lexicons, and the repetition-dominance statistic, which are properties of the Switchboard telephone-conversation register and should be re-measured for any new language/dialect/modality before being relied upon.

### Gaps
- No direct literature was found comparing disfluency *rates* or *type distributions* (repetition vs. substitution vs. restart proportions) cross-linguistically or across dialects of the same language — this would be needed to know whether, e.g., a heavily code-switched or dialect-inflected utterance shows a different disfluency-type profile than Switchboard's English baseline. Flag as open question for the broader project.
- No literature was found specifically testing whether Levelt's well-formedness rule for repairs (syntactic well-formedness of the concatenated pre/post-interruption text) holds in languages with substantially different word order or morphological structure from Dutch/English (e.g., strongly agglutinative or verb-final Indian languages) — the Kochar et al. paper gestures at "linguistic nuances" requiring special handling but I could not confirm whether this specific formal constraint was tested or revised.
