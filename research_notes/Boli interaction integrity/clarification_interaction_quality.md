## Key Question 1: Are targeted/specific clarification questions established as more effective than generic ones ("can you repeat that?")? Is there a formal taxonomy of clarification types, and evidence for which types work best in which situations?

### Takeaway
Yes — this is well-established, decades-old ground. There is a canonical linguistic taxonomy (Ginzburg & Purver and colleagues) classifying clarification requests by function (Repetition, Clausal/Confirmation, Intended-content, Correction), and a separate, more recent, empirically-validated literature (2020s, including EACL 2024) showing that specific/targeted clarification questions outperform generic repeat-requests on user satisfaction and resolution. This is not a novel finding to establish — it is foundational prior work to build on.

### Cited Findings
- Ginzburg, Purver, and colleagues proposed the first widely-used scheme classifying the *functions* of clarification requests (CRs), including: **Repetition** (hearer can't identify a surface parameter — "What did you say?"), **Clausal** (uncertain value for a clausal/propositional dialogue-history parameter, typically yes/no confirmable), **Intended** (hearer finds no value for a parameter and needs reformulation — "Who is Bo?"), and **Correction** (hearer believes the speaker erred and offers an alternative — "Did you mean to say 'Bro'?"). — [The Theory and Use of Clarification Requests in Dialogue](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236273309_The_Theory_and_Use_of_Clarification_Requests_in_Dialogue); [On the Means for Clarification in Dialogue](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/2892453_On_the_Means_for_Clarification_in_Dialogue)
- Immediate linguistic context disambiguates which CR type is intended: a repetition reading licenses verbatim repeat of the original utterance; a clausal/confirmation reading typically gets a yes/no answer; an intended-content reading requires the responder to reformulate rather than repeat. — [On the Means for Clarification in Dialogue](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/2892453_On_the_Means_for_Clarification_in_Dialogue)
- "Interest in clarification mechanisms by the artificial intelligence community dates back to the start of the century [2000s]" — i.e., this is explicitly flagged by the field itself as a long-running, not new, line of work. — [MAC: A Multi-Agent Framework for Interactive User Clarification](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.13154v1)
- Generic requests for a repeat/rephrase ("What did you say?"/"Please repeat") carry no contextual information from the prior utterance, whereas targeted clarification questions reference the specific part of the utterance thought to be misheard/misunderstood (using the portions believed correctly recognized), to pinpoint the exact source of confusion. — [search synthesis, multiple sources including USPTO patent "Clarifying natural language input using targeted questions"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9805718)
- A USPTO patent specifically covers "Targeted clarification questions in speech recognition with concept presence score and concept correctness score" — i.e., targeted (vs. generic) clarification as an ASR-specific engineering technique has already been patented/productized. — [USPTO patent 9953644](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9953644)
- "Clarifying the Path to User Satisfaction: An Investigation into Clarification Usefulness" (Rahmani, Wang, Aliannejadi, Naghiaei, Yilmaz; Findings of ACL: EACL 2024, pp. 1266-1277) finds that **specificity, sentiment, and query characteristics significantly impact user satisfaction** with clarifying questions in conversational search/IR, and builds models (random forest, BERT, LLMs) predicting clarification usefulness with "a minimum performance boost of 45%" over baselines (exact baseline/metric not specified in the snippet). — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-eacl.84/); [arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01934)
- A 2024 SIGDIAL paper, "Analyzing and Enhancing Clarification Strategies for..." directly studies clarification strategy quality/enhancement (title truncated in search results; full scope not confirmed from snippet alone). — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.sigdial-1.25.pdf)
- A recent (2026 IWSDS / arXiv 2512) "MAC" multi-agent clarification framework proposes a newer taxonomy of *ambiguity types* (not CR function types) to drive clarification strategy: Domain Ambiguity (query could match multiple domains), Intent Ambiguity (domain clear, goal unclear), Vague Goal Specification (query too broad to act on), Contextual Disambiguation (unclear referents like "it"/"that place"). — [MAC paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.13154v1); [OpenReview version](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=pDOtqzaZAf)

### Inferences
- There are now (at least) two parallel taxonomic traditions: an older *linguistic/functional* taxonomy of CRs (Ginzburg & Purver, early-2000s, focused on *why* the system doesn't understand) and a newer *ambiguity-type* taxonomy from the LLM-agent era (2024-2026, focused on *what kind of information is missing*). A research project building a clarification-strategy component should situate itself relative to both, and should not present "targeted beats generic" as a new finding — it is textbook.
- The fact that "targeted clarification" is independently patented (ASR-specific) and separately studied in IR/conversational-search (EACL 2024) suggests the generic-vs-specific finding has been independently rediscovered/re-validated across at least three different subfields (SDS/ASR repair, conversational search, and now LLM agents), which strengthens confidence in the effect but also means claiming novelty here would be weak.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm the *exact* numeric effect sizes from the EACL 2024 paper (e.g., correlation of specificity with satisfaction, or what the "45% minimum performance boost" baseline/metric actually was) because WebFetch to arxiv.org/aclanthology.org was not attempted for this specific paper and only search snippets were available.
- I did not find a study that directly and quantitatively compares CR-taxonomy *type* (Repetition vs. Clausal vs. Intended vs. Correction) against task outcomes in a controlled experiment — the Ginzburg/Purver work establishes the taxonomy and linguistic licensing conditions, but head-to-head effectiveness-by-type evidence was not surfaced in this pass.

## Key Question 2: What is "inappropriate clarification request" research? When does a system ask for clarification it shouldn't, and what does this cost?

### Takeaway
This is an established, named sub-literature with roots in classic spoken dialogue systems (SDS) work and renewed attention from the LLM-agent era ("over-asking"). The core finding across both eras is consistent: clarification requests triggered by misidentified error type/location feel confusing or unjustified to users, and asking unnecessary or excessive clarification questions measurably degrades efficiency/user experience without improving (and sometimes while hurting) task success.

### Cited Findings
- "Detecting Inappropriate Clarification Requests in Spoken Dialogue Systems" is a named paper/research line: spoken dialogue systems ask for clarification when they believe they've misunderstood the user, but when the error type or location is *misidentified*, the resulting clarification request appears confusing or inappropriate to the user. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/301405052_Detecting_Inappropriate_Clarification_Requests_in_Spoken_Dialogue_Systems)
- A classifier built to detect such inappropriate CRs, trained on features extracted from user responses in laboratory studies, achieved **88.5% accuracy and .885 F-measure**. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/301405052_Detecting_Inappropriate_Clarification_Requests_in_Spoken_Dialogue_Systems)
- In the modern LLM-agent literature, "over-asking" is an explicitly named failure mode: current frontier models show behavior ranging from over-asking (reported in **52% of sessions** in one study) to never asking at all — i.e., poor calibration in *when* to ask is still unsolved even in frontier LLM agents as of the 2025-2026 papers surfaced. — [Ask Early, Ask Late, Ask Right](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.07937)
- On fully-specified tasks (i.e., where no clarification is actually needed), unnecessary questions are dominated by three categories: style preferences, restatement of information already present in the prompt/data, and out-of-scope requests — i.e., over-asking is "rarely a legitimate response to missing task information." — [Asking What Matters: Reward-Driven Clarification for Software Engineering Tasks](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.14624)
- Excessive/over-clarification introduces "context pollution" and redundant interactions that disrupt an agent's reasoning flow without adding task-relevant constraints; the number of clarification questions asked can be disproportionate to the performance improvement gained, with success rates plateauing despite larger question sets while the proportion of *answerable* questions actually decreases as question count grows. — [Asking What Matters](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.14624); [related alphaxiv synthesis](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.07937)
- A reward-design approach grounded in empirical analysis of information impact and user-answerability matched a strong baseline's (GPT-5's) resolution rate while asking **41% fewer questions** — framed as evidence that better-targeted (not just more) clarification is the efficient path. — [Asking What Matters](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.14624)

### Inferences
- "Inappropriate clarification" research has essentially re-emerged twice: once in classical SDS (ASR-era, ~2015-ish based on the ResearchGate paper's topic area, though exact publication year wasn't confirmed) framed as *misattributed error type/location*, and again in the 2024-2026 LLM-agent era framed as *miscalibrated decision to ask at all* ("over-asking" vs. "under-asking"). A voice-AI project studying interaction breakdown should treat "don't ask for clarification you don't need" as an established cost-bearing failure mode, not a novel insight, and should cite both eras to show awareness of the lineage.
- The common thread across both eras is a *calibration* problem — the system's internal confidence/uncertainty estimate about what's actually wrong doesn't match reality, and clarification is the costly, user-facing symptom of that miscalibration. This maps directly onto a "detect breakdown and pick the right repair" framing: the research gap isn't "should we ask" (well-studied) but "how do we estimate, moment to moment, whether we actually need to."

### Gaps
- I did not find a precise publication year/venue/authors for "Detecting Inappropriate Clarification Requests in Spoken Dialogue Systems" beyond the ResearchGate listing — would need a direct fetch (blocked) to confirm venue and exact methodology (what "laboratory studies" and what features).
- I did not find quantified user-experience cost metrics (e.g., drop in satisfaction score, increase in abandonment rate) specifically tied to *inappropriate* clarification in the classical SDS paper — the modern LLM-agent papers give efficiency/question-count metrics but not satisfaction-score deltas.

## Key Question 3: What is PARADISE and the Interaction Quality tradition? How far back does live, in-conversation quality/satisfaction estimation go, how mature/solved is it, and how has it been used to adapt dialogue policy in real time?

### Takeaway
PARADISE (1997, Walker/Litman/Kamm/Abella) is the foundational framework for dialogue system evaluation generally, but it is a **post-hoc/offline** regression framework, not inherently a live estimator. The specific "live, turn-by-turn, in-conversation" estimation tradition is **Interaction Quality (IQ)**, developed mainly by Schmitt, Ultes, and Minker starting around 2011-2012 on the LEGO (CMU Let's Go) corpus, explicitly as a real-time, automatically-computable proxy for user satisfaction intended to drive online dialogue policy adaptation/reinforcement learning. This makes "estimate how the conversation is going, live" an established research tradition dating back roughly 15 years (and conceptually traceable to PARADISE's ~29-year-old roots), not a new idea — but it is also explicitly still an active research area as of 2023-2025, not a solved problem, with ongoing work (BiLSTMs, hybrid-HMMs, SLU-confidence-based prediction, integrative reviews) still trying to improve turn-level estimation accuracy and its downstream use in policy learning.

### Cited Findings
- PARADISE ("PARAdigm for DIalogue System Evaluation") was developed by Marilyn A. Walker, Diane J. Litman, Candace A. Kamm, and Alicia Abella, originally published in 1997 (ACL). It decouples task requirements from dialogue behavior via a decision-theoretic framework, using a linear combination of task success and dialogue "costs" (e.g., duration) to predict/model user satisfaction, enabling comparison across dialogue strategies and even across different tasks by normalizing for task complexity. It remains "one of the earliest frameworks... still widely used as a baseline." — [PARADISE original paper](https://aclanthology.org/P97-1035.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/PARADISE:-A-Framework-for-Evaluating-Spoken-Agents-Walker-Litman/2aa2db01525c36f5717de5abf83f04ad32481331); [The PARADISE Evaluation Framework: Issues and Findings](https://aclanthology.org/J06-2004.pdf)
- PARADISE relies on explicit task-success information (often hard to obtain automatically/in real time), which is a recognized limitation when trying to use it as a live, continuously-computable reward signal. — [search synthesis, reinforcement-learning dialogue policy literature]
- The **Interaction Quality (IQ)** paradigm, built on the LEGO (Let's Go bus information system) corpus by Schmitt, Ultes, and Minker, models interaction quality on a discrete 1-5 scale ("extremely unsatisfied" to "satisfied") computed from a **three-level feature hierarchy**: exchange level (the current turn), window level (last three exchanges), and dialogue level (the whole dialogue so far) — explicitly designed to be automatically and continuously computable during the live interaction, not only after the fact. — [Analysis of an Extended Interaction Quality Corpus](https://www.uni-ulm.de/fileadmin/website_uni_ulm/iui.iwsds.2015/iwsds2015_submission_4.pdf); [Interaction Quality: Assessing the quality of ongoing spoken dialog interaction by experts](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282322614_Interaction_Quality_Assessing_the_quality_of_ongoing_spoken_dialog_interaction_by_experts-And_how_it_relates_to_user_satisfaction)
- Interaction parameters for IQ estimation span both the signal level (number of turns, turn duration, pause duration, speech/noise levels) and the symbolic level (word count, word accuracy, concept accuracy, appropriateness of system prompts, etc.). — [search synthesis]
- "Automatic turn and dialogue level quality evaluation of end-user interactions with SDS is vital for identifying problematic conversations and for optimizing dialogue policy using a data-driven approach, such as reinforcement learning" — and "getting a good estimation of the Interaction Quality (IQ) of a spoken dialogue helps increase user satisfaction, as the dialogue strategy may be adapted accordingly" — i.e., the explicit, stated purpose of IQ research from early on was real-time policy adaptation. — [search synthesis of IQ literature]
- "Improving Interaction Quality Estimation with BiLSTMs and the Impact on Dialogue Policy Learning" (arXiv, relatively recent) reports a proposed model predicting defects with **>76% correlation with live interaction-quality defects**, vs. a **46% baseline** — direct evidence that improving live IQ estimation is still an active, incrementally-improving research problem, not solved. — [arXiv 2001.07615](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.07615)
- Earlier IQ work used Hybrid-HMMs for turn-level IQ estimation (SIGDIAL/related workshop, 2014). — [ACL Anthology W14-4328](https://aclanthology.org/W14-4328/)
- An IQ-based reward estimator trained on dialogues from a bus information system (i.e., the LEGO/IQ tradition) produced well-performing dialogue policies (in both success rate and user satisfaction) across multiple domains, using only domain-independent interaction-related information — directly demonstrating IQ-as-reward-signal for cross-domain dialogue policy learning/transfer. — [search synthesis, dialogue policy RL literature]
- More recent work (2023, ACM UMAP/related venue) addresses "Predicting Interaction Quality of Conversational Assistants With Spoken Language Understanding Model Confidences" — using SLU model confidence scores as a live predictive signal for IQ, showing the field continues to refine what *signals* feed the live estimate (an evolution toward richer, multi-signal inputs rather than a single aggregate score). — [ACM DL](https://doi.org/10.1145/3583780.3615493)
- A 2025+ "integrative review of 125 empirical studies" (2017-2025) on user-perceived interaction quality across text-, voice-, and LLM-powered systems identifies three layers of user judgment: a pragmatic core (usability, task effectiveness, conversational competence), a social-affective layer (social presence, warmth, synchronicity), and an accountability/inclusion layer (explanation efficacy, transparency, accessibility, fairness) — indicating the field has broadened well beyond the original PARADISE/IQ scope (task success + cost) into a much richer, multi-dimensional construct, and that synthesis/review work on this topic is still being actively produced. — [MDPI integrative review, "Assessing Interaction Quality in Human–AI Dialogue"](https://www.mdpi.com/2504-4990/8/2/28)
- Separately, "First Insight into Quality-Adaptive Dialogue" and "Dialogue Management for User-Centered Adaptive Dialogue" are named prior works specifically on using quality estimates to adapt dialogue management live. — [ResearchGate: First Insight into Quality-Adaptive Dialogue](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262787637_First_Insight_into_Quality-Adaptive_Dialogue); [ResearchGate: Dialogue Management for User-Centered Adaptive Dialogue](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260121727_Dialogue_Management_for_User-Centered_Adaptive_Dialogue)
- A 2026 paper, "Optimizing Conversational Quality in Spoken Dialogue..." (ACL Findings 2026 per the listing) indicates the live-quality-estimation-for-optimization line of work is continuing into the newest LLM-era spoken dialogue systems. — [ACL Anthology, 2026.findings-acl.2040](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.2040.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Estimate how the conversation is going, live" is **not novel** — it is a ~15-year-old named sub-field (IQ) sitting on top of a ~29-year-old evaluation paradigm (PARADISE), with an explicitly stated original purpose (since at least the early 2010s) of driving real-time dialogue policy adaptation via reinforcement learning. Any voice-AI project proposing "detect interaction quality live and adapt" needs to position itself clearly against PARADISE → IQ → IQ-as-RL-reward as prior art, not as the innovation itself.
- However, the field is **not stalled or solved** — correlation/accuracy numbers (e.g., 76% vs. 46% baseline) show real headroom remains in turn-level estimation accuracy, and the signal set feeding these estimators has been evolving (from hand-crafted signal/symbolic features → BiLSTMs/HMMs → SLU-confidence-based features → today's broader social/accountability dimensions). This is the clearest opening for "what's new": IQ work has mostly compressed signal into a single ordinal quality/satisfaction score per turn; the richer, more *structured and semantically interpretable* signal set a voice-AI project proposes (e.g., semantic progress, repeated-slot-request counts, repair-attempt counts as distinct tracked quantities rather than inputs collapsed into one scalar) is a plausible, defensible point of differentiation — reframing from "predict a single satisfaction number" to "track distinct, interpretable breakdown indicators that jointly diagnose *why* a conversation is degrading, to select a *matched* repair strategy" rather than just a generic policy switch.
- The explicit multi-level feature hierarchy of IQ (exchange/window/dialogue level) is conceptually close to what a "repeated-slot-request count" or "repair-attempt count" would be — meaning such signals are not unprecedented in kind, but IQ's published feature sets (per the search snippets) emphasize ASR/SLU accuracy, turn/pause timing, and prompt appropriateness, not explicitly semantic-progress tracking or repair-attempt-as-a-named-cumulative-counter. This is a point the project should verify by reading an actual IQ feature-parameter list in full (not accessible this pass) before claiming it as a gap.

## Key Question 4: What recent (2023-2026) work studies real customer frustration (not generic sentiment) in a DEPLOYED task-oriented system? What methods did it use, and how did LLM-based detection perform vs. baselines on their internal benchmark?

### Takeaway
The clearest, most directly on-point paper is **"Stupid robot, I want to speak to a human!" User Frustration Detection in Task-Oriented Dialog Systems** (arXiv 2411.17437, also published at COLING 2025 Industry track), which explicitly frames itself as the first work on **unsupervised** LLM-based user-frustration detection in a **deployed** TOD system, comparing a deployed keyword-based production baseline, open-source sentiment/emotion models, dialogue-breakdown-detection methods, and in-context-learning LLM-based detection. The deployed keyword baseline achieved near-zero recall (catching real frustration almost entirely by luck), while the LLM-based approach substantially outperformed all alternatives on their internal benchmark.

### Cited Findings
- Paper: "'Stupid robot, I want to speak to a human!' User Frustration Detection in Task-Oriented Dialog Systems" — published as arXiv:2411.17437 (Nov 2024) and as a COLING 2025 Industry Track paper. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17437); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17437v1); [ACL Anthology / COLING 2025 Industry](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-industry.23/)
- The paper presents "a unique perspective from industry, analyzing user frustration in conversations from a deployed TOD system," and is described (in its own framing, per search synthesis) as the **first work targeting unsupervised user frustration detection using LLMs within deployed TOD systems**. — [arXiv/ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv241117437H/abstract)
- Methods compared: (1) the system's existing **deployed keyword-based approach**, (2) **open-source sentiment analysis** methods, (3) **dialogue breakdown detection** methods, and (4) an **in-context-learning LLM-based detector**. — [arXiv/COLING paper synthesis](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-industry.23.pdf)
- The deployed **keyword-based baseline achieved 100% precision but only ~1% recall** for frustrated conversations, yielding a **Macro-F1 of 41%** — i.e., in production, keyword matching almost never catches real frustration, because (per the paper's own argument) real-world frustration is expressed through repetition, negation, and unresolved tasks rather than profanity or explicit negative-sentiment words. — [search synthesis of paper]
- **Traditional sentiment/emotion models performed poorly**, especially when run over the full conversation history, indicating weak contextual understanding of frustration as distinct from generic negative sentiment. — [search synthesis of paper]
- The **LLM-based in-context-learning approach outperformed all other methods**: >33% relative Macro-F1 improvement over sentiment/emotion detection methods, and >22% relative Macro-F1 improvement over dialogue-breakdown-detection methods. (Note: an earlier search pass also surfaced a "16% relative improvement in F1" figure for the LLM approach on the internal benchmark — this may refer to a different baseline comparison within the same paper, e.g., vs. the strongest non-LLM baseline overall rather than vs. sentiment/breakdown methods specifically; I could not fully reconcile the 16% figure against the 33%/22% figures from the two separate search passes without full-text access, so both are reported here and should be treated as needing reconciliation against the primary source.) — [search synthesis, two separate queries][arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.17437)
- Related/adjacent papers surfaced but not read in full: "TRACER: Early Failure Detection for Task-Oriented Dialogue" (arXiv, proposes early detection of dialogue failure, conceptually adjacent to frustration detection but framed around task failure rather than user affect) and "Reliable LLM-based User Simulator for Task-Oriented Dialogue Systems" (ACL SciChat 2024, about simulating users rather than detecting frustration in real deployed logs). — [TRACER](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.03974); [Reliable LLM-based User Simulator](https://aclanthology.org/2024.scichat-1.3/)
- A practitioner/industry blog ("How to detect user frustration in your LLM agent") also surfaced, suggesting the "Stupid robot" paper has already propagated into applied/industry discourse as a reference point, not just academic citation. — [Latitude blog](https://latitude.so/blog/how-to-detect-user-frustration-in-your-llm-agent)

### Inferences
- This paper is a strong, directly-relevant anchor citation for the Boli project: it is recent (2024/2025), industry-grounded (real deployed system, not a lab study), explicitly contrasts simple/keyword/sentiment baselines against LLM-based detection, and explicitly argues that **frustration is linguistically and behaviorally distinct from generic negative sentiment** (expressed via repetition, negation, unresolved tasks) — this directly supports a thesis that "frustration/breakdown detection needs structured, dialogue-specific signals (e.g., repeated-slot-request count, repair-attempt count) rather than a generic sentiment/satisfaction score," since the paper's own argument is that generic sentiment models fail specifically because they miss these structural/behavioral cues.
- The fact that this paper was *industry*-authored and chose **unsupervised, in-context-learning LLM detection** (rather than a fine-tuned classifier) suggests the field has moved toward LLM-as-judge-style detection for frustration as the current practical state of the art circa 2024-2025, which is useful context for method selection in the Boli project, but also means "use an LLM to judge frustration" is not itself a novel method choice.

## Key Question 5: Is there experimental evidence on the downsides of repeating the same question/prompt verbatim vs. rephrasing it? Does older SDS literature establish that verbatim repetition specifically degrades user impression even when the question is correct?

### Takeaway
Yes, and this is classic, decades-old SDS literature. A scoping review citing the NIST 2000 Communicator Evaluation (nine mixed-initiative telephone dialogue systems) reports that using **repetition as a system repair strategy produced significantly higher rates of user frustration than rephrasing** — direct experimental/corpus evidence from the original golden era of SDS research that verbatim repetition specifically (not just "the system made an error") degrades user experience.

### Cited Findings
- "System and User Strategies to Repair Conversational Breakdowns of Spoken Dialogue Systems: A Scoping Review" (CUI 2024 / ACM) reports: a study examining the **NIST 2000 Communicator Evaluation**, covering **nine mixed-initiative telephone dialogue systems**, found that **"employing repetitions as a system-repair strategy resulted in significantly higher rates of frustration compared to rephrasing."** — [ACM DL, full HTML](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3640794.3665558); [Strathprints PDF](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/89541/1/Algjamdi-etal-CUI-2024-System-and-user-strategies-to-repair-conversational-breakdowns-of-spoken-dialogue.pdf)
- The same scoping review notes that when systems transfer repair responsibility to the user, they may either repeat the inquiry verbatim (to give the user another chance to articulate their request) or ask the user to rephrase — framing these as the two primary system-initiated repair moves, with repetition and reformulation also being the two primary *user*-initiated repair strategies (most commonly used by users, repetition being the more direct/common one, especially when users feel misunderstood). — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3640794.3665558)
- More generally (not SDS-specific, but corroborating): "when having conversations with repeat-prone chatbots, users are likely to lose interest soon," and "monotonous repetitive utterances may quickly cause users to lose interest in continuing the dialogue" — consistent qualitative framing across the broader repetition-in-dialogue-generation literature. — [search synthesis, dialogue generation repetition literature]
- A newer related paper, "The Art of Repair in Human-Agent Conversations: A Taxonomy of Repair Strategies by Users and LLM-Based Conversational Agents," extends this repair-strategy taxonomy work into the LLM-agent era, suggesting the repetition-vs-rephrasing repair-strategy question is being actively revisited for LLM-based (not just classical SDS) agents. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/395190410_The_Art_of_Repair_in_Human-Agent_Conversations_A_Taxonomy_of_Repair_Strategies_by_Users_and_LLM-Based_Conversational_Agents)

### Inferences
- The NIST 2000 Communicator Evaluation finding is a strong, citable, primary-era empirical anchor for the Boli project's claim that "repeating the same prompt verbatim is a worse repair strategy than rephrasing," and it long predates LLM-based voice AI — meaning the project should present this as confirming/extending known SDS wisdom into a new (LLM voice-AI) setting, not as a novel discovery.

## Key Question 6: Is "estimate how the conversation is going and adapt" itself novel, or well-trodden ground — and if well-trodden, what would be genuinely new about doing it with a richer, structured signal set (semantic progress, repeated-slot-request, repair-attempt count) rather than a single satisfaction score?

### Takeaway
**Well-trodden ground, unambiguously.** The core idea — continuously estimate interaction/conversation quality during a live dialogue and use that estimate to adapt system behavior/policy — is explicitly established research dating to PARADISE (1997) and, as a live/turn-level/policy-adaptation-oriented tradition specifically, to Interaction Quality (IQ) research from roughly the early 2010s onward, with continuous incremental work through 2023-2026. The genuinely open and defensible angle is not "estimate live and adapt" (taken) but (1) using a **richer, interpretable, multi-signal structured state** (e.g., semantic task-progress tracking, a counter for repeated requests of the same slot, a counter for repair attempts) instead of collapsing everything into a single scalar satisfaction/IQ score, and (2) explicitly using that structured state to select *among qualitatively different repair strategies* (not just to trigger a generic "escalate" or "end call" policy action, which is closer to what classical IQ-driven RL policies did).

### Cited Findings
- PARADISE (1997) established user-satisfaction modeling via a linear combination of task success and dialogue cost factors — the founding act of "model how well this dialogue is going" in the field. — [PARADISE original](https://aclanthology.org/P97-1035.pdf)
- Interaction Quality (Schmitt/Ultes/Minker, LEGO corpus, ~2011-2015 era per the IWSDS 2015 "Analysis of an Extended Interaction Quality Corpus" and related citations) operationalized live, turn-by-turn quality estimation using a three-level (exchange/window/dialogue) feature hierarchy, explicitly for "optimizing dialogue policy using a data-driven approach, such as reinforcement learning." — [IWSDS 2015 paper](https://www.uni-ulm.de/fileadmin/website_uni_ulm/iui.iwsds.2015/iwsds2015_submission_4.pdf)
- IQ-based reward estimation has already been used to train cross-domain dialogue policies via RL (bus-information-system-trained IQ reward generalizing to other domains using only domain-independent interaction features) — i.e., "use a live quality estimate to adapt policy" is not just proposed but has been implemented and published. — [search synthesis, dialogue policy RL literature]
- IQ's own published feature set already spans multiple structured, non-scalar signal types (turn count, pause duration, word/concept accuracy, prompt appropriateness) aggregated across exchange/window/dialogue levels — i.e., the *idea* of using multiple structured signals rather than a single input is also not new; what IQ research still collapses these into, however, is a single ordinal **output** (the 1-5 IQ score) used as the adaptation signal, per the scale description found. — [search synthesis, Schmitt/Ultes IQ literature]
- Recent work (2023 ACM UMAP-related paper on SLU-confidence-based IQ prediction; 2025 integrative review of 125 studies spanning text/voice/LLM systems across pragmatic, social-affective, and accountability layers) shows the field is still actively expanding *what signals* feed live quality estimation and *what dimensions* "quality" should even cover — the move from narrow signal sets toward broader, more structured signal sets is an ongoing, recent trend, not something the Boli project would be inventing from nothing, but also not something fully worked out yet either. — [ACM paper](https://doi.org/10.1145/3583780.3615493); [MDPI integrative review](https://www.mdpi.com/2504-4990/8/2/28)
- The 2024 "System and User Strategies to Repair Conversational Breakdowns" scoping review and the 2025 "Art of Repair" taxonomy paper show that *mapping breakdown type to a matched repair strategy* (as opposed to a single generic repair/escalation action) is itself an active, recent (2024-2025) research question being explicitly taxonomized — suggesting the "match repair strategy to diagnosed cause" framing is a live research frontier rather than settled. — [ACM DL scoping review](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3640794.3665558); [Art of Repair taxonomy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/395190410_The_Art_of_Repair_in_Human-Agent_Conversations_A_Taxonomy_of_Repair_Strategies_by_Users_and_LLM-Based_Conversational_Agents)
- The "Stupid robot" frustration paper's central argument — that generic sentiment analysis fails because real frustration shows up as repetition, negation, and unresolved tasks, not explicit negative words — is itself an explicit, recent (2024/2025), industry-validated argument *for* moving beyond a single scalar sentiment/satisfaction signal toward structured behavioral markers, which directly supports (without being identical to) the Boli project's proposed approach. — [arXiv 2411.17437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17437)

### Inferences
- The honest, adversarial verdict: **"Estimate interaction quality live and adapt" is not novel — it is close to 30 years old as a concept (PARADISE) and ~15 years old as a specifically *live/turn-level* tradition (Interaction Quality), with continuous publication through 2026.** A report or paper from this project that presents live quality/satisfaction estimation as its core contribution would be vulnerable to a reviewer simply citing PARADISE and IQ.
- The most defensible novelty claims, based on what this research pass surfaced, are narrower and more specific than "estimate and adapt": (1) **replacing a single aggregated satisfaction/IQ scalar with a small set of named, semantically-interpretable structured counters** (repeated-slot-request count, repair-attempt count, semantic task-progress) that are individually diagnostic of *why* an interaction is degrading, rather than only *that* it is degrading; and (2) using that structured diagnostic state to **select among qualitatively distinct repair strategies** (e.g., switch from repetition to rephrasing, escalate specificity of a clarification question, or change register) rather than the coarser adaptation actions (continue/escalate/end-call, or abstract RL policy-parameter adjustment) that the classical IQ-to-RL-reward pipeline typically produced. Even here, the 2024-2025 "repair strategy taxonomy" papers (Art of Repair; CUI 2024 scoping review) show other researchers are converging on exactly this framing right now, so the opening is real but narrow and contested, not wide open.
- A corollary: the project's framing should explicitly distinguish itself from "another satisfaction-prediction model" (saturated, 15-30 years of prior art) and position itself as being about the **diagnostic/structured-state layer that sits between breakdown detection and repair-strategy selection** — which is the part of this literature that looks least mature and most recently (2024-2026) active, per the "Art of Repair" and frustration-structural-signal findings above.

### Gaps
