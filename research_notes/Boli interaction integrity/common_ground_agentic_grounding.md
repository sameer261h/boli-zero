## What is the "common ground" literature in dialogue/discourse research (classic through modern computational treatments), and what does it establish?

### Takeaway
"Common ground" and "grounding" are decades-old, foundational constructs in psycholinguistics and discourse theory (Clark & Brennan 1991 is the canonical citation), holding that conversation is a *collaborative, incremental process of establishing and verifying mutual belief* — not independent, stateless turns — and this was followed by a line of computational/formal models (Traum 1994 onward) that attempt to operationalize grounding as explicit system state. This is squarely "settled, old" literature, not a new idea; what's new is applying/evaluating it against LLM-based and agentic systems.

### Cited Findings
- Clark and Brennan's "Grounding in Communication" (1991) defines common ground as the collection of mutual knowledge, mutual beliefs, and mutual assumptions essential to communication, and grounding as the interactive, collaborative process of establishing this shared mutual belief. — [Grounding in Communication (PDF)](https://worrydream.com/refs/Clark_H_1991_-_Grounding_in_Communication.pdf); [Wikipedia: Grounding in communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grounding_in_communication)
- Per Clark & Brennan, participants must "coordinate both the content and process" of conversation: content-coordination builds mutual knowledge/beliefs/assumptions, and as gaps are identified, participants coordinate *process* to resolve misunderstandings/misalignments. — [Grounding in Communication (PDF)](https://worrydream.com/refs/Clark_H_1991_-_Grounding_in_Communication.pdf)
- Grounding cues can signal problems (e.g., requests for clarification) or demonstrate understanding (acknowledgment, a relevant next turn, continued attention); participants look for positive evidence of understanding rather than assuming it by default. — [Grounding in Communication (PDF)](https://worrydream.com/refs/Clark_H_1991_-_Grounding_in_Communication.pdf)
- David Traum's 1994 PhD dissertation, "A Computational Theory of Grounding in Natural Language Conversation" (U. Rochester), gives the first major computational formalization: a protocol for determining whether material has been grounded (or what it would take to ground it), tied to agents' mental states, and introduces "grounding acts" — speech acts whose function is specifically to ground other (illocutionary) speech acts. — [Traum computational theory (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/34074816_A_Computational_Theory_of_Grounding_in_Natural_Language_Conversation); [Traum, Computational Approaches to Dialogue (PDF)](https://people.ict.usc.edu/traum/public_html/Papers/traum-computational.pdf)
- Traum & Allen's 1994 ACL paper "Discourse Obligations in Dialogue Processing" and later "Modelling Grounding and Discourse Obligations Using Update Rules" extend this into formal update-rule models of grounding state. — [ACM DL entry](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/974305.974306)
- A later empirically-based computational model of grounding in dialogue (SIGDIAL 2007 lineage) attempts to fit grounding behavior to observed dialogue data rather than purely logical/rule-based specification. — [An empirically based computational model of grounding in dialogue (ACL Anthology PDF)](https://aclanthology.org/2007.sigdial-1.49.pdf)
- Formal (rule/logic-based) approaches to modeling common grounding exist, but "capturing their full complexities in realistic, situated conversations remains a formidable problem"; most existing data-driven grounding literature is limited to closed-domain, goal-oriented settings. — synthesized from search results on computational models of common ground (see multiple sources above); exact primary-source quote not independently verified due to fetch restrictions.
- "Common Ground: Between Formal Pragmatics and Psycholinguistics" (Annual Review of Linguistics) is a recent review bridging the formal-semantics/pragmatics treatment of common ground with the psycholinguistic (Clark-style) treatment — indicating the construct has two historically somewhat separate theoretical lineages (formal pragmatics vs. collaborative/psycholinguistic) that are still being reconciled as of this review. — [Annual Review of Linguistics](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-linguistics-041824-032410)
- A 2021 paper, "Grounding 'Grounding' in NLP," critiques the NLP field's frequent conflation/loose use of "grounding" (symbol grounding vs. Clark-style conversational grounding), signaling that even within NLP the term has drifted from its original communicative-grounding meaning. — [Grounding 'Grounding' in NLP (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.02192)

### Inferences
- The field has a genuine terminological hazard: "grounding" in NLP/ML increasingly means "grounding in external data/documents" (RAG-style) rather than Clark & Brennan's interactional/mutual-belief sense. Any report synthesizing this cluster should explicitly disambiguate "conversational/communicative grounding" (mutual belief maintenance) from "symbol/data grounding" (connecting language to referents/external knowledge), since search results on "grounding" return a mix of both.
- The fact that two theoretical lineages (formal pragmatics and psycholinguistics) are still being actively reconciled in 2024-2025 review literature suggests that despite ~35 years of research, there is no single unified formal model of common ground — multiple partial, task-specific formalizations coexist (Traum's grounding acts/obligations, QUD-based formal pragmatics models, data-driven/empirical grounding models).

## Is there a 2025 survey covering ~448 papers mapping this space? What is its scope/structure?

### Takeaway
Yes — "Building Common Ground in Dialogue: A Survey" (Anikina, Leippert, Ostermann; 2nd LUHME Workshop, ACL Anthology, published/presented October 2025, Bologna) is confirmed as a large-scale survey, and a secondary citation of it reports it covers 448 papers and specifically flags that evaluation frameworks for *dynamic* grounding in LM interactions remain largely absent.

### Cited Findings
- The survey "Building Common Ground in Dialogue: A Survey" by Tatiana Anikina, Alina Leippert, and Simon Ostermann was published in the Proceedings of the 2nd LUHME Workshop, October 2025, Bologna, Italy. — [ACL Anthology: 2025.luhme-1.2](https://aclanthology.org/2025.luhme-1.2/); [DFKI publication page](https://www.dfki.de/en/web/research/projects-and-publications/publication/15981)
- The survey frames common ground as a "heavily loaded term" whose scope ranges from domain-specific/personal shared experiences to common-sense knowledge, and notes that representationally, common ground can be uni- or multi-modal, and static or dynamic. — [search synthesis citing ACL Anthology 2025.luhme-1.2](https://aclanthology.org/2025.luhme-1.2/)
- The survey "outlines different dimensions of common ground and describes modeling approaches for several grounding tasks, discusses issues caused by the lack of common ground in human-LM interactions, and suggests future research directions." — [search synthesis citing ACL Anthology 2025.luhme-1.2](https://aclanthology.org/2025.luhme-1.2/)
- A citing source states: "A recent survey of 448 papers highlights that evaluation frameworks for dynamic grounding in Language Model (LM) interactions remain largely absent. This gap relates to the need to design tasks that require agents to negotiate shared plans, repair misunderstandings, and track commitments across turns." — [search synthesis; original citing paper not individually identified, found via query "Building Common Ground in Dialogue survey Anikina Ostermann gaps future directions"]

### Inferences
- The "~448 papers" figure the user asked me to verify is corroborated by at least one independent citing source describing it as "a recent survey of 448 papers" in the same breath as naming dynamic-grounding evaluation gaps — strongly suggesting this is the Anikina/Leippert/Ostermann LUHME 2025 survey, though I was not able to open the primary PDF to confirm the exact number appears in its own abstract/methodology section.
- The survey's identified gap — "agents [need] to negotiate shared plans, repair misunderstandings, and track commitments across turns" with inadequate evaluation frameworks for *dynamic* (as opposed to static/one-shot) grounding — is the single most directly relevant finding for the Boli project's hypothesis: it is an explicit, recent (2025), large-scale academic confirmation that (a) commitment-tracking across turns is recognized as a core open problem, and (b) current LM evaluation does not adequately test for it.
