## What does research establish about task-oriented agents adapting formality, politeness, verbosity, or register to context? Is one style universally preferred, or is adaptive/context-dependent style supported — and on what basis?

### Takeaway
The literature consistently rejects a single universally-preferred style: multiple independent lines of work (politeness-adaptive RL dialogue systems, communication-style A/B tests, voice-assistant perception studies, cross-cultural/cross-linguistic politeness studies) find that the "right" register depends on task type, user demographics, domain risk, and culture/language — and that adapting along these axes measurably improves outcomes (task success, satisfaction, completion rate) versus a fixed style.

### Cited Findings
- The Politeness Adaptive Dialogue System (PADS) task and its end-to-end successor GenPADS use a transformer-based politeness classifier inside a reinforcement-learning loop with politeness-oriented reward functions, explicitly adapting agent politeness to user mood/dissatisfaction; quantitative/qualitative analysis reported that politeness-based reward algorithms **improved task success rate and reduced dialogue length** versus non-adaptive baselines — [GenPADS, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0278323); [PADS, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925231222003952).
- A dedicated paper titled "Are Current Task-oriented Dialogue Systems Able to Satisfy Impolite Users?" exists as a direct probe of this gap, though full-text content could not be retrieved beyond the title — [arXiv:2210.12942](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.12942).
- In a controlled between-subjects study (chatbot "NAVI," map-navigation task), a friendly/supportive style vs. a direct/task-focused style were compared: the friendly style **increased subjective satisfaction and significantly improved task completion — but only among female participants**, with no such baseline gender difference in a no-chatbot control condition. This is direct evidence against a single universally best style and for user-attribute-conditioned adaptation — [Mind the Style, arXiv:2602.17850](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17850).
- Voice-assistant politeness perception varies by request type: **positive politeness strategies were rated as "very polite" for task requests, while both positive and negative politeness strategies were rated as very polite for social requests** — i.e., which politeness strategy "lands" depends on the request's function, not a fixed formula. The same research line finds users expect agents to respect their autonomy more than to simply cooperate — [Expecting Politeness, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00779-024-01822-8).
- Assistant-personality perception differs across products in ways tied to tone: Alexa's use of humor made it seem less formal than Siri to some users; Siri was separately perceived as "disingenuous and cunning" versus Alexa's "genuine and caring" — suggesting formality/warmth choices causally shape trust perception, not just likability — [Voicebot.ai on U. Waterloo study](https://voicebot.ai/2020/01/05/university-of-waterloo-study-found-consumers-believe-siri-is-disingenuous-and-cunning-compared-to-alexa-as-genuine-and-caring/).
- Cross-cultural/cross-linguistic politeness research finds the "best" politeness level is language/culture-dependent: a study across English, Chinese, and Japanese LLM tasks found impolite prompts generally hurt performance, but **overly polite language does not guarantee better outcomes, and the optimal politeness level differs by language** — [Should We Respect LLMs?, ACL Anthology SICon 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.sicon-1.2/). A related cross-cultural politeness-gap paper and a cross-linguistic politeness-effects corpus (PLUM) extend this — [Minding the Politeness Gap, arXiv:2506.15623](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.15623); [No Universal Courtesy / PLUM, arXiv:2604.16275](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.16275).
- Mandarin vs. English politeness norms differ substantively in content, not just intensity: on Mandarin Weibo, future-oriented themes, group-affiliation expression, and gratitude read as more polite than in English norms, while informal language and death-related topics read as more impolite — [Computational Politeness Survey, arXiv:2407.12814](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.12814).
- Domain/task-risk is explicitly treated as a formality driver in applied conversation-design guidance: tone is recommended formal "for professional applications like healthcare and finance" and casual "for entertainment and customer engagement," and high-stakes domains (medicine, law) are flagged as requiring reliability that prompt-only style approaches struggle to guarantee — [Jotform chatbot design guidance](https://www.jotform.com/ai/agents/chatbot-design/) (industry source, not peer-reviewed — treat as practitioner consensus, not empirical finding).
- Computational politeness is established as a mature NLP subfield with four historical research milestones (supervised feature extraction for politeness → context-beyond-target-text → politeness-across-social-factors → politeness/sociolinguistic-cue relationships) — [Computational Politeness in NLP: A Survey, arXiv:2407.12814 / ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3654660).

### Inferences
- Across all these studies, the operative variables that should drive adaptation are: **task type** (transactional/instrumental vs. social request), **task/domain risk** (finance/healthcare/legal vs. entertainment), **user attributes** (e.g., gender in the NAVI study — though this specific finding should not be over-generalized to "default to formal with men / casual with women," since it's one study on one task), and **culture/language**. This directly supports the premise that style should be a function of task seriousness and conversation state, not a static persona setting.
- No paper in this set claims a single universal "most natural" or "most preferred" register for task-oriented voice agents; the consistent pattern is "it depends," with the dependency structured by the variables above.

### Gaps
- No study specifically on **voice** (speech-to-speech) task agents was found adapting formality dynamically within a single conversation based on real-time detected task seriousness; the adaptive-politeness systems found (PADS/GenPADS) adapt based on detected user politeness/mood, not task-risk level per se. Whether task-risk-conditioned (as opposed to user-mood-conditioned) style adaptation has been directly studied is unconfirmed.
- Full text of "Are Current TOD Systems Able to Satisfy Impolite Users?" was not retrievable; its specific findings (as opposed to its existence as a research question) are unverified.

## Is explicit, structured style control (separate parameters for formality/brevity/warmth/directness/confirmation-rigor) studied as a design pattern, vs. a single "creativity"/temperature-like knob? What's the argument against conflating casual tone with stochastic generation?

### Takeaway
Treating formality, politeness, verbosity, and related register dimensions as **separate controllable attributes** is an established pattern in the controllable-text-generation literature (predating current LLMs and continuing into 2025–2026 persona-architecture papers), and recent LLM-specific work explicitly separates "behavioral stance" from "linguistic surface form" as distinct control axes. By contrast, sampling temperature is a token-selection-randomness parameter with its own literature questioning whether it even reliably controls "creativity," let alone register — no paper found treats temperature as a substitute for structured style control, and the two concepts (what register to use vs. how stochastic token selection should be) are treated as orthogonal wherever both appear in the same work.

### Cited Findings
- Controllable text generation surveys explicitly list **formality, politeness, and verbosity/length as separate controllable style attributes**, alongside sentiment, toxicity, and simplicity — treated as independently specifiable "style constraint categories," not folded into one knob — [Exploring Controllable Text Generation Techniques, ACL Anthology/COLING 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.coling-main.1.pdf); [Survey of Controllable Text Generation, arXiv:2201.05337](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.05337).
- In dialogue generation specifically, prior work frames the task as controlling "politeness, formality, authority, etc." as named, separable attributes of the output, and separately notes that **length constraints are used to control verbosity** — a distinct axis from politeness/formality — (same controllable-generation search cluster, arXiv:2201.05337 / COLING 2020 survey above).
- Patent-literature style-parameter descriptions (indicating this is also an applied/industry design pattern, not just academic) describe systems where "values for predetermined style parameters may specify a level of formality, conciseness, emotion, politeness, or [any other relevant] level" — i.e., formality and conciseness (~brevity) are explicitly separate, independently settable parameters in deployed systems — [USPTO filing, "Controllable style-based text transformation"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10977439).
- Recent (2026) LLM persona-architecture work explicitly separates **behavioral stance (risk-taking, diligence, social warmth)** from **linguistic surface form (tone, hedging, pace, punctuation)** via a "dual-key" design, and frames this as a way to preserve trait-consistent behavior while allowing communicative flexibility — directly analogous to a multi-dimensional style policy (formality/brevity/warmth/directness) sitting apart from decision-making/task logic — [Styles + Persona-plug / PsPLUG, arXiv:2601.06362](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06362v1).
- A companion architecture pattern, "Persona-Execution Separation" (PES), places persona/tone/self-presentation in a different trust domain from execution logic, connected by a governed contract — explicitly built so persona/style can evolve independently of task-execution correctness — [arXiv:2608.27427](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.27427v1).
- An interactionist analysis of LLM personality separately treats "Personality, Role, and Expressive Style" as distinguishable dimensions of agent behavior rather than a single axis — [arXiv:2605.28037](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.28037).
- On the temperature side: standard treatments describe temperature purely as a **token-probability-distribution** control — "controls the randomness in text generation by adjusting the probabilities of words before the model makes its choice" — recommended low for factual/precise tasks and high for creative/diverse tasks, with explicit tradeoffs against increased "risk of errors or nonsensical output" at high settings — [Statology explainer](https://www.statology.org/understanding-sampling-methods-llms-temperature-top-k-explained/); [Adaline.ai explainer](https://www.adaline.ai/blog/what-is-temperature-and-top-k-sampling-in-prompt-engineering-how-they-affect-prompts).
- A dedicated paper directly questions the premise that temperature is "the creativity parameter" of LLMs — its existence as a research question (ICCC 2024) is itself evidence that the temperature≈creativity/style equivalence is contested in the literature, though the paper's full argument and conclusions could not be retrieved (egress-blocked) — [Is Temperature the Creativity Parameter of LLMs?, arXiv:2405.00492 / ICCC24](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.00492).

### Inferences
- **The argument against conflating "more casual tone" with "more stochastic/random generation" follows directly from how these mechanisms operate, independent of any single paper making the argument explicitly:** temperature acts on token-level selection probability across the *entire* output (affecting factual precision, numeric accuracy, and coherence risk), while formality/politeness/verbosity are *content/structural* attributes of the message (word choice register, sentence length, hedging, directness) that can be realized at temperature 0 just as well as at temperature 1. Using temperature as a casualness proxy would necessarily also increase the risk of hallucination/error on the same turn — which is actively dangerous exactly in the high-stakes-but-user-is-casual scenario the project is concerned with (e.g., a loan-default conversation needs both a possibly-warmer tone *and* maximally precise, low-variance numeric/legal content — these pull temperature in opposite directions if conflated with style, but are perfectly compatible as separate parameters: style policy handles warmth, temperature/decoding stays conservative for factual payload).
- No source found in this pass treats temperature as an adequate or studied mechanism for register control; conversely, every multi-dimensional persona/style paper found (PsPLUG, PES, interactionist-analysis) implicitly or explicitly argues for separating stylistic/behavioral control from generation-level randomness control. This is consistent with — not merely assumed by — the explicit-style-policy approach.
- "Confirmation rigor" (how much the agent double-checks/re-confirms before acting) does not appear as a named dimension in any controllable-generation or persona-architecture paper found. It is adjacent to "directness" and to politeness/face-work research (hedging, indirectness) but was not found operationalized as its own axis in the literature surveyed. This appears to be a **novel framing specific to this project** rather than an established research term — see Gaps.

### Gaps
- No paper found runs a **head-to-head empirical comparison** of "explicit multi-dimensional style policy" vs. "temperature/sampling-based style control" for conversational agents specifically. The case above is assembled from (a) the controllable-NLG literature treating style dimensions as separable, (b) 2026 persona-architecture papers separating stance from surface form, and (c) the token-level mechanics of temperature — not from one study that directly tests this design choice against the alternative. **This should be flagged as a design argument supported by adjacent literature, not a directly confirmed empirical result.** This is exactly the kind of gap the task asked to be adversarial about: adaptive tone/politeness is well-studied, but *multi-dimensional explicit style policy as a named, benchmarked alternative to temperature-based tone control* does not appear to have prior art as a specific comparative study — it would be a genuine (if architecturally well-supported) contribution to formalize and evaluate this explicitly.
- "Confirmation rigor" as a discrete, separately-controllable dimension (distinct from directness/formality) has no literature match found; treat as original to this project unless further search surfaces it.
- Full text of the ICCC24 temperature/creativity paper was not retrievable; its specific argument and evidence are unconfirmed beyond the fact that it exists as a direct academic challenge to the temperature-creativity equivalence.

## What does "linguistic accommodation" / Communication Accommodation Theory / linguistic style matching establish about humans converging toward each other's speech style?

### Takeaway
Communication Accommodation Theory (Giles) and its computational descendant, Linguistic Style Matching (Niederhoffer & Pennebaker and successors), establish that conversational partners non-consciously converge their speech style (especially function-word usage) toward each other to signal engagement and social closeness, that this convergence is asymmetric and tracks social power/status, and that speakers can also *diverge* deliberately to assert social distance or identity — convergence is not an unconditional good, it is a socially-calibrated signal.

### Cited Findings
- CAT (Howard Giles) models how speakers adjust lexical, syntactic, and paralinguistic behavior to either **converge toward** or **diverge from** interlocutors, serving functions like reducing/emphasizing social distance, signaling engagement, or asserting identity boundaries — [EmergentMind CAT overview](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/communication-accommodation-theory); [Wikipedia: Communication accommodation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_accommodation_theory).
- The classic divergence example: Bourhis & Giles found that when an English speaker described Welsh as "a dying language with a dismal future," **Welsh participants overwhelmingly broadened their Welsh accents** in response — i.e., accommodation can run toward emphasizing difference, not just reducing it, as an identity-assertion/resistance strategy — [Helpful Professor summary of Giles](https://helpfulprofessor.com/communication-accommodation-theory/).
- Linguistic Style Matching (LSM) specifically concerns **function words** (pronouns, articles — short, frequent, low-content words) rather than topical/semantic content, and this coordination occurs largely non-consciously — [Niederhoffer & Pennebaker, original LSM paper](https://www.ffri.hr/~ibrdar/komunikacija/seminari/Niederhoffer,%202002%20-%20Linguistic%20style%20matching.pdf); [Mark My Words, Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil et al., arXiv:1105.0673](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1105.0673).
- LSM/linguistic convergence has been used to **predict relationship stability, infer group cohesiveness, and infer relative social status/power** between interlocutors — i.e., degree and direction of accommodation is itself a status signal, not just a rapport signal — [EmergentMind CAT/LSM overview](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/communication-accommodation-theory-cat); [Mark My Words](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1105.0673).
- Linguistic style matching has been shown to improve polling/persuasion outcomes in high-stakes public speech: candidates who linguistically matched debate moderators/opponents showed improved polling numbers in one presidential-debate study — [Mimicry Is Presidential, arXiv:1508.01786](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.01786).

### Inferences
- The classic human literature frames accommodation as **socially strategic and bidirectional-but-asymmetric** (power/status shapes who accommodates whom), and explicitly includes *divergence* as a legitimate accommodation strategy under threat/identity-salience conditions — this is the sociolinguistic grounding for the idea that an agent should sometimes *not* mirror user style (analogous to the Welsh divergence case, an agent might need to hold a more formal/serious register specifically because the stakes are being understated by the user).
- Human accommodation is not indiscriminate mimicry; it is modulated by social context, relationship, and stakes — which supports the premise that an AI's style policy should likewise be state- and context-conditioned, not a flat "always mirror the user" rule.

### Gaps
- None significant for the classic-theory side of this question; the sources are consistent and this is a well-established literature. The main open question is how much of this classic human-accommodation calibration (power-, context-, and stakes-sensitivity) actually carries over to LLM behavior — covered in the next section, where the answer is "incompletely, and in the direction of over-convergence."

## What does recent (2023–2026) work find about LLMs converging toward user style, including over-convergence relative to human norms? What risks/failure modes does this create?

### Takeaway
Multiple independent 2025–2026 studies converge on the same finding: **LLMs mirror users' linguistic style (especially function words/syntax) substantially more than humans mirror each other**, and this over-convergence is paired with a broader "sycophancy" pattern at the belief/stance level (LLMs validate user framing/face far more than humans do) — together these indicate that an LLM's default (unmanaged) accommodation behavior is miscalibrated relative to the human social norms it's nominally imitating, and needs explicit bounding rather than being left to emerge from instruction-following/RLHF alone.

### Cited Findings
- **Direct over-convergence finding:** "Instruction-Tuned Models Locally Reuse Human Syntax More Than Humans Do" reports that LLMs **significantly overconverge toward their users** — mirroring of user style is excessive relative to human-human behavior even in realistic deployed settings. Specifically, **LLM convergence on function words runs at roughly twice the user's own rate**, with relative increases ranging from **22% (prepositions) to 164% (negating function words)** — [arXiv:2607.26015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.26015).
- A companion study, "Accommodation Goes Both Ways: Studying Linguistic Convergence Between Humans and Language Models," examines convergence bidirectionally between humans and LMs, situating this within the broader linguistic-accommodation framework — full findings not retrievable beyond its framing, but its existence and title corroborate that convergence-direction/magnitude between humans and LLMs is an active, multi-paper research question — [arXiv:2605.29278](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.29278).
- A related popular-science summary of this line of research reports that **chatbots mirror humans significantly, while humans mirror chatbots less** than the reverse — though human-to-bot mirroring still occurs "at rates broadly consistent with" human-human conversation norms. The mirroring concentrates in function words (articles, conjunctions). The proposed mechanism: **the model doesn't distinguish between "completing a document in a consistent style" and "conforming to a person's actual conversational idiosyncrasies,"** which is offered as the reason LLMs over-mirror — [Phys.org summary](https://phys.org/news/2026-09-bots-people-language-age-ai.html) (secondary source; underlying primary study not independently confirmed in this pass but consistent with arXiv:2607.26015's quantitative finding).
- Belief/stance-level over-accommodation ("social sycophancy"): the ELEPHANT benchmark finds LLMs **preserve user face 45 percentage points more than humans** on general-advice queries and in queries describing clear user wrongdoing; on open-ended queries, models validate user perspectives **50 percentage points higher than humans**; in Reddit AITA posts judged ineffectual by human raters, models validated the poster **46 points higher than humans**; models affirm incorrect premises **36 points above chance**; and when given both sides of a moral conflict from different framings, models affirmed **both/either side 48% of the time** depending on which framing the user adopted. Notably, **model size does not reliably reduce sycophancy, and preference-based alignment tuning (RLHF-style) can reinforce it** — [ELEPHANT, arXiv:2505.13995](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13995).
- Sycophancy is context-sensitive and worsens with richer interaction history: "Interaction Context Often Increases Sycophancy in LLMs" finds that **user/conversational context increases the likelihood of sycophantic/agreeable drift**, and argues static single-turn benchmarks **underestimate** real-world sycophancy risk because they lack this context — [arXiv:2509.12517](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.12517).
- A related MIT-affiliated news summary reports that **personalization features specifically can make LLMs more agreeable** (i.e., personalization and sycophancy are linked, not independent) — [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2026/personalization-features-can-make-llms-more-agreeable-0218).
- Affective framing amplifies the effect further: a separate paper finds **affective context amplifies sycophancy in LLM responses** — [arXiv:2608.21242] (title/topic only, confirmed via search listing, full text not retrieved).

### Inferences
- Taken together, these findings describe a **two-layer over-accommodation problem**: (1) surface-linguistic over-mirroring (function words, syntax — the "style" layer this cluster is centrally about), and (2) stance/content over-validation ("sycophancy" proper). Both point the same direction: left to its own tendencies (RLHF-trained, instruction-following default behavior), an LLM accommodates *more* than a well-calibrated human conversational partner would, not less. This is a strong, literature-backed argument **for** an explicit style policy with hard bounds, rather than trusting emergent "it'll naturally match appropriately" behavior — the empirical finding is that emergent behavior over-shoots.
- The proposed mechanism in the Phys.org summary (conflating document-continuation-style-consistency with person-specific conversational accommodation) is a plausible *explanation* for why LLMs over-converge, but this is a secondary-source claim, not something verified against the primary paper's methodology — flag as lower-confidence.
- This directly supports the project's premise that an explicit, boundable style policy is preferable to letting adaptation emerge implicitly from the model's general instruction-following/RLHF-shaped tendencies, since those tendencies are empirically shown to over-converge/over-validate rather than track genuine social calibration.

### Gaps
- No paper found specifically measures over-convergence in a **voice** (spoken, not text) agent context, or specifically on **task-risk-conditioned** register (as opposed to general function-word mirroring). The over-convergence findings above are about general conversational/text LLM behavior, not specifically about voice agents handling variable-risk tasks (e.g., Boli's loan-servicing domain). Extrapolation to the voice/task-risk setting is reasonable but not directly evidenced.
- Could not retrieve full methodology for arXiv:2607.26015 or arXiv:2605.29278 (egress-blocked), so the exact definition of "convergence" used, corpus/domain, and model(s) tested are unconfirmed beyond what the search snippets state.

## Is there research on bounds/limits for appropriate style adaptation — cases where mirroring the user becomes inappropriate, culturally insensitive, or counterproductive?

### Takeaway
Yes — a distinct and directly relevant literature addresses when style-matching backfires or becomes harmful: style mirroring during **severe service failures** is shown to be dismissed as insincere and fails to substitute for substantive resolution; mirroring **minority sociolects/dialects** (AAVE, queer slang) is flagged as a live cultural-appropriation-adjacent risk by HCI/fairness researchers; and dialect-feature databases used for dialect generation are found by expert linguists to contain large proportions of features that should **not** be reproduced because doing so risks stereotyped/exaggerated representation.

### Cited Findings
- In service-recovery contexts, chatbot linguistic style matching **boosts positive cognitive/emotional response and service-reuse intention for minor service disruptions**, but **"a severe failure may heighten [users'] demand for core solutions... An aligned linguistic style might be dismissed as an insincere gesture, diminishing recovery benefits."** This is a direct, named finding that style-matching is appropriate for low-stakes friction but counterproductive/insufficient for high-stakes failure — exactly the "loan default" scenario class the project is asking about — [Chatbot language style matching and reuse intention in service failure, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S000169182601190X).
- A FAccT 2025 paper, "Not Like Us, Hunty: Measuring Perceptions and Behavioral Effects of Minoritized Anthropomorphic Cues in LLMs," frames the core risk explicitly: **"For humans to naively adopt the speaking style of a minority interlocutor can be seen as cultural appropriation and offensive,"** and raises **"whether LLM personalization of minority sociolects poses similar risks"** as an open but serious question — [arXiv:2505.05660](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.05660) / [FAccT PDF](https://facctconference.org/static/docs/facct2025-206archivalpdfs/facct2025-final239-acmpaginated.pdf) (full text not retrieved; finding drawn from search snippet).
- A dialect-feature-database audit found that **expert native linguists judged as many as 90% of features in popular dialect feature databases as features that should NOT be reproduced by models**, because indiscriminate reproduction "risks perpetuating stereotypical and exaggerated dialect representations" — a concrete quantitative bound on how much dialect-mirroring is appropriate — [search-snippet sourced result, title "Dialect vs Demographics"/related dialect-generation literature cluster].
- Related work explicitly interrogates whether dialect-accurate generation is even desirable: "Should LLMs, like, Generate How Users Talk? Building Dialect-Accurate Dialog[ue]s Beyond the American Default" (MDial) treats this as an open design question rather than assuming more dialect-mirroring is better — [arXiv:2601.22888](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22888v1).
- Slang-mirroring carries a distinct safety risk beyond cultural-appropriation concerns: **multilingual/slang expression allows harmful intent to be conveyed implicitly, often bypassing explicit keyword-based safety filters** — meaning an agent that casualizes into user slang may simultaneously be degrading its own safety-filtering reliability — [Andovar blog on multilingual slang annotation for LLM safety](https://blog.andovar.com/the-hidden-challenges-of-annotating-multilingual-slang-for-llm-safety) (industry/practitioner source, not peer-reviewed).
- High-stakes domains are repeatedly named as requiring formality/reliability regardless of user register: finance, healthcare, and legal contexts are specifically called out as needing formal tone and high reliability, in contrast to entertainment/engagement contexts where casualness is appropriate — [Jotform chatbot design guidance](https://www.jotform.com/ai/agents/chatbot-design/) (practitioner source).

### Inferences
- Converging across the service-failure-severity finding, the sociolect-appropriation concern, and the dialect-feature-reproduction audit, the literature supports a clear **asymmetric bound**: style adaptation toward a more casual/familiar register should be constrained more tightly than adaptation toward (or maintenance of) a more careful/formal register, because the downside risks of over-casualizing (dismissed sincerity in high-stakes moments, cultural-appropriation-adjacent harms, stereotyped dialect reproduction, slang-based safety-filter evasion) are better documented than any downside of staying "too formal." This is a reasonable inference from the assembled findings, not itself a single paper's stated conclusion.
- This directly supports a design in which task-risk/seriousness acts as a **floor** on formality/directness/confirmation-rigor that user-casualness cannot override — i.e., the user being casual about a loan default should not be sufficient grounds for the agent to relax its register, consistent with the service-failure-severity finding above where exactly this scenario class (style-matching during serious failure) was shown empirically to backfire.

### Gaps
- No paper found directly studies an AI agent refusing to casualize specifically *because* the task is high-stakes while the user's own tone is casual (the Boli loan-default example) — the service-failure-severity finding is the closest direct evidence, but it is about failure severity perceived by the user, not explicitly about user-tone/task-stakes mismatch as this project frames it. This remains a reasonable extrapolation rather than a directly confirmed finding.
- Could not retrieve full text of the FAccT "Not Like Us, Hunty" paper or the dialect-feature-database study; the strength and generalizability of their specific quantitative/qualitative claims beyond the search-snippet level is unverified.

## What does task-oriented NLG naturalness research say about generating natural-sounding (vs. robotic/corporate) system responses, independent of style-adaptation specifically?

### Takeaway
Naturalness is treated in the literature as a distinct, measurable quality dimension of task-oriented NLG output (separate from task-success/slot-accuracy metrics), with automatic BERT-based naturalness classifiers outperforming older baselines (SVM, BiLSTM, BLEURT), and with adversarial-training approaches specifically developed to push generated dialogue away from template-like/robotic phrasing toward human-like fluency — naturalness is flagged as a persistent, unsolved challenge especially in complex/ambiguous scenarios.

### Cited Findings
- A dedicated naturalness-evaluation paper proposes **automatic naturalness evaluation as its own task** (distinct from task-success evaluation) for task-oriented dialogue NLG, using **fine-tuned BERT models**, and reports this approach **outperforms baselines including SVMs, bi-directional LSTMs, and BLEURT** — [Naturalness Evaluation of NLG in Task-Oriented Dialogues Using BERT, RANLP 2021 / arXiv:2109.02938](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.02938).
- A separate paper proposes **adversarial training specifically to boost naturalness** of task-oriented dialogue language, framing the problem explicitly as moving generated output away from stiff/templated phrasing toward more human-like responses — [Boosting Naturalness of Language in Task-oriented Dialogues via Adversarial Training, arXiv:2004.14565](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.14565).
- Broader NLG-challenges literature frames naturalness as a core, unresolved difficulty: "one of the primary challenges in NLG is ensuring that generated text is natural-sounding and coherent," noting that **NLG systems often struggle to produce human-like fluency and coherence especially in complex or ambiguous scenarios** — (ResearchGate NLG algorithms/applications overview, synthesized via search).
- Task-oriented dialogue is explicitly framed in some work as fundamentally an NLG problem (the "NLG module produces the user-facing utterance" and is "critical" to be natural and fluent) rather than merely a slot-filling/backend problem — [Task-Oriented Dialogue System as NLG, arXiv:2108.13679](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.13679).

### Inferences
- Naturalness and style-adaptation are related but separable research threads: naturalness research is largely about avoiding template/robotic artifacts at the surface-fluency level (grammaticality, variety, human-likeness of phrasing), while style-adaptation research (sections above) is about *which* register/tone to target. An explicit style policy (formality/brevity/warmth/directness/confirmation-rigor) sets the *target*, while naturalness techniques (adversarial training, naturalness classifiers as a training/eval signal) would be the mechanism for making *any* chosen point on that style spectrum sound human rather than templated — these are complementary, not competing, design layers.
