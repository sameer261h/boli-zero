## Does human-like voice/conversational cues increase social presence, and does that straightforwardly increase trust?

### Takeaway
Research establishes fairly consistently that anthropomorphic/human-like design cues (voice humanness, interactivity, name, tone) increase perceived social presence, and that social presence is *correlated* with trust — but multiple sources explicitly flag this relationship as non-linear, bidirectional-risk-laden, and dependent on dialogue length, attitudes, and task type, not a simple "more human-like → more trust" dial.

### Cited Findings
- Perceived anthropomorphism influences the formation of social presence, which in turn affects trust; anthropomorphism was found to enhance both *functional* trust (belief in reliable task performance) and *relational* trust (emotional/psychological connection) — per search snippet of Haziraj (2025) TU Wien thesis, "Influence of Anthropomorphism in AI-enabled Voice Assistants on User Trust" — [Source](https://repositum.tuwien.at/bitstream/20.500.12708/216266/1/Haziraj%20Edona%20-%202025%20-%20Influence%20of%20Anthropomorphism%20in%20AI%20Voice%20Assistants%20on...pdf)
- "Humanised voice, interactive communication capability and cognitive features evoke a sense of social presence that **may positively or negatively impact user trust**" (note the explicit hedge toward non-uniform effect) — per search snippet of "Anthropomorphism and social presence in Human–Virtual service assistant interactions: The role of dialog length and attitudes," ScienceDirect — [Source](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563222001650)
- The same ScienceDirect study's title itself foregrounds that the anthropomorphism→social-presence→trust link is moderated by **dialog length and user attitudes**, i.e., it is not a fixed, context-free effect — [Source](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563222001650)
- Voice pitch, tone, and the assistant's name significantly shape perceived trustworthiness, warmth, and relational closeness; a warm/empathetic tone was particularly effective in building trust in sensitive/personal-topic scenarios (implying effectiveness is topic/stakes-dependent, not universal) — per search snippet, source unspecified within the search summary (likely one of the anthropomorphism/VSA papers returned) — [Source](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14413582231181140)
- When anthropomorphic features are embedded in conversational AI, users show a tendency toward trust/attachment that can lead to **inadvertent privacy compromise and emotional overreliance** — a documented downside risk, not merely a benefit — per search snippet, "All Too Human? Mapping and Mitigating the Risks from Anthropomorphic AI" (AAAI/AIES) — [Source](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/download/31613/33780/35677)
- Relationship development with voice assistants (a social-in-nature process) correlates with increased trust and anthropomorphism over time — per search snippet, "Exploring Interactions Between Trust, Anthropomorphism, and Relationship Development in Voice Assistants" (arXiv) — [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.01923)

### Inferences
- The literature converges on: anthropomorphism/social presence and trust are correlated and mechanistically linked, but the relationship is explicitly described in multiple sources as moderated (by dialogue length, user attitude, topic sensitivity, task stakes) rather than monotonic — i.e., "more human-like" does not reliably mean "more trusted," and can create overreliance/privacy risk. This directly supports the project's instinct to avoid treating "more human-sounding" as an unambiguous good.
- This body of work is largely about *general* anthropomorphism (voice warmth, naming, persona), not specifically about the identity-disclosure question — it's adjacent context/justification, not a direct answer to "should the AI say it's an AI."

### Gaps
- I did not find a study directly quantifying whether disclosing AI identity (vs. not) *causally reduces* perceived social presence or trust in a controlled experiment within the voice-AI-specific literature (the general "chatbot disclosure" literature below addresses an adjacent but not identical question). This is a meaningful remaining gap for the project's specific question about *how* to disclose.

## Is there a named, recent (2024–2026) "disclosure by design" framework, and what does it say about disclosure reliability under adversarial/role-play conditions?

### Takeaway
No single, clearly-branded "disclosure by design" framework specific to voice AI turned up as a dominant, widely-cited term of art in this search pass. The most directly relevant and strongest empirical evidence on *disclosure reliability under adversarial conditions* is **RealityTest** (arXiv 2606.00168, also covered by UK AISI), a large-scale multimodal/multilingual study that is squarely on-point for the project's "does disclosure become unreliable under pressure" question. A separately-named "DAISY" framework exists in the literature but is about AI-assisted-writing disclosure in academic publishing, not voice-agent identity disclosure — flagged explicitly so it isn't misapplied.

### Cited Findings
- **RealityTest** is described as "the first large-scale multimodal and multilingual evaluation that comprehensively tests whether AI systems disclose their identity when asked, grounded in human data on how people actually encounter and question AI identity in the real world" — per search snippet — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00168); also covered by the UK AI Safety Institute — [Source](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/research/realitytest-how-people-probe-ai-identity-and-whether-models-disclose-it)
- Only **31% of people ask about identity directly** in ambiguous scenarios; the questions people actually ask are far more diverse than machine-generated/synthetic benchmark queries — per search snippet of RealityTest — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00168)
- Testing 17 text models and 6 speech models, disclosure probability varied enormously: **8%–92% for text models, 10%–57% for speech models** — per search snippet of RealityTest — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00168)
- **A single system-prompt "suppression instruction" reduces disclosure rates to below 30%, even in the best-performing models** — directly answering the project's question about whether disclosure becomes unreliable when something (e.g., a role-play framing or an instruction) pushes against it — per search snippet of RealityTest — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00168)
- "How the question is phrased and the context of the conversation matter more for disclosure than which model is being tested" — i.e., disclosure reliability is a property of conversational framing/context as much as of model choice, supporting the "disclosure needs to be designed as a robust behavior, not just a capability" framing — per search snippet of RealityTest — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00168)
- A related, separately-indexed arXiv paper, **"AI Identity Disclosure Under Professional Personas: A Gap Between Capacity and Consistency"** (arXiv 2511.21569), appears directly relevant to role-play/persona-adversarial disclosure reliability by title, though I was not able to retrieve its findings via search snippet in this pass (full text was not fetched due to the arxiv.org block) — [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21569v10)
- A second adjacent paper, **"The Artificial Self: Characterising the landscape of AI identity"** (arXiv 2603.11353), also surfaced as relevant to the AI-identity-disclosure space but was not retrievable in detail this pass — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.11353)
- The **Identity Design (ID) Framework** — "a layered framework for building coherent, interpretable identities in artificial agents," with 12 identity design principles across three levels of interaction — includes a concrete pattern called **disclosure-based prompting**: e.g., an agent asked "How are you feeling?" responding "Although I have no emotions, my systems are functioning as intended," explicitly framed as preserving sociability while clarifying identity — this is close in spirit to the project's "honest but natural, not corporate boilerplate" goal — per search snippet of "Designing Artificial Identity: The Identity Design Framework and Research Agenda" — [Source](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3800645.3812962)
- **DAISY** (a separate, differently-scoped framework) operationalizes AI disclosure as a "form-based, author-facing interaction" for disclosing AI assistance in academic writing (not voice-agent identity disclosure); it frames disclosure as "a sociotechnical practice rather than merely a compliance statement," and found that structured disclosure UI improved completeness of disclosure statements without reducing author comfort — relevant as a *general design-philosophy analog* ("disclosure as designed sociotechnical practice, not boilerplate") but **not a match for voice-AI identity disclosure** — flagging explicitly so this isn't miscited as the right paper — per search snippet — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.02760)
- Separately, regulatory "disclosure by design" pressure is concrete and dated: EU AI Act Article 50 requires the agent to disclose it is AI, in the caller's language, "at the start of the interaction or in a way the caller can reasonably register before any consequential exchange"; California's AB 2905 (effective Jan 1, 2025) requires verbal disclosure at the opening of automated calls; proposed FCC rules would formally define "AI-generated call" and require real-time disclosure — per search snippet — [Source](https://www.topcalls.ai/blog/ai-disclosure-laws); [Source](https://www.regal.ai/blog/ai-agent-ethics-disclosure-timing)

### Inferences
- RealityTest is the strongest, most directly on-point empirical answer to the project's specific question: disclosure is *not* a reliable, guaranteed behavior as currently implemented across models — it degrades sharply (to below 30% in top models) under a single suppression/role-play-adjacent instruction, and depends more on conversational framing than on raw model capability. This is strong support for the project's "disclosure must be a designed-in, adversarially-robust behavioral property" thesis — it is not already solved, and the paper itself frames it as an open problem ("the gap between baseline performance and real-world robustness is large").
- No evidence surfaced of one dominant, widely-adopted "disclosure by design" *named framework* specific to voice AI in the 2024–2026 window; the space seems to still be assembling (RealityTest as an eval, the ID Framework as a design-principles framework, DAISY as a disclosure-UI framework in an adjacent domain, plus a scatter of regulatory "disclosure by design" requirements). The project could reasonably present itself as filling a real gap here rather than restating settled prior art — but should not claim "disclosure by design" as if it originated the term, since regulatory and design-framework literature already uses close variants of that concept.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve full findings of "AI Identity Disclosure Under Professional Personas" (2511.21569) or "The Artificial Self" (2603.11353) due to the arxiv.org egress block — these titles strongly suggest they are directly on-point for the role-play/persona-adversarial disclosure question and should be prioritized for full retrieval in a follow-up pass (e.g., via Google cache, a non-arxiv mirror, or a different fetch tool/proxy route) if available to the report writer.
- Could not confirm whether RealityTest's speech-model disclosure testing included voice-specific adversarial conditions (e.g., spoken role-play setup, "pretend you're human for this call") as a distinct experimental condition versus text-only suppression instructions — the snippet describes an aggregate 10%-57% disclosure rate for speech models but I don't have the breakdown by adversarial-condition type.

## Is trust in voice assistants general, or highly task-specific — and does human-likeness increase trust uniformly?

### Takeaway
Research explicitly rejects a "general trust" model in favor of task-specific, stakes-dependent trust: users calibrate trust per task/function, not per agent as a whole, and measured trust scores vary dramatically by task stakes (e.g., music playback vs. financial transfer).

### Cited Findings
- "Trust is calibrated per task, not per agent, and varies systematically across tasks. Users commonly restricted trust in voice-based assistants to particular tasks and functions" — per search snippet of "A Mixed-Methods Approach to Understanding User Trust after Voice Assistant Failures" (CHI, arXiv 2303.00164) — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.00164)
- Quantified stakes-dependence: average trust score was **3.29/5 for playing music**, dropping to **2.34/5 for texting a coworker**, and **1.56/5 for transferring money** — per search snippet, same study — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.00164)
- Users often discontinue using a voice assistant for a *specific task* after a failure in that task, then resume only after trust is rebuilt — frequently by first using the assistant for simpler tasks or the same task in a lower-stakes context — per search snippet, same study — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.00164)
- Theoretical grounding: "humans do not trust in general; rather, trust is always directional — A could trust B with regard to task X, and C with regard to task Y. High functional specificity means that a person's trust reflects the specific capabilities of the AI's subfunctions and modes" — per search snippet (theoretical/review framing, not a single empirical study) — [Source](https://www.ischool.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/sproject_attachments/humanai_capstonereport-final.pdf)

### Inferences
- This directly supports a key design implication for the project: a more human-like voice/interaction style should not be assumed to raise trust uniformly across all tasks; it may raise comfort/engagement for low-stakes, social tasks while having little or even negative effect on trust in high-stakes tasks (financial, personal-data, safety-relevant). This is a clean, well-evidenced empirical basis for arguing register/trust calibration should vary with task seriousness — which maps directly onto the project's stated design goal (disclosure register appropriate to task seriousness).

### Gaps
- Did not find a study that crosses the anthropomorphism/human-likeness manipulation *and* the task-stakes manipulation in the same experiment (i.e., directly testing "does a more human voice help trust more in low-stakes tasks than high-stakes ones") — the task-specificity finding and the anthropomorphism-trust finding come from separate literatures that the project would need to synthesize as an inference rather than cite as a single joint finding.

## What empirical research exists on why users tease, abuse, confuse, probe, or make absurd requests of conversational/voice agents?

### Takeaway
This is a genuinely empirically-studied area, mainly in CHI/HCI and computational-linguistics/dialogue-systems venues (not just anecdote), with identified motivations spanning frustration/system-breakdown-driven aggression, disinhibition effects of computer-mediated communication, intentional system-limit-testing/curiosity, and distinct playful/humor-seeking behavior documented as its own category — plus a smaller, more anecdotal/survey-based literature on gendered abuse patterns.

### Cited Findings
- Prevalence estimates vary widely across studies: one line of research found **up to 50% of human-machine exchanges were verbally abusive**; another put the range at **10%–50% of interactions** being abusive — per search snippet (exact source of the "50%" figure not individually disambiguated in the synthesized summary; treat as approximate/variable across study methodologies) — [Source](https://zenodo.org/records/18111839)
- In a study of real chatbot conversations, **4.29% of messages directed at the chatbot contained profanity**, and notably **80% of conversations contained profanity** rather than it coming from a small number of exceptionally abusive users — i.e., mild abusive language is broadly distributed across the user base, not concentrated in a hostile minority — per search snippet of Bartneck & Keijsers, "What's to bullying a bot? Correlates between chatbot humanlikeness and abuse" — [Source](https://www.bartneck.de/publications/2021/WhatsToBullyingABot/bartneckKeijsersInteractionStudies2021.pdf)
- A study of 283 Cleverbot conversations found **higher counts of verbal aggression and sexual comments when the chatbot appeared more humanlike in its behavior** — but the authors explicitly caution that no experimental manipulation was conducted, so **causality cannot be inferred** (correlational only) — per search snippet, same study — [Source](https://www.bartneck.de/publications/2021/WhatsToBullyingABot/bartneckKeijsersInteractionStudies2021.pdf)
- "The less mind participants attributed to the robot, the more aggressive responses they gave" — from the related "Mindless Robots get Bullied" HRI study — per search snippet — [Source](http://www.bartneck.de/publications/2018/mindless-robot-gets-bullied/keijsersBartneckHRI2018.pdf)
- Frustration/system-error mechanism: "When the conversational agent lacks the ability to understand the user's input correctly and errors occur, the resulting dissatisfaction can lead to aggressive user behavior... frustration drives aggression, with users with high impulsivity tending to severely insult a conversational agent when it makes an error" — per search snippet — [Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12116430/) (and related sources in that search cluster)
- "Abusive language often arises in response to dialogic breakdowns rather than as isolated or context-free acts. Triggers include incorrect or irrelevant system responses, repeated refusal to comply with user requests, and perceived circular or evasive dialogue" — per search snippet — [Source](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3313831.3376461) (Empathy Is All You Need cluster)
- Disinhibition mechanism: "Verbal interaction with conversational agents provides an ideal environment for aggressive and sexual disinhibition, as computer-mediated communication reduces social pressure" — per search snippet — [Source](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0144929X.2025.2533352)
- A **2.5-year longitudinal family-home study ("Alexa, shut up!", Behaviour & Information Technology, 2026)** with 128 parents completing six surveys over 2.5 years found: parents rated themselves as more polite to the voice assistant than their children were; and **perceived relationship closeness between children and the VA positively predicted children's commanding tone, politeness, and verbal-abuse tendencies toward the VA over time**, whereas for parents, closeness predicted only politeness (not abuse) — a genuinely longitudinal, empirical (not anecdotal) finding — per search snippet — [Source](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0144929X.2025.2533352)
- Gendered pattern: users direct more sexual/profane comments toward female-presenting chatbots than male-presenting ones, attributed partly to the default feminine personas of popular assistants — per search snippet — [Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12116430/)
- Distinct from abuse: a dedicated empirical taxonomy of **playful (non-hostile) requests** exists. "Alexa, what do you do for fun?" / "Alexa, do you want to build a snowman?" (Amazon Science / arXiv 2105.05571) analyzed real-world Alexa traffic and built "a taxonomy of playful requests rooted in theories of humor," covering absurd requests like "Order a zillion gummy bears" — directly analogous to the project's drive-through example — per search snippet — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05571); Amazon Science version — [Source](https://www.amazon.science/publications/alexa-do-you-want-to-build-a-snowman-characterizing-playful-requests-to-conversational-agents)
- "Personification is one of the most frequent types of playfulness exhibited by users" with Alexa; a Wizard-of-Oz study found that **enabling the assistant to recognize humor/playful opportunities increased user satisfaction** — per search snippet, same paper cluster — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05571)
- Survey-based research (cited within that cluster) found users engage in playful requests "for fun or to avoid boredom," including asking for jokes, taking quizzes, reading poems, playing social games — explicitly naming boredom/entertainment-seeking as a distinct, non-hostile motivation category, separate from frustration-driven aggression — per search snippet — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05571)
- A newer annotation-scheme study, **"When Users Turn Hostile: Rude, Aggressive, and Abusive Interactions with AI Chatbots"** (Dec 2025), built a 1,200-utterance/180-conversation hybrid corpus and found hostile behavior **escalates along coherent interactional trajectories tied to system breakdowns** (incorrect responses, refusals, repetitive output): rudeness signals early dissatisfaction, aggression introduces confrontational blame/dominance cues, and abuse shifts toward expressive degradation/dehumanization; inter-annotator reliability was substantial (Krippendorff's α = 0.72), with the rude–aggressive boundary being the most ambiguous — per search snippet — [Source](https://zenodo.org/records/18111839)

### Inferences
- The literature clearly distinguishes at least three separable motivational clusters with different empirical support: (1) frustration/breakdown-driven aggression (well-studied, mechanistically linked to specific system failures — wrong answers, refusals, circularity), (2) disinhibition-driven casual abuse/profanity broadly distributed across most users rather than a hostile minority (supported by the 80%-of-conversations-contain-profanity finding), and (3) genuinely playful/intrinsically-motivated humor-seeking, which is a *separate, non-hostile* category with its own taxonomy and literature (the Alexa playful-requests line of work). This three-way split is a defensible, evidence-backed basis for the project's instinct not to collapse all adversarial-sounding behavior into "distress requiring sympathy."
- The "When Users Turn Hostile" escalation-trajectory finding (rudeness → aggression → abuse, tied to system breakdowns) is a strong evidentiary basis for a graduated/severity-sensitive response policy, since it shows these are empirically distinguishable stages rather than one undifferentiated phenomenon.
- The absurd/excessive-order drive-through example in the project brief maps closely onto the "Order a zillion gummy bears" example documented in the playful-requests literature — this is a studied phenomenon, not a novel observation, so the project should cite rather than claim to have discovered this category of behavior.

### Gaps
- The quantitative prevalence figures (50%, 10-50%, 4.29%/80%) come from different studies with likely different definitions of "abusive"/"profane" and different corpora (crowd-sourced vs. real deployment vs. text chatbot vs. voice) — I could not fully verify or reconcile these different base rates against each other; the report writer should treat these as illustrative rather than as a single consistent statistic.
- I found no research specifically and directly studying the "ordering excessive items at a drive-through to mess with the AI" scenario as its own documented category (e.g., in QSR/fast-food voice-ordering AI literature specifically) — the closest documented analog is the general "absurd/playful request" taxonomy from the Alexa research, not a drive-through-specific study. This should be flagged as an inference-by-analogy, not a direct citation match.
- Could not find rigorous experimental (as opposed to survey/corpus-correlational) research isolating *boredom* as a causal driver of adversarial (as opposed to merely playful) behavior specifically — the boredom-avoidance motivation evidence is about playful/fun-seeking requests, not about hostile/adversarial-probing requests, which the project's key questions treat as potentially a different population of users.

## What does research find about effective response strategies to abusive/adversarial input, and is there caution against assuming hostility = distress?

### Takeaway
A consistent, multi-study empirical line (mostly CHI/dialogue-systems venues, using controlled or crowd-sourced evaluation, not anecdote) finds that **empathetic and polite-refusal responses outperform both avoidance/ignoring and counterattack/confrontation strategies**, that the optimal strategy is **adaptive to abuse severity and type** (not one-size-fits-all), and that the field explicitly recognizes a distinction between genuinely distressed/frustrated hostility and playful/provocative "insults," cautioning against a single sympathetic-response default for all hostile-sounding input.

### Cited Findings
- Comparing three response styles (Avoidance, Empathy, Counterattacking) across three abuse types (Insult, Threat, Swearing): **"Empathetic response styles helped mitigate aggressive user reactions, compared to avoidance or counterattack strategies. Participants were less angry and more guilty with the empathetic agent than the other two agents"** — per search snippet, "Empathy Is All You Need: How a Conversational Agent Should Respond to Verbal Abuse" (CHI 2020) — [Source](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3313831.3376461)
- "An empathetic response from the conversational agent was preferable over an avoidant approach (no-response/ignore), which may be the most frequently-used strategy by commercial CAs currently" — per search snippet, "Should an Agent Be Ignoring It? A Study of Verbal Abuse Types and Conversational Agents' Response Styles" (CHI 2019) — [Source](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3290607.3312826)
- Design recommendation from the same research cluster: "When users verbally abuse an agent, it is necessary for the agent to ask users about the real intention of their statements, and if users express negative feelings with the intent of abuse, the chatbot should ask users what features make them upset and show a willingness to solve the problem" — i.e., clarifying intent rather than assuming distress or assuming hostility by default — per search snippet — [Source](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3290607.3312826)
- Graduated/severity-sensitive design recommendation: "gentle clarification or acknowledgment in response to rudeness, explicit boundary-setting accompanied by explanation during aggression, and firm refusal combined with safety-oriented messaging in cases of abuse" — per search snippet, likely from the "When Users Turn Hostile" paper's design implications — [Source](https://zenodo.org/records/18111839)
- Large crowd-sourced evaluation: **"polite refusal" scored highly across the board**, and users "consistently prefer polite refusal, followed by no answer"; **"Avoidance is most appropriate after sexual demands"** specifically (i.e., context-dependent, not a single best strategy); **participants had significantly higher negative reactions to the counterattacking agent** than to other response styles; demographic factors (e.g., age) and abuse severity shifted which response users judged appropriate — per search snippet, "A Crowd-based Evaluation of Abuse Response Strategies in Conversational Agents" (arXiv 1909.04387 / ACL Anthology W19-5942) — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04387)
- Explicit caution against collapsing all hostile-sounding input into one category: "People may display playful intentions when they insult and provoke AI agents. Such 'insults' could be playful or could be an innocent way for people to release tension, like other forms of cursing and swearing" — and separately, "playful utterances are often answered by 'I don't know what you mean,' or interpreted literally by the virtual assistant, leading to poor results" — showing the literature treats mistaking playful provocation for genuine hostility (and vice versa) as a real design failure mode, not just a theoretical concern — per search snippet — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05571) and search-synthesized framing around it
- Historical industry course-correction: in 2017, voice assistants' default responses to overt verbal/sexual harassment were "playfully evasive or positive"; public backlash (petition with ~17,000 signatures per the snippet) specifically targeted the flirtatious/submissive responses to open sexual harassment, prompting companies to make responses more assertive/corrective — this is a documented industry response-strategy shift, though sourced from secondary/voicebot-industry coverage rather than peer-reviewed research — per search snippet — [Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12116430/)
- A dedicated academic paper directly engages the normative question of whether AI assistants *should* function as behavior-correcting/moralizing agents toward abusive users: "Can chatbots teach us how to behave? Examining assumptions about user interactions with AI assistants and their social implications" frames this as an open, debated question rather than a settled best practice, and notes "the extent and nature of the problem are unclear due to a lack of data on user interactions" — directly relevant to the project's stated wish to avoid reflexive moralizing — per search snippet — [Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12116430/)

### Inferences
- The empirical base is reasonably strong and convergent across at least four independent studies/clusters (Ashktorab et al./"Should an Agent Be Ignoring It?"; "Empathy Is All You Need"; the crowd-based evaluation paper; and the newer "When Users Turn Hostile" corpus study) that: empathetic/polite-refusal responses beat both silent avoidance and confrontational counterattack, and that the right response is a function of abuse *type* and *severity*, not a single fixed script. This gives the project solid empirical grounding for a graduated response policy.
- On the specific question of "don't assume all hostile behavior = distress," the clearest support is indirect: the playful-requests literature (Alexa "do you want to build a snowman") establishes that a substantial, well-documented category of provocative/absurd/teasing user behavior is intrinsically-motivated play, not distress or genuine hostility, and that misreading it (treating it as a literal request or a confused/distressed one) is itself a documented failure mode. Combined with the "When Users Turn Hostile" severity-staging finding (rudeness is early/low-stakes, often not yet "abuse"), there is a reasonable evidence-based case that a flat sympathetic/concerned response to all hostile-register input risks being miscalibrated for playful provocation. However, I did not find a study that directly and explicitly tests "sympathetic/concerned response vs. unbothered/task-focused response to playful provocation specifically" as its own experimental comparison — this specific comparison (which is what the project most wants) appears to be an inference the project would be drawing by combining two adjacent literatures (abuse-response-strategy research + playful-request research), not a single study that already answers it.

### Gaps
- No study found that directly experimentally compares "treat hostile-sounding input as playful/stay task-focused and unbothered" against "treat it as possible distress/respond with concern" as two named conditions. This is a genuine open question/novel contribution opportunity for the project, not an already-answered one — it should be flagged to the report writer as such.
- The abuse-response-strategy literature (empathy/polite-refusal/avoidance/counterattack) is predominantly *text-chatbot* research (CHI/HCI chatbot studies); I found comparatively little that is voice-specific and controlled (the "Alexa, shut up!" study is voice-specific and longitudinal but is about relationship-closeness predictors of abuse, not about comparing agent *response* strategies). The project should be cautious about assuming text-chatbot response-strategy findings transfer directly to spoken, real-time voice interaction, where prosody, turn-taking, and the social stakes of tone of voice likely differ.
- Could not retrieve full text of "How AI Assistants Respond to Repeated Abuse" (arXiv 2609.17547) due to the arxiv.org block — this title suggests it may bear directly on escalation/graduated-response dynamics and should be prioritized for retrieval if a working fetch route becomes available.

## What should this project NOT claim as novel? (Established vs. open ground)

### Takeaway
Both halves of the project's motivating questions sit on genuinely well-established empirical ground in several respects — the project should present itself as synthesizing/applying established findings to a *voice-specific, task-seriousness-calibrated* design problem, not as discovering these phenomena. The most defensible claims to novelty are narrower: (a) adversarial-robustness of disclosure specifically (RealityTest-type findings are recent, 2024-2026, and the field explicitly calls this unresolved), and (b) the specific combination of "disclosure register calibrated to task seriousness" + "distinguishing playful provocation from distress in a voice-specific, real-time context," which does not appear to have a single existing study directly testing it.

### Cited Findings
- Well-established, NOT novel: that human-like design cues raise social presence and (generally) trust is a long-standing, multiply-replicated finding across the anthropomorphism/voice-assistant literature (multiple studies, several pre-2024) — per the cluster of sources above — [Source](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14413582231181140); [Source](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563222001650)
- Well-established, NOT novel: that abusive/aggressive user behavior toward conversational agents is common, partly frustration/breakdown-driven, partly disinhibition-driven, and broadly distributed across users rather than concentrated in a hostile minority — documented since at least 2018-2021 (Bartneck & Keijsers lineage) — [Source](https://www.bartneck.de/publications/2021/WhatsToBullyingABot/bartneckKeijsersInteractionStudies2021.pdf)
- Well-established, NOT novel: that empathetic/polite-refusal responses to abuse outperform avoidance and counterattack, and that response strategy should vary with abuse type/severity — established by at least four convergent studies from 2019-2025 (CHI 2019, CHI 2020, the crowd-based evaluation paper, and the Dec 2025 hostility-corpus paper) — [Source](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3290607.3312826); [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04387)
- Well-established, NOT novel: that playful/absurd requests ("order a zillion gummy bears") are a distinct, studied, non-hostile category of user behavior with its own taxonomy, going back to at least 2021 (the Alexa playful-requests paper) — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05571)
- Well-established, NOT novel: that industry voice-assistant makers have already publicly moved away from flirtatious/submissive responses to harassment toward more assertive/corrective responses, following the 2017-2019 MeToo-era controversy — this is documented history, not a new design insight the project would be originating — [Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12116430/)
- Genuinely more open/recent (2024-2026), reasonable novelty ground: the specific finding that AI identity disclosure is *unreliable under adversarial/suppressive conditions* (RealityTest's <30%-disclosure-under-suppression-instruction finding) is recent and the paper itself frames real-world adversarial robustness of disclosure as an unresolved gap in current evaluation practice — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00168)
- Genuinely more open, reasonable novelty ground: I found no study directly testing "stay unbothered/task-focused vs. respond with sympathy" as competing response strategies specifically for *playful* (as opposed to hostile) provocation — this experimental gap is explicitly noted above and appears to be real, not an artifact of incomplete search.

### Inferences
- The project should frame its contribution as: (1) applying the now-fairly-mature abuse-response-strategy literature and the newer disclosure-reliability literature specifically to the voice-AI, task-seriousness-calibrated setting, and (2) filling a specific, narrower, and genuinely less-studied gap — distinguishing playful/adversarial-but-non-distressed behavior from genuine distress in real-time voice interaction and prescribing a response strategy for the former that avoids both corporate-boilerplate disclosure and reflexive sympathetic moralizing. Neither "disclose your AI identity" nor "respond calmly to rude users" is novel in the abstract; the novelty is in the synthesis and in voice-specific adversarial robustness.

### Gaps
- I could not assess how much of this literature has already been operationalized inside major commercial voice-assistant systems' actual (non-public) response policies, since vendor internal design documentation is not publicly searchable; the "industry/applied research on voice assistant abuse handling" suggested source category in the brief mostly surfaced regulatory-compliance blogs (Regal.ai, Retell AI, Thoughtly, TopCalls.ai) rather than primary vendor research publications — these are compliance/sales-oriented secondary sources, not rigorous empirical research, and should be weighted accordingly (useful for regulatory context, not for behavioral-science claims).
