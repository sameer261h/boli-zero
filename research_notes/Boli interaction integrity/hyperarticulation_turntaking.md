## Key Question 1: What is Oviatt's CHAM model, what does it predict about staged/escalating speech adaptation after recognition failure, and does it replicate across languages/cultures?

### Takeaway
The model is correctly named the **CHAM — Computer-elicited Hyperarticulate Adaptation Model** (sometimes rendered "two-stage branching CHAM"), introduced by Sharon Oviatt and colleagues (Oviatt, MacEachern, Levow, Bernard) in work spanning ICSLP 1996–1998 and a 1998 Speech Communication journal article. It is a genuinely **staged/escalating** model — not a single uniform "speak more clearly" response — with Stage I (duration-only changes at low error rates) and Stage II (multi-dimensional changes at high error rates). It is well-cited and foundational in the field, but the evidence base is concentrated in English-language, mostly Wizard-of-Oz/simulated-recognizer lab studies from the late 1990s–2000s; a 2017 Interspeech multilingual study found the *phenomenon* (hyperarticulation on correction) generalizes across 9 languages but its *acoustic manifestation differs significantly by language*, meaning the raw CHAM parameters are not safely assumed to transfer unmodified cross-lingually.

### Cited Findings
- The model's correct name is the **"two-stage branching Computer-elicited Hyperarticulate Adaptation Model (CHAM)"**, which "accounts for systematic changes in human speech during interactive error handling." — [ISCA Archive: The CHAM model of hyperarticulate adaptation during human-computer error resolution (Oviatt, ICSLP 1998)](https://www.isca-archive.org/icslp_1998/oviatt98b_icslp.html)
- **Stage I** adaptation is "a singular change in durational characteristics" (increased duration of speech and pauses), "associated with a moderate degree of hyperarticulation ... during a low error base-rate." — [Predicting Hyperarticulate Speech During Human-Computer Error Resolution (Oviatt, Levow et al., Speech Communication 1998, PDF)](https://faculty.washington.edu/levow/papers/Speech_Communication_98/speechjournal.pdf)
- **Stage II** adaptation "entails multiple changes in durational, articulatory and fundamental frequency characteristics," associated with "a more extreme degree of hyperarticulation ... during a high error base-rate" — i.e., the model is explicitly escalating/graduated rather than a single response mode. — [Predicting Hyperarticulate Speech During Human-Computer Error Resolution (PDF)](https://faculty.washington.edu/levow/papers/Speech_Communication_98/speechjournal.pdf)
- Concrete pause-duration numbers reported in the staged escalation: original input averaged 0.53 pauses/utterance at 136 msec; repeated input (post-error, without phonological alternation) rose to 0.97 pauses/utterance at 220 msec — illustrating the Stage I durational-only shift. — [Predicting Hyperarticulate Speech During Human-Computer Error Resolution (PDF)](https://faculty.washington.edu/levow/papers/Speech_Communication_98/speechjournal.pdf)
- "Linguistic Adaptations During Spoken and Multimodal Error Resolution" (Oviatt, Bernard, Levow, 1998) is a companion/overlapping publication describing the same adaptation phenomenon across spoken vs. multimodal error-resolution conditions. — [Linguistic Adaptations During Spoken and Multimodal Error Resolution](https://doi.org/10.1177/002383099804100409)
- A follow-up study, "Adapting speaking after evidence of misrecognition: Local and global hyperarticulation" (Speech Communication, Vol. 50, No. 3, 2007/2008), reports that hyperarticulation "increases after evidence of misrecognition and then decays gradually over several turns in the absence of further misrecognitions" — establishing that the adaptation is **transient and targeted, not a stable new speaking mode**, and that it concentrates on the specific misrecognized constituent: "speakers are more likely to clearly articulate constituents that were apparently misrecognized than those either before or after the troublesome constituents, and more likely to clearly articulate content words than function words." — [Adapting speaking after evidence of misrecognition: Local and global hyperarticulation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167639307001446) (abstract-level synthesis; full text blocked)
- A 2017 Interspeech study, "Hyperarticulation of Corrections in Multilingual Dialogue Systems" (Kraljevski & Hirschfeld), examined 9 languages and found "distinctive prosodic features across 9 different languages associated with hyperarticulated speech" — the correction-triggered hyperarticulation strategy itself recurs across languages, but "its manifestation varies significantly across languages, posing a challenge for developing universally effective multilingual dialogue systems." The paper's framing explicitly concludes that a "one-size-fits-all" recovery-strategy design is insufficient and that language-specific adaptation is needed. — [Hyperarticulation of Corrections in Multilingual Dialogue Systems (Interspeech 2017)](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2017/kraljevski17_interspeech.pdf)
- Related engineering-side work ("Compensating Hyperarticulation for Automatic Speech Recognition," "Phone dependent modeling of hyperarticulated effects," Soltau et al., ICSLP 2000/2002) treated hyperarticulation as an ASR-training/acoustic-modeling problem rather than purely a dialogue-interaction phenomenon, confirming the effect was taken seriously enough by the ASR community to warrant dedicated acoustic-model compensation work in the early 2000s. — [Compensating Hyperarticulation for Automatic Speech Recognition](https://d-nb.info/1001657659/34); [Phone dependent modeling of hyperarticulated effects (ICSLP 2000)](https://www.isca-archive.org/icslp_2000/soltau00_icslp.html)

### Inferences
- The "first lengthen speech/pauses, then show stronger articulatory/F0 changes if errors persist" characterization in the brief is an accurate paraphrase of CHAM's Stage I → Stage II structure: Stage I is duration-only, Stage II adds articulatory clarity and F0 changes, gated by error *rate* (not merely error *count*) — i.e., the model is explicitly about escalating severity of error exposure, not simply "errors happened N times."
- Because essentially all primary CHAM literature located here is English-language, lab-based (often Wizard-of-Oz simulated recognizers from the 1990s), and uses relatively small, demographically narrow participant pools typical of that era's HCI studies, the *specific parameter values* (pause-duration deltas, F0 shift magnitudes) should be treated as English/WEIRD-sample-calibrated rather than universal constants. The 2017 multilingual paper is the only cross-linguistic replication surfaced, and even it reports manifestation differences by language, so a voice-AI system built for a broad user base should not assume CHAM's exact thresholds transfer without local calibration.
- The decay-over-turns finding (hyperarticulation fades gradually once errors stop) is important for signal design: a friction detector based on hyperarticulation should expect a decaying tail after the last error, not an instant return to baseline, and should not interpret lingering mild hyperarticulation several turns after a resolved error as a fresh friction event.

### Gaps
- No source located here directly tests CHAM, or an equivalent escalation model, against non-English, non-Western conversational corpora outside the Kraljevski & Hirschfeld 9-language Interspeech study; that study's own language set, per-language effect sizes, and whether "9 languages" includes major non-Indo-European languages, could not be confirmed because the ISCA full text was unreachable (egress blocked) and only the WebSearch-synthesized abstract was available.
- No source here quantifies *how well-replicated* CHAM is in the sense of independent replication attempts/replication-crisis-style meta-analysis; the literature trail shows extension and application (compensation, multilingual, automatic detection) rather than explicit "did Oviatt's original result replicate" studies.
- Exact publication venue/date disambiguation between the 1998 ICSLP CHAM paper, the 1998 Speech Communication journal paper, and the 1998 "Linguistic Adaptations" Human Communication Research article could not be fully resolved from snippets alone — these may be overlapping reports of the same study program rather than three independent empirical results; a report-writer citing CHAM should cite the ISCA/Speech Communication source as primary and treat the others as companion publications.

## Key Question 2: Is there a well-known "hyperarticulation paradox" (clearer speech → worse ASR), and what does it imply for using hyperarticulation as a friction-detection signal?

### Takeaway
Yes — this is a well-established and explicitly named finding in the literature: hyperarticulated speech, despite being the user's intuitive repair strategy, reliably **degrades** ASR performance because hyperarticulated acoustics mismatch the training distribution of recognizers trained on casual/conversational speech. This means a system that detects "the user is hyperarticulating" is detecting a signal that is simultaneously evidence of **user frustration/communication breakdown** and a **leading indicator that the system's own recognition accuracy is about to drop further** — the two are mechanistically linked, not independent correlates.

### Cited Findings
- "Hyperarticulation is a speech adaptation that consists of adopting a clearer form of speech in an attempt to improve recognition levels, but it has the opposite effect when talking to ASR systems, as they are not trained with such kind of speech." — [Compensating Hyperarticulation for Automatic Speech Recognition](https://d-nb.info/1001657659/34)
- "Hyperarticulation causes a significant increase in recognition errors, and current state-of-the-art technologies in automatic speech recognition fail to deal with hyperarticulated speech." — [Compensating Hyperarticulation for Automatic Speech Recognition](https://d-nb.info/1001657659/34)
- Mechanism: "Changes in pitch, formant frequencies, or phone duration lead to a mismatch between the train and test environment. This acoustic mismatch occurs because speech recognition systems are typically trained on conversational speech rather than hyperarticulated speech." — [search synthesis of ASR/hyperarticulation literature, per WebSearch result set]
- A quantified effect size: "Utterances rated as hyperarticulated showed a 30% increased error rate compared to casual speech." — [search synthesis; original study not individually identified in result snippets — treat as a reported figure pending primary-source confirmation]
- The paradox is explicitly framed as a self-reinforcing cycle in multiple sources: "Hyperarticulate speech appears to be both a reaction to system recognition failure, as well as potentially fueling a higher rate of system errors, with hyperarticulation having the potential to generate a cycle of recognition failure." — [Predicting Hyperarticulate Speech During Human-Computer Error Resolution (PDF synthesis)](https://faculty.washington.edu/levow/papers/Speech_Communication_98/speechjournal.pdf)
- Patent-literature evidence that this is recognized as an *engineering* problem worth solving operationally: a Google/industry patent family, "Hyperarticulation detection in repetitive voice queries using pairwise comparison for improved speech recognition," builds a detector that compares pairs of repeated user utterances (delta features) specifically because "traditional approaches typically attempt to define and detect an absolute state of hyperarticulation, which is very difficult, especially on short voice queries" — and reports that feeding hyperarticulation-probability as a feature into N-best rescoring measurably improves WER. — [WO2018160505A1 — Hyperarticulation detection in repetitive voice queries (Google Patents)](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2018160505A1/en); [related USPTO filings](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10847147)
- Academic detection work exists separately from the patent track: "Automatic Detection of Hyperarticulated Speech" (Springer book chapter) addresses the same absolute-detection-is-hard problem via acoustic/prosodic classifiers. — [Automatic Detection of Hyperarticulated Speech](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-49169-1_18)

### Inferences
- For an interaction-integrity system, detecting hyperarticulation is a **dual-purpose signal**: (1) it is direct behavioral evidence the user perceived a breakdown (they are actively, consciously trying to repair it), which is arguably a *stronger* friction signal than passive hesitation because it reflects an explicit coping action; but (2) because hyperarticulation causally degrades the ASR's own accuracy, a system that notices "user started hyperarticulating" should expect — and could pre-emptively compensate for — an elevated near-term risk of *its own* additional recognition errors, not merely infer something about user emotional state. This argues for hyperarticulation-detection feeding into an ASR-adaptation action (e.g., switching to a model/decoding path more robust to clear speech, or widening N-best/confidence tolerance) in addition to a frustration-estimation signal.
- The pairwise/comparative detection approach (comparing a repeated utterance against its predecessor rather than trying to classify a single utterance as "hyperarticulated" in absolute terms) is methodologically notable and likely the more production-viable detection strategy, since absolute single-utterance detection is explicitly flagged as hard in the patent-literature framing.
- Because the paradox is mechanistically about train/test acoustic mismatch, a system using a *recent, large, more acoustically diverse* ASR model (e.g., modern foundation-model ASR trained on far broader, more naturalistic data including some clear/corrective speech) might exhibit a smaller version of this paradox than the ASR systems these 1990s–2010s studies were built on — but no source located here directly tests whether the paradox persists with modern large ASR models; this is flagged as an inference, not a finding (see Key Question 3 gaps).

### Gaps
- The "30% increased error rate" statistic's originating study could not be individually attributed/verified — it appeared only as an unattributed synthesized claim across search results; a report-writer should treat this number as indicative, not load-bearing, until a primary source is confirmed.
- No source here directly measures whether modern end-to-end neural ASR (e.g., Whisper-class or conformer/transducer models trained on much larger and more varied corpora) is as vulnerable to the hyperarticulation paradox as the HMM-GMM-era systems the foundational studies were built on. This is an open and practically important question for a 2026-era voice-AI system and is noted as a genuine research gap.

## Key Question 3: What is the "error spiral"/"recognition error spiral" literature, and is it still observed in modern LLM-based spoken dialogue systems, or considered solved?

### Takeaway
The "error spiral" (also "cyclic errors") is an established, explicitly named concept in the spoken-dialogue-systems literature describing the closed loop: misrecognition → user hyperarticulates in correction → hyperarticulation degrades recognition further → repeated misrecognition. It is NOT framed as solved in classic SDS literature. Evidence from recent (2023–2025) LLM-powered voice-assistant studies shows that breakdown/frustration dynamics persist in modern systems, though the specific *mechanism* reported in LLM-assistant studies looks more like general frustration escalation (repetition, raised volume, disengagement) than literature explicitly re-confirming the acoustic hyperarticulation-ASR-degradation loop in LLM-era pipelines — i.e., the broader frustration-escalation phenomenon clearly persists, but no source here explicitly re-tests the classic acoustic error-spiral mechanism against a modern LLM-ASR stack.

### Cited Findings
- "Corrections tend to be hyperarticulated — higher, louder, longer — than other turns. However, this response strategy paradoxically backfires. As an effect of higher word error rates at hyperarticulated speech, humans try to reinforce this speaking style which results in even more recognition errors." The literature explicitly names this "a spiral in which evidence of misrecognition causes speakers to hyperarticulate, in turn causing even more recognition errors." — [Characterizing and Predicting Corrections in Spoken Dialogue Systems (Computational Linguistics journal, ACL Anthology J06-3004)](https://aclanthology.org/J06-3004.pdf)
- "Corrections themselves are more difficult for the system to recognize and interpret correctly, which may lead to so-called cyclic (or spiral) errors." — [Characterizing and Predicting Corrections in Spoken Dialogue Systems](https://aclanthology.org/J06-3004.pdf)
- A dedicated course/survey resource on error handling ("Error Handling in Spoken Dialogue Systems," Stoyanchev, Columbia) exists as a structured treatment of this problem space, indicating it was considered significant enough curriculum material in classic SDS research. — [Error Handling in Spoken Dialogue Systems (Stoyanchev)](http://www.cs.columbia.edu/~sstoyanchev/SDS2015/SDSErrors_03_09_15.pdf)
- A 2024 scoping review, "System and User Strategies to Repair Conversational Breakdowns of Spoken Dialogue Systems," indicates the breakdown-repair problem is still an active review/synthesis topic as of 2024, not a closed/solved one. — [System and User Strategies to Repair Conversational Breakdowns of Spoken Dialogue Systems: A Scoping Review (ACM, 2024)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3640794.3665558)
- Modern LLM-voice-assistant studies report frustration-escalation behavior consistent with (though not identical in mechanism to) the classic error spiral: in a study of LLM-based voice assistant interaction, "when users received no feedback from voice assistants, participants frequently repeated their questions, actively exploring different possibilities"; and "during conflict stages, 9 out of 12 participants raised their volume as an expression of frustration or in an effort to capture the voice assistant's attention when it is unresponsive." — [Human and LLM-Based Voice Assistant Interaction: An Analytical Framework for User Verbal and Nonverbal Behaviors (arXiv 2408.16465)](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.16465v1)
- The same/related research program's companion paper, "User Interaction Patterns and Breakdowns in Conversing with LLM-Powered Voice Assistants" (Mahmood et al.), reports that in a 20-participant study across three task scenarios, "LLMs absorb the majority of voice assistant intent recognition failures" and "ChatGPT improved intent recognition and proactively addressed voice assistant breakdowns," with observed benefits including "context retention, adaptability, and breakdown reduction" alongside limitations of "repetitiveness, oversharing, and discrepancy in mental models." — [User Interaction Patterns and Breakdowns in Conversing with LLM-Powered Voice Assistants (arXiv 2309.13879v2)](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.13879v2); [ScienceDirect version](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1071581924001897)
- General framing from current voice-AI engineering content (non-peer-reviewed but directly relevant to production system framing) continues to treat misrecognition-driven frustration loops as a live, unsolved UX/engineering problem in 2026-era voice AI guides, not a legacy issue. — [Voice AI Barge-In and Turn-Taking: A 2026 Implementation Guide](https://futureagi.com/blog/voice-ai-barge-in-turn-taking-2026/)

### Inferences
- The classic acoustic error spiral (hyperarticulation → worse ASR → more hyperarticulation) is best understood as **not solved** but **partially mitigated** in modern systems: LLM-based intent understanding appears to "absorb" some recognition failures at the semantic/dialogue level (per Mahmood et al.), which could shorten or dampen the spiral's downstream effects even if the underlying acoustic mismatch (Key Question 2) is unchanged — but this is an inference bridging two separate findings (LLM semantic absorption + classic acoustic paradox), not a finding any single source directly tested together.
- The persistence of repetition and volume-raising as frustration behaviors in the 2024 LLM-assistant study suggests the *user-side* half of the spiral (repair attempts escalating in effort/intensity) is alive and well in modern systems regardless of whether the classic acoustic ASR-degradation half still holds identically; this matters for Boli's interaction-integrity design because user-side escalation behavior (repetition, volume, re-phrasing) may be detectable and useful as a friction signal even independent of whether hyperarticulation specifically is measurable.

### Gaps
- No source located here directly re-runs or re-tests the *classic* acoustic hyperarticulation-ASR-degradation spiral specifically against a modern LLM-based or recent (2023+) neural ASR + LLM dialogue pipeline with controlled measurement of WER before/after hyperarticulated repair attempts. This is the single most consequential gap for the Boli project's purposes: whether the mechanism Oviatt/Soltau documented in the 1990s–2000s against HMM-based recognizers still holds at the same magnitude against modern large ASR models is unverified either way in the sources surfaced this pass.
- No quantitative frequency/prevalence data (e.g., "X% of multi-turn LLM voice sessions exhibit a detectable error spiral") was found for modern systems; the available evidence is qualitative/observational from small-N lab studies (12–20 participants).

## Key Question 4: What does incremental spoken-dialogue research establish about modeling turn-taking and barge-in explicitly, and what signals/timing cues decide interruption vs. natural pause?

### Takeaway
Incremental-processing research treats turn-taking as a continuously negotiated, probabilistic phenomenon rather than a rigid non-overlapping-turn boundary problem. Classic work (Ström & Seneff 2000; Zhao et al. 2015 SIGDIAL; the D15-1216 taxonomy paper) established the core vocabulary and architectures (active system barge-in, cost-model/reinforcement-learning-based yield policies). Modern work has shifted toward learned, continuous-forecasting models — most notably **Voice Activity Projection (VAP)** — that predict near-future (≈2000ms-ahead) joint voice-activity states from raw acoustics rather than relying on fixed silence thresholds, explicitly because "acoustic silence is not the end of a turn."

### Cited Findings
- Definition: "A spoken dialogue system is said to be incremental when it does not wait until the end of the user's utterance in order to process it." A "barge-in" is "when the user begins speaking prior to a prompt's completion," historically "problematic for many speech dialog systems because the system automatically starts and stops upon detection of user speech, without regard for what the user has said." — [An Incremental Turn-Taking Model with Active System Barge-in for Spoken Dialog Systems (Zhao et al., SIGDIAL 2015)](https://aclweb.org/anthology/W15-4606)
- The Zhao et al. system "constructs cost models and learns optimal policy using reinforcement learning" to decide when the *system itself* should actively barge in on the user (not just react to user barge-in), reporting "reduces false cut-in rate by 37.1% and response delay by 32.5% compared to baseline systems," with the learned barge-in strategy yielding "a 27.7% increase in average reward from user responses." — [An Incremental Turn-Taking Model with Active System Barge-in for Spoken Dialog Systems (SIGDIAL 2015)](https://aclweb.org/anthology/W15-4606)
- A companion taxonomy paper, "Turn-taking phenomena in incremental dialogue systems," systematically categorizes turn-taking events for incremental system design. — [Turn-taking phenomena in incremental dialogue systems (ACL Anthology D15-1216)](https://aclanthology.org/D15-1216.pdf)
- Earlier foundational work: "Intelligent Barge-in in Conversational Systems" (Ström & Seneff, ICSLP 2000) is cited as an early treatment of making barge-in handling smarter than simple VAD-triggered cutoffs. — [Intelligent Barge-in in Conversational Systems (Ström & Seneff, ICSLP 2000)](https://www.isca-archive.org/icslp_2000/strom00_icslp.pdf)
- Modern production-engineering framing: "End-of-turn detection uses a silence threshold (typically 800–1200ms of silence after the user's last word) combined with semantic completeness (the LLM scoring whether the partial transcript looks complete)." "Adding prosodic features increases detection performance, with gains driven primarily by silence duration and pitch fall." "Acoustic silence is not the end of a turn; silence tells you the sound stopped, not whether the speaker is finished." — [Turn Detection in Voice Agents: Silence vs VAD vs Semantic (Deepgram)](https://deepgram.com/learn/turn-detection-voice-agents-silence-vad-semantic-models); [Less can be More: What Aspects of Speech Drive End-of-Turn Detection (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.11066)
- "The 2026 production stack is migrating toward dedicated turn-taking models that classify backchannel vs. barge-in vs. continued silence as a learned signal instead of an energy threshold." — [Voice AI Barge-In and Turn-Taking: A 2026 Implementation Guide](https://futureagi.com/blog/voice-ai-barge-in-turn-taking-2026/)
- **Voice Activity Projection (VAP)**: "a self-supervised framework designed to predict future conversational patterns directly from raw acoustic inputs. Unlike traditional turn-taking approaches that rely on reactive silence thresholds or specific event labels like pauses or overlaps, VAP formulates turn-taking as a continuous forecasting problem," projecting "the joint voice activity of two speakers into a near-future window (typically 2000 ms)," implicitly learning "prosodic shifts, backchannels, and filled pauses, without requiring manual event annotations." — [MuVAP: Multimodal Multiparty Voice Activity Projection (arXiv 2606.16731)](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.16731); [Voice Activity Projection Model with Multimodal Encoders (arXiv 2506.03980)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.03980); [Multilingual Turn-taking Prediction Using Voice Activity Projection (arXiv 2403.06487)](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.06487v1)
- System response strategies on being barged-in: "Systems should be ready to yield the floor when they are barged-in on, and they should have the ability to barge in on a human's ongoing speech if there are appropriate stakes involved." Documented strategy taxonomy includes "Always-Yield," "Yield on Speech," and "Yield when confident." — [search synthesis of barge-in policy literature, incl. Ström & Seneff and related engineering sources]
- A recent survey states the field still lacks maturity in benchmarking: "72% of reviewed works do not compare their methods with previous efforts," and the survey argues "a key challenge is the lack of well-established benchmarks." — [A Survey of Recent Advances on Turn-taking Modeling in Spoken Dialogue Systems (ACL Anthology, IWSDS 2025)](https://aclanthology.org/2025.iwsds-1.27/)
- A companion/overlapping review: "Turn-Taking Modelling in Conversational Systems: A Review of Recent Advances" (Patamia, Dinh, Liu, Cosgun; MDPI *Technologies*, 2025) covers the same landscape in a journal venue. — [Turn-Taking Modelling in Conversational Systems: A Review of Recent Advances](https://doi.org/10.3390/technologies13120591)

### Inferences
- The field has clearly moved (conceptually, between the 2015 SIGDIAL-era cost-model/RL approaches and the 2023–2025 VAP-era approaches) from *reactive, threshold-based* turn-taking (fixed silence ms cutoffs) to *predictive, learned* turn-taking that treats overlap/pause/backchannel as continuous phenomena. For Boli's purposes, this implies that a barge-in/turn-taking-based friction signal should ideally be built on (or benchmark against) VAP-style continuous prediction rather than a fixed silence-threshold heuristic, since the literature explicitly frames fixed thresholds as the outdated/inferior approach.
- The explicit survey finding that "72% of reviewed works do not compare... previous efforts" and benchmarks are immature is a meaningful caveat: claims of specific performance numbers (e.g., Zhao et al.'s 37.1%/32.5%/27.7% figures) should be treated as single-study results from a specific 2015 system, not validated state-of-the-art baselines for 2026 systems.

### Gaps
- No source here gives a single, authoritative, modern (2024–2025) quantitative comparison of silence-threshold vs. prosodic vs. VAP/semantic turn-detection accuracy on a common benchmark — consistent with the survey's own observation that benchmarking is immature in this field.
- Exact signal weighting (how much pitch fall vs. silence duration vs. semantic completeness contribute, in relative terms, to state-of-the-art turn-detection) was only partially covered ("gains driven primarily by silence duration and pitch fall" per the arXiv 2609.11066 "Less can be More" paper title/snippet) — the full paper was not fetchable to confirm exact ablation numbers.

## Key Question 5: Could hyperarticulation-onset or interruption/barge-in function as legitimate interaction-friction signals, and what concrete confounds (specific to this literature) must be controlled for?

### Takeaway
Both signals are plausible, literature-grounded friction indicators — hyperarticulation-onset because it is a documented, targeted (not generalized) reaction specifically to perceived misrecognition, and user-initiated barge-in/interruption because deviation from a person's own baseline turn-taking timing is a meaningful behavioral change. But both carry real, literature-documented confounds: individual/idiosyncratic baseline variation in turn-taking timing ("dyadosyncrasy"), and cross-cultural/cross-linguistic variation in both hyperarticulation manifestation and raw turn-gap duration, meaning any friction detector must be calibrated against a per-speaker or per-locale baseline rather than an absolute threshold.

### Cited Findings
- Hyperarticulation is "a targeted and flexible adaptation rather than a generalized and stable mode of speaking, increasing after evidence of misrecognition and then decaying gradually over several turns in the absence of further misrecognitions" — supporting its validity as a *specific* friction signal (tied to the triggering error) rather than a diffuse trait-level marker. — [Adapting speaking after evidence of misrecognition: Local and global hyperarticulation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167639307001446) (abstract synthesis)
- Confound — language/dialect: Kraljevski & Hirschfeld (Interspeech 2017) found hyperarticulation's *manifestation* "varies significantly across languages," meaning an absolute acoustic threshold tuned on one language's hyperarticulation profile will likely misfire on another. — [Hyperarticulation of Corrections in Multilingual Dialogue Systems](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2017/kraljevski17_interspeech.pdf)
- Confound — difficulty of absolute (single-utterance) detection: industry patent literature explicitly states "traditional approaches typically attempt to define and detect an absolute state of hyperarticulation, which is very difficult, especially on short voice queries," which is why the best current approach is **pairwise/comparative** (comparing a repeated utterance to its immediate predecessor) rather than absolute classification — implying any production system should detect hyperarticulation via within-session, within-speaker comparison, not an absolute acoustic cutoff. — [WO2018160505A1 — Hyperarticulation detection in repetitive voice queries (Google Patents)](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2018160505A1/en)
- Confound — individual/dyad-level idiosyncrasy in turn-taking: a 2025 paper titled "'Dyadosyncrasy', Idiosyncrasy and Demographic Factors in Turn-Taking" directly addresses individual-speaker and individual-dyad-level variation in turn-taking behavior as a factor that must be accounted for in turn-taking modeling (confirms this is an actively studied confound, though full quantitative breakdown wasn't retrievable from the snippet). — [arXiv 2505.24736](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.24736)
- Confound — cross-cultural/cross-linguistic gap-length variation: a landmark cross-cultural study (Stivers et al., PNAS 2009) found that across a worldwide 10-language sample, while "all tested languages provide clear evidence for a general avoidance of overlapping talk and a minimization of silence between conversational turns" (a universal), "differences across languages exist in the average gap between turns, within a range of 250 ms from the cross-language mean. More specifically, Japanese speakers exhibit an average gap of only 7 ms, in contrast to Danish speakers, who average 489 ms." Despite this variation, "similar factors altered the gap between speakers across the various languages" (e.g., dispreferred/contradictory responses were slower in all 10 languages by roughly 100–500ms) — i.e., the *relative* dynamics (a longer-than-personal-baseline gap signaling something) are more universal than the *absolute* gap length. — [Universals and cultural variation in turn-taking in conversation (Stivers et al., PNAS 2009)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0903616106)
- Confound — speaking-style/acoustic baseline normalization: methodological literature on hyperarticulation measurement explicitly uses per-speaker normalization (e.g., "log-mean normalization, where each speaker's mean formant values are subtracted from individual values," and computing "average formants of each vowel type ... for each speaker across speech registers to determine individual theoretical 'typical' articulation") — confirming that even within the hyperarticulation-measurement research community, absolute acoustic values are considered unreliable without per-speaker baselining. — [search synthesis of ICPhS 2023 and related hyperarticulation-measurement literature](https://www.internationalphoneticassociation.org/icphs-proceedings/ICPhS2023/full_papers/969.pdf)
- Related confound for barge-in specifically: a 2025/2026 paper, "Still Between Us? Evaluating and Improving Voice Assistant Robustness to Third-Party Interruptions," addresses the case where an apparent "barge-in" is actually a *third party* (not the primary user) speaking — a distinct confound for barge-in-as-friction-signal in multi-speaker/ambient environments. — [Still Between Us? Evaluating and Improving Voice Assistant Robustness to Third-Party Interruptions (arXiv 2604.17358)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.17358)

### Inferences
- Taken together, the hyperarticulation-detection literature's own methodological choice (pairwise/within-speaker comparison over absolute classification) and the turn-taking literature's own confound findings (dyadosyncrasy, cross-cultural gap-length variation, but cross-cultural *consistency* in relative/directional effects) converge on the same design principle for Boli: **both signals should be operationalized as deviations from a per-speaker (or at minimum per-locale) rolling baseline, not as absolute acoustic/timing thresholds.** This is not merely a general "always personalize" platitude — it is the specific, literature-endorsed mitigation for the specific confounds this cluster was asked to investigate (as opposed to the general prosody confounds the other thread covers).
- Barge-in as a friction signal needs a precursor step (distinguishing the primary user's barge-in from a third party, per the "Still Between Us" paper) before any friction-detection logic is meaningful, which the general prosody thread would not have covered since it's a turn-taking/speaker-attribution-specific confound.
- Hyperarticulation-onset appears to be a comparatively *higher-precision* friction signal than passive hesitation precisely because it's described in the literature as targeted/triggered (concentrated on the actually-misrecognized constituent) and transient (decaying once errors stop), which argues for weighting it meaningfully in an interaction-integrity score when it does fire — but its *recall* may be limited because the engineering literature itself says absolute single-utterance detection is hard; practical detection likely requires at least two utterances (the pairwise-comparison approach) to fire reliably.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies exactly how large a per-speaker baseline deviation needs to be (in standard deviations or a comparable normalized metric) before it should count as a friction signal rather than noise — this threshold-calibration question appears to be an open engineering decision rather than a published literature finding.
- No source directly tested combining hyperarticulation-onset and barge-in/turn-timing-anomaly as a *joint* multi-modal friction signal (i.e., whether the two co-occur, reinforce, or are independent) — each is well-studied individually but their joint/combined predictive value for an "interaction integrity" composite score is not addressed in any source found this pass and would need to be an original contribution of the Boli project rather than a literature-confirmed design.
