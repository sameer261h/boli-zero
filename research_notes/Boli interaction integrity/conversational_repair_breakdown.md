## What is Third Position Repair (TPR) in Conversation Analysis? Who established it, and how has it been operationalized computationally?

### Takeaway
Third Position Repair is a well-established, classic Conversation Analysis (CA) construct (Schegloff, 1992) describing the sequential slot — A says X, B responds based on a misreading of X, A then repairs B's understanding in the third turn — that CA treats as "the last structurally provided defense of intersubjectivity in conversation." It has been computationally operationalized multiple times in the last three years (2023–2024), via purpose-built datasets (REPAIR-QA) and benchmarks (BlockWorld-Repairs), making it one of the more "solved-enough-to-benchmark" areas of this literature, though models still underperform humans on it.

### Cited Findings
- A third-position repair is "a self-initiated self-repair that is initiated by the producer of the trouble source after the recipient's talk that reveals that the producer of the trouble source has been misunderstood." The minimal model: first position = A's turn (instruction/proposal/assertion); second position = B's response displaying some understanding of A's turn; third position = A's opportunity to confirm, revise, or correct after seeing B's displayed understanding. — [emcawiki: Third position repair](https://emcawiki.net/Third_position_repair); [emcawiki: Third position](https://emcawiki.net/Third_position)
- Third position repairs exhibit a highly recurrent four-part format: a repair initiation (typically "no"), an agreement/acceptance (e.g., "I know"), a rejection (often "I don't mean X"), and the repair proper (often "I mean" + a contrast, reformulation, or specification). — [emcawiki: Third position repair](https://emcawiki.net/Third_position_repair)
- Schegloff (1992) characterized third-position repair as "the last structurally provided defense of intersubjectivity in conversation" — the last structurally "cheap" opportunity for A to correct B's misunderstanding before downstream turns build on the wrong premise. — [Schegloff, "Repair after Next Turn" (PDF)](https://people.brandeis.edu/~smalamud/ling140/3-6-schegloff-SharoneHorowitHandler.pdf)
- TPRs address two main kinds of trouble: problematic references and problematic sequential implicativeness. — [emcawiki: Third position repair](https://emcawiki.net/Third_position_repair)
- Computational operationalization #1 — REPAIR-QA (SIGDIAL 2023, Balaraman, Eshghi, Konstas, Papaioannou, Heriot-Watt/alanaai): the first large dataset of TPRs built specifically for a conversational question-answering setting, used to fine-tune T5 and to probe GPT-3. — [ACL Anthology: No that's not what I meant](https://aclanthology.org/2023.sigdial-1.52/); [arXiv:2307.16689](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.16689); [GitHub: alanaai/Repair-QA](https://github.com/alanaai/Repair-QA)
- Computational operationalization #2 — BlockWorld-Repairs (EMNLP 2024, Heriot-Watt): extends the classic "Block World" instruction-following manipulation benchmark with brief dialogues in which humans produce multimodal TPRs, specifically because the original Block World dataset "did not account for dialogue or clarificational exchanges as strategies to overcome environmental ambiguity, which humans commonly use to repair miscommunications." — [ACL Anthology: Repairs in a Block World](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.643/); [arXiv:2409.14247](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14247)
- On BlockWorld-Repairs, "all models significantly underperform" humans at processing and responding to TPRs, though VLMs benefit from specialized losses targeting relevant tokens during fine-tuning, improving performance and generalizability. — [Liner review of Repairs in a Block World](https://liner.com/review/repairs-in-block-world-new-benchmark-for-handling-user-corrections); [ACL Anthology: Repairs in a Block World](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.643/)
- Other-initiated repair (the broader CA category that TPR sits within) has an established cross-linguistic typology: Dingemanse & Enfield (2014/2015, *Studies in Language* / *Open Linguistics*) analyzed ten languages and found a cross-linguistically viable three-way taxonomy of repair-initiator types, built from functional (linguistic-formatting) and sequential (interactional-environment) properties. — [Dingemanse & Enfield, "Other-initiated repair across languages"](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.2478/opli-2014-0007/html?lang=en); [Radboud repository PDF](https://repository.ubn.ru.nl/bitstream/handle/2066/204973/204973.pdf?sequence=1)

### Inferences
- TPR is best understood as the *narrowest, most structurally crisp* slice of conversational repair — it has a template-like linguistic signature ("no, I mean...") that makes it unusually tractable to operationalize as a supervised NLP task, which likely explains why it (rather than broader "other-initiated repair") has become the preferred unit for recent LLM/VLM benchmarks.
- The existence of two independent TPR benchmarks in different modalities (text QA via REPAIR-QA; embodied/multimodal instruction-following via BlockWorld-Repairs) within about a year of each other (2023–2024) suggests TPR-handling is an active, converging benchmark target in the dialogue-systems community, not a one-off paper.

## What is REPAIR-QA? What did it find about how well current strong LLMs handle TPR out of the box, and what repair-specific training/data improved it?

### Takeaway
REPAIR-QA (SIGDIAL 2023, "No that's not what I meant: Handling Third Position Repair in Conversational Question Answering," Balaraman, Eshghi, Konstas, Papaioannou) is the first large TPR dataset built for conversational QA; GPT-3 performed poorly out-of-the-box on TPR handling, and performance improved significantly once the models were exposed to/trained on REPAIR-QA data.

### Cited Findings
- REPAIR-QA is described as "the first large dataset of Third Position Repairs (TPRs) in a conversational question answering setting." — [arXiv:2307.16689 abstract via WebSearch](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.16689)
- The authors performed both automatic and human evaluations on a fine-tuned T5 model and on OpenAI's GPT-3 LLMs. — [ACL Anthology: 2023.sigdial-1.52](https://aclanthology.org/2023.sigdial-1.52/)
- Finding: "GPT-3 models showed poor out-of-the-box performance on TPR's, which then significantly improved when exposed to Repair-QA." — [WebSearch synthesis of ACL Anthology / arXiv abstract](https://aclanthology.org/2023.sigdial-1.52/)
- Code/data released at the alanaai GitHub organization (Heriot-Watt-affiliated). — [GitHub: alanaai/Repair-QA](https://github.com/alanaai/Repair-QA)

### Inferences
- The pattern ("strong general-purpose LLM fails on a repair-specific phenomenon out of the box, but a small amount of task-specific exposure/fine-tuning closes much of the gap") recurs across this whole literature (see also BlockWorld-Repairs and "Talking to a Know-It-All GPT" below) — it suggests TPR/repair handling is not an emergent capability of scale alone, and needs explicit data or architectural attention even in 2023–2026-era models.

## What is Contextual Rephrase Detection ("ContReph")? What signals beyond pairwise similarity does it use, and how much better is it than naive similarity-based rephrase detection?

### Takeaway
ContReph (EMNLP 2021, Amazon Alexa team, "Contextual Rephrase Detection for Reducing Friction in Dialogue Systems") reframes rephrase detection as a dialogue-context problem rather than a pairwise-utterance-similarity problem, adding dialogue history, users' implicit feedback, and inter-turn timing gaps as signals, and the paper reports it "significantly outperforms" pairwise rephrase-detection baselines — though the exact outperformance margin (F1/AUC numbers) could not be retrieved due to the proxy block on both the ACL Anthology and Amazon Science pages.

### Cited Findings
- Problem framing: "Rephrase detection has long been treated as a task with pairwise input, which does not fully utilize the contextual information (e.g. users' implicit feedback)." Users tend to rephrase their query until they get a satisfactory response from voice assistants (Alexa, Google Assistant, Siri), and friction arises from errors in different system components or from user-side slips. — [Amazon Science: Contextual rephrase detection for reducing friction in dialogue system](https://www.amazon.science/publications/contextual-rephrase-detection-for-reducing-friction-in-dialogue-system); [ACL Anthology: 2021.emnlp-main.143](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.143/)
- Mechanism: ContReph "leverages the dialogue context and user-agent interaction signals, including user's implicit feedback and the time gap between different turns," and "detects if any of the user queries in the dialogue session is rephrased, and then extracts the most probable rephrase span that led to a satisfactory response from the dialogue agent." — [Amazon Science publication page via WebSearch](https://www.amazon.science/publications/contextual-rephrase-detection-for-reducing-friction-in-dialogue-system)
- Result (qualitative only, from search snippet, not independently verified against a numeric table): using these contextual signals "can help significantly outperform the pairwise rephrase detection models." — [Amazon Science / ACL Anthology via WebSearch](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.143/)
- A related, more recent industry reference point: Google's own "Contextual Rephrasing in Google Assistant" research blog post exists, indicating this is a cross-industry (Amazon + Google) problem area, not unique to one company's assistant. — [Google Research blog: Contextual Rephrasing in Google Assistant](https://research.google/blog/contextual-rephrasing-in-google-assistant/)
- A 2024/2025-era industry follow-on exists at Rasa ("Smaller LLMs, Smarter Costs: Context Rephrasing"), suggesting the problem has since been revisited with small/efficient LLMs rather than the original feature-engineered contextual model. — [Rasa blog: Smaller LLMs, Smarter Costs: Context Rephrasing](https://rasa.com/blog/contextual-rephrasing-with-small-llms)

### Inferences
- The explicit design choice to add "implicit feedback" and "inter-turn timing gap" as first-class signals (rather than only text similarity) is directly relevant to an interaction-integrity layer: it is empirical precedent, from a shipped production system (Alexa), that *non-linguistic, interaction-level* signals (timing, downstream user behavior) materially improve detection of system-induced trouble — this is a reusable design pattern, not something to re-derive from scratch.
- That both Amazon and Google have independently published on "contextual rephrase/rephrasing detection" suggests this specific sub-problem (detecting *that* a user is rephrasing because of system failure, as opposed to detecting *what kind* of breakdown occurred) is comparatively mature/solved in industry, at least for the voice-assistant single-turn-command use case.

### Gaps
- Exact performance numbers (F1, AUC, precision/recall deltas vs. pairwise baseline) could not be retrieved — both the ACL Anthology abstract/PDF page and the Amazon Science page returned EGRESS_BLOCKED on WebFetch, and no third-party source quoted the numeric results table in search snippets. This is the single largest unresolved quantitative gap in this research question.
- Could not determine whether ContReph's "implicit feedback" signal specifically means things like barge-in, repeat-after-silence, or explicit negative utterances ("no," "stop") — the search snippets described it only generically.

## What does research on "user repetition as correction" find (circa 2021)? Does it verify the "19% relative WER reduction at 2% false-alarm rate" figure?

### Takeaway
**Confirmed.** The commonly-cited "19% relative WER reduction at 2% false-alarm rate" figure traces to Apple's 2021 Interspeech paper "User-Initiated Repetition-Based Recovery in Multi-Utterance Dialogue Systems" (Nguyen et al.), which built an end-to-end two-step attention pointer network that merges a user's repeated/partial correction back into the original misrecognized query.

### Cited Findings
- Paper: "User-Initiated Repetition-Based Recovery in Multi-Utterance Dialogue Systems," Apple, presented at Interspeech 2021 (arXiv:2108.01208). Problem statement: unlike human-human communication, where people recover from misrecognized words/phrases by repeating them, this recovery mechanism was not available in human-machine communication prior to this work. — [Apple Machine Learning Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/user-initiated-repetition-based-recovery); [ISCA Archive PDF (Interspeech 2021)](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2021/nguyen21_interspeech.pdf); [arXiv:2108.01208](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.01208)
- Mechanism: when a user repeats part of a phrase, the system rewrites the original query to incorporate the correction via an "end-to-end 2-step attention pointer network" that merges the incorrectly-understood first utterance with the correction follow-up utterance. The two-step process first attends to the first utterance to identify which parts of the original query to preserve, then attends to the second (correction) utterance to identify replacement words, with a final layer deciding whether to pull a word from turn 1, turn 2, or terminate the sequence. — [WebSearch synthesis of arXiv/Apple ML Research](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.01208)
- **Headline figure, verified:** "The proposed model outperforms the rule based baseline, reducing Word Error Rate by 19% relative at 2% False Alarm Rate on annotated data." — [arXiv:2108.01208 / ISCA Interspeech 2021 / Apple ML Research, via WebSearch](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2021/nguyen21_interspeech.pdf)
- A second, related number from the same body of results: the "2-Step Attention model achieved a maximum WERR (Word Error Rate Reduction) of 25.80%," "significantly outperforming a standard pointer network which achieved a maximum WERR of only 19.03%." Note this 19.03% figure is a *different* comparison (2-step attention vs. standard pointer network's own max WERR) from the "19% relative WER reduction at 2% FAR vs. rule-based baseline" headline figure — the two 19%-ish numbers appear to be two distinct results from the same paper, not the same statistic restated, and should not be conflated. — [WebSearch synthesis, source as above]

### Inferences
- The "19% at 2% FAR" framing (reporting WER reduction at a *fixed, low false-alarm-rate operating point*) implies the paper explicitly tuned for a conservative deployment regime — i.e., the system was evaluated for how much it can safely recover without introducing many bad "corrections" into cases where the user wasn't actually correcting anything. This operating-point framing (precision-constrained recall) is a pattern worth reusing for any new repair-detection mechanism: report performance at a deployment-realistic false-alarm budget, not just best-case numbers.

### Gaps
- Could not read the full paper (arXiv and ISCA archive both blocked) to confirm the exact annotated test-set size, the definition of "annotated data," or whether the 19%-at-2%-FAR number is for the full 2-step model or an ablation. The two similar-looking "19%" statistics above (19% relative WERR at 2% FAR vs. rule baseline; and 19.03% max WERR for standard pointer network vs. 2-step's 25.80%) could not be fully disambiguated from search snippets alone — flagging this explicitly rather than guessing which is "the" commonly-cited figure (most likely it is the first, the rule-baseline comparison, since that matches the commonly-cited phrasing exactly).
- Did not find task-success-rate (as opposed to WER) numbers for this system.

Repairing Intelligent Voice Assistant Errors Improves Interaction" was also surfaced by search but not investigated further; it looks like a user-study/HCI angle on self-repair, complementary to but distinct from the ASR-level merging approach — flagged as a pointer for other clusters in this research thread, not pursued here as it falls outside "user repetition as correction" per se.

## What dialogue-breakdown-repair corpora exist covering different kinds of system-induced breakdown and expected repair? What do 2024–2026 papers say about how well systems handle the full range of breakdown types — is this still open, and where specifically do systems fail?

### Takeaway
Several purpose-built breakdown-type corpora and detection models now exist (Japan-centric breakdown-repair corpora with 10-type taxonomies; MultConDB for industrial conversational-AI breakdown detection; OIR-detection work with prosody), and a 2024 scoping review plus multiple 2024–2026 papers confirm this remains a genuinely open problem — current systems still struggle particularly with knowing *how* to repair once a breakdown type is identified, not just detecting that one occurred.

### Cited Findings
- **Corpus — Japanese Dialogue Breakdown Repair Corpus (LREC 2026):** targets ten breakdown types: "Wrong information," "Ignore question," "Ignore expectation," "Unclear intention," "Topic transition error," "Lack of information," "Self-contradiction," "Contradiction (with user utterance)," "Repetition," and a "Lack of [relevant response]" category; built from personality-questionnaire data and 3,990 dialogue repair examples collected from 57 participants; used to train an LLM specialized in generating repair utterances per breakdown type, reported to achieve higher accuracy than existing models. — [ACL Anthology: A Corpus for Personalized Dialogue Breakdown Repair in Japanese Open-Domain Conversations](https://aclanthology.org/2026.lrec-1.227/)
- **Corpus/task framing:** the LREC paper explicitly states the gap it fills — "when a system breakdown occurs, it remains unclear how the system should perform the repair, and no corpus was previously available to investigate this issue." — [WebSearch synthesis of ACL Anthology 2026.lrec-1.227](https://aclanthology.org/2026.lrec-1.227/)
- **Related generation paper (SIGDIAL 2026):** "Generation of Dialogue Breakdown Repair Utterance in Non-task Oriented Conversation" — a separate, closely related paper specifically on generating the repair utterance itself (not just detecting breakdown), reinforcing that detection and generation of the repair are treated as distinct sub-problems in this literature. — [ACL Anthology: 2026.sigdial-1.38](https://aclanthology.org/2026.sigdial-1.38/)
- **Detection — MultConDB (NAACL 2024 Industry Track):** a Multimodal Contextual Dialogue Breakdown model for real-time detection in conversational AI, combining current+past utterance context via LSTM + attention (optimal attention window size 5, balancing performance and inference speed for real-time use). Reported F1 = 69.27, a 15% F1 improvement over a Multimodal Transformer (MulT A+T) baseline; precision 65.96%, recall 72.94%. On an unseen September-2023 holdout set it generalized to F1 71.22 (precision 65.77%, recall 77.66%), which the authors present as evidence it generalizes across conversational-AI model versions. Qualitative analysis shows it clusters breakdown samples and distinguishes causes such as AI-agent silence vs. interruptions. — [ACL Anthology: Multimodal Contextual Dialogue Breakdown Detection for Conversational AI Models](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-industry.25/); [arXiv:2404.08156 via WebSearch](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.08156)
- **Detection — "Mm, Wat?" (EMNLP 2025):** detects Other-Initiated Repair (OIR) requests — i.e., the moment a *user* signals trouble/breakdown — in Dutch dialogues, using a multimodal model integrating linguistic and prosodic features explicitly grounded in Conversation Analysis categories. Finding: prosodic cues complement linguistic features and "significantly improve the results" over linguistic-only detection. — [arXiv:2510.24628](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.24628); [GitHub: haanh764/other_initiated_repair_detection](https://github.com/haanh764/other_initiated_repair_detection)
- **Scoping review (CUI 2024, Algjamdi et al.):** "System and User Strategies to Repair Conversational Breakdowns of Spoken Dialogue Systems: A Scoping Review." Categorizes system-repair strategies into six categories: confirmation, information, social, solve, ask, and disclosure. — [ACM: 10.1145/3640794.3665558](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3640794.3665558)
- **Open-problem framing, 2025:** "Detect, Explain, Escalate: Sustainable Dialogue Breakdown Management for LLM Agents" — the existence of a 2025 paper specifically proposing a *detect → explain → escalate* pipeline (rather than assuming automatic repair is always sufficient) implies that, as of 2025, breakdown handling still sometimes requires human escalation rather than being fully automatable. — [arXiv:2504.18839](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.18839)
- **Open-problem framing, broader:** "ChatChecker: A Framework for Dialogue System Testing and Evaluation Through Non-cooperative User Simulation" (2025) exists specifically to stress-test dialogue systems with adversarial/non-cooperative simulated users, implying standard evaluation still under-covers breakdown robustness. — [arXiv:2507.16792](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16792)
- **Open-problem framing, LLM-specific robustness paper:** "Talking to a Know-It-All GPT or a Second-Guesser Claude? How Repair reveals unreliable Multi-Turn Behavior in LLMs" (2026) simulates four-turn conversations with five instruction-tuned LLMs (GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4.5, DeepSeek-R1-distill-llama-70b, Phi-4, Mistral-7b-instruct-v0.3) around solvable/unsolvable math questions to probe repair behavior. Finding: large, systematic differences between models — GPT-4o and Phi-4 are "stubborn" (persist with initial, sometimes-incorrect answers even after correction), while Claude revises more readily but "introduces new errors" when flipping; none of the models handle repair uniformly reliably. — [arXiv:2604.19245](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.19245); [ACL Anthology: 2026.acl-long.651](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.651/)
- **Cross-disciplinary taxonomy effort (2026):** "Cross-Disciplinary Taxonomy and Modeling of Misunderstanding Generation, Amplification, and Detection, from Pragmatics to AI Agents" (Abbaschian, 2026) consolidates nine normally-non-citing fields into eleven distinct "failure modes" across eight analytical layers of the misunderstanding process (generation → amplification → detection/repair), with 8 mechanisms primarily generating divergence, 2 amplifying it, and 1 governing detection/repair. This is explicitly framed as needed because the relevant literatures (pragmatics, CA, HCI, NLP, etc.) don't currently talk to each other — itself evidence that no single widely-adopted cross-field taxonomy yet exists. — [arXiv:2608.13604](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13604)

### Inferences
- The clear 2024–2026 trend is a division of labor into (a) *detecting that* a breakdown/repair-trigger occurred (MultConDB, "Mm, Wat?"), and (b) *generating the right repair utterance given the breakdown type* (the Japanese LREC corpus, the companion SIGDIAL 2026 generation paper) — these are being treated as genuinely separate, both-still-hard sub-problems, not a single solved pipeline.
- The newness of the detect→explain→escalate framing (2025) and the Japanese personalized-repair corpus (2026) — both very recent — combined with the explicit statement in the LREC paper that "no corpus was previously available" for breakdown-type-specific repair, is reasonably strong evidence that **full-range breakdown-type-aware repair generation is still an open, unsolved problem as of late 2025/2026**, not a solved commodity capability, even though *detection* of that something has gone wrong (rephrase detection, OIR detection, MultConDB-style breakdown detection) is comparatively more mature.
- The "Know-It-All GPT or Second-Guesser Claude" finding — that different frontier LLMs fail in *opposite* directions (one too stubborn, one too quick to flip and introduce new errors) — is an important adversarial-novelty data point: it suggests that simply using a bigger/better frontier LLM as the dialogue engine does **not** by itself solve interaction-integrity/repair reliability; model-specific repair *tendencies* exist and apparently need to be measured and corrected for, which is a legitimate novel angle for a new project rather than something already closed out.

- Could not retrieve the full six-category definitions from the CUI 2024 scoping review (confirmation/information/social/solve/ask/disclosure) — only the category names were recoverable from search snippets; the review's conclusions on open-vs-solved status could not be directly quoted since strathprints.strath.ac.uk was blocked.
- Did not find a single, widely-cited benchmark that tests a system across *all* breakdown types simultaneously with a unified leaderboard — each paper found here (MultConDB, Japanese LREC corpus, BlockWorld-Repairs, REPAIR-QA) covers a different slice (industrial chat breakdown, personalized open-domain Japanese repair, multimodal TPR, QA TPR respectively), suggesting the field is still fragmented rather than converged on one eval standard. ## Across this literature, what should be reused rather than reinvented? Is there an existing taxonomy of repair/breakdown types a new project should adopt wholesale?

### Takeaway
Yes — there is a strong candidate for wholesale reuse at the *linguistic/structural* level (Dingemanse & Enfield's cross-linguistic other-initiated-repair typology, and Schegloff's turn-position repair framework) and at the *system-strategy* level (the CUI 2024 scoping review's six-category repair-strategy taxonomy and the Japanese corpus's ten-type breakdown taxonomy) — but no single taxonomy spans both breakdown-cause and repair-strategy end-to-end for voice dialogue specifically, which is a legitimate gap/opportunity rather than something to re-derive at the linguistic-primitives level.

### Cited Findings
- Dingemanse & Enfield's cross-linguistic typology of other-initiated repair (three basic repair-initiator types, validated across ten languages) is presented in the literature as a genuinely general, field-standard typology rather than a one-off categorization scheme for a single corpus. — [Dingemanse & Enfield, 2014/2015](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.2478/opli-2014-0007/html?lang=en)
- The CUI 2024 scoping review's six repair-strategy categories (confirmation, information, social, solve, ask, disclosure) is the most recent (2024) attempt at a system-side repair-strategy taxonomy specifically for spoken dialogue systems, synthesized from a scoping review (i.e., it is itself a literature-synthesis product, which is exactly the kind of artifact a new project should cite and build on rather than re-derive). — [ACM: System and User Strategies to Repair Conversational Breakdowns](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3640794.3665558)
- The Japanese LREC 2026 corpus's ten breakdown types (Wrong information, Ignore question, Ignore expectation, Unclear intention, Topic transition error, Lack of information, Self-contradiction, Contradiction, Repetition, Lack of relevant response) is the most granular recent breakdown-type taxonomy found in this pass, though it is built for Japanese open-domain chat, not voice/task-oriented English dialogue. — [ACL Anthology: 2026.lrec-1.227](https://aclanthology.org/2026.lrec-1.227/)
- The 2026 cross-disciplinary taxonomy paper (Abbaschian) explicitly motivates itself by noting that the relevant fields (pragmatics, CA, HCI, NLP/AI-agents, etc.) "do not ordinarily cite one another," implying that as of 2026 there is still no single dominant, universally-adopted taxonomy spanning both the linguistic-cause side and the AI-system-repair side of this problem. — [arXiv:2608.13604](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13604)

### Inferences
- The clearest "reuse, don't reinvent" recommendation from this pass: (1) adopt Schegloff's turn-position repair framework (first/second/third position) as the basic sequential model, since it is decades-old, uncontested, and already the organizing structure for the newest LLM benchmarks (REPAIR-QA, BlockWorld-Repairs); (2) adopt or adapt Dingemanse & Enfield's repair-initiator typology for the *user-signals-trouble* side; (3) adopt or adapt the CUI 2024 six-category system-strategy taxonomy for the *system-responds* side; and (4) treat breakdown-*type* taxonomies (like the Japanese ten-type corpus) as a starting template to adapt for voice/task-oriented English use cases rather than a ready-made fit, since no existing breakdown-type taxonomy found here was built for a voice-AI, task-oriented context.
- The genuinely open/novel territory for a new project, per this literature, is specifically: (a) unifying breakdown-detection and repair-strategy-selection into one voice-native pipeline (most existing work treats detection and generation as separate problems, and most of it is text/chat, not speech); and (b) characterizing *which* repair tendency a given LLM backbone exhibits (stubborn vs. over-correcting, per the "Know-It-All GPT or Second-Guesser Claude" paper) and compensating for it, which appears to be an essentially unaddressed angle as of this search.

### Gaps
- Did not find any taxonomy, in this pass, that was explicitly validated for *voice* (speech-channel, ASR-mediated) interaction breakdown as opposed to text-chat breakdown — this is worth flagging strongly to the report writer and to other clusters in this research thread (e.g., the ASR/paralinguistic-marker clusters already completed), since it suggests the "breakdown taxonomy" building block for this project's voice-AI use case may need adaptation rather than straight reuse.
