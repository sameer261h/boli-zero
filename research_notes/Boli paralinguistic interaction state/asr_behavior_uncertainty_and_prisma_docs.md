## In the general ASR/spoken-dialogue literature, does ASR confidence, hypothesis instability, or error-pattern behavior correlate with speaker hesitation, uncertainty, or interaction difficulty?

### Takeaway
Yes — this is an established, multi-decade research area with concrete named metrics (word/utterance confidence, N-best instability, "flicker," prosody-ASR-error correlation, problematic-dialogue prediction), but it is overwhelmingly framed around predicting *ASR correctness/WER* or *dialogue-system task failure*, not around inferring the *speaker's* subjective hesitation or emotional uncertainty as an end in itself. The closest the literature gets to "speaker-state inference from ASR behavior" is prosody-based prediction of recognition errors and problematic-dialogue prediction, both of which are about system-level difficulty rather than directly modeling speaker psychological uncertainty.

### Cited Findings
- Confidence scores are the standard uncertainty measurement in ASR, and "the efficacy of confidence score-based data selection crucially depends on its correlation with the WER" — i.e., confidence is validated against recognition error rate, the system's own difficulty, not against speaker-side labels — [Unsupervised Uncertainty Measures of ASR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04288) (Interspeech 2022 archive copy: [ISCA](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/tu22b_interspeech.pdf))
- The same line of work reports that "the sequence-level uncertainty of DNN-based ASR models is strongly correlated with speech intelligibility," linking model uncertainty to a property of the audio/speaker signal itself rather than only to model internals — [arXiv 2204.04288](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.04288)
- Streaming/incremental ASR produces partial hypotheses that are revised as more audio arrives; "flicker" is a defined metric that computes Levenshtein distance between consecutive partial hypotheses to quantify instability/revision frequency, and on-device RNN-T models are "especially prone to instability, revising their transcripts frequently" — [Analyzing the Quality and Stability of a Streaming End-to-End On-Device Speech Recognizer](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.01416) (Shangguan et al., Interspeech 2020; also [ISCA copy](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2020/shangguan20_interspeech.pdf))
- Earlier incremental-ASR work established that "partial results are unstable in the sense that they may later be revised," and proposed explicit metrics for stability vs. accuracy trade-offs in incremental recognizers — [Stability and Accuracy in Incremental Speech Recognition](https://aclanthology.org/W11-2014.pdf) (Baumann/Schlangen-era SIGdial work; also on [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220794474_Stability_and_Accuracy_in_Incremental_Speech_Recognition))
- Prosodic features (not ASR confidence per se, but a closely related acoustic-behavior signal) have long been shown to predict ASR misrecognition: "prosody improves the detection of ASR errors, and prosodic features can improve prediction of ASR misrecognitions over standard techniques for ASR rejection" — [Generalizing Prosodic Prediction of Speech Recognition Errors](https://www.isca-archive.org/icslp_2000/hirschberg00_icslp.pdf) (Hirschberg et al., ICSLP 2000)
- Spoken dialogue systems research has built explicit "problematic dialogue" predictors using features that include recognition confidence and dialogue features, to flag when a conversation is going badly (a system-level difficulty signal, adjacent to but not identical to speaker-uncertainty detection) — [Automatically Training a Problematic Dialogue Predictor for a Spoken Dialogue System](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1106.1817)
- Classic spoken-dialogue error literature frames ASR misrecognition handling as a core design problem: "users of spoken dialogue systems have difficulty correcting system misconceptions, so it is important for ASR systems to know when their best hypothesis is incorrect" — [Error Detection and Recovery in Spoken Dialogue Systems](https://aclanthology.org/W04-3006.pdf)
- A 2021 patent family ("Detecting potential significant errors in speech recognition results" / "Detecting potential medically-significant errors...") explicitly describes using co-located "signs of potential uncertainty" (including **prosody information and hesitation vocalization information**) across multiple speech inputs to flag which specific words/phrases the speaker was likely uncertain about — this is the single closest hit found to "ASR-adjacent signal → speaker uncertainty," but it is a patent application, not peer-reviewed research, and it leans on prosody/hesitation-vocalization cues (acoustic), not on ASR confidence/hypothesis behavior alone — [USPTO filing, e.g. 9685153](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9685153)
- N-best hypothesis utilization is used in robustness-oriented NLU work (running downstream models over multiple ASR n-best hypotheses) as a way to hedge against ASR uncertainty in dialogue systems, per "How Robust r u? Evaluating Task-Oriented Dialogue Systems on Spoken Conversations" — [arXiv 2109.13489](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.13489)
- Confidence estimation for end-to-end ASR is a large, active sub-literature (BLSTM-based, entropy-based, Whisper-based, ELECTRA-rescoring-based methods), but multiple recent papers note that "confidence scores remain unreliable" even as E2E accuracy improves, "suggesting a more fundamental problem" with calibration — synthesized across [Fast Entropy-Based Word Confidence (arXiv 2212.08703)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.08703), [Multi-Task Word/Utterance Confidence with Deletion Prediction (arXiv 2104.12870)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.12870), [Adopting Whisper for Confidence Estimation (arXiv 2502.13446)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.13446)
- Under noise specifically, modern E2E ASR (e.g., Whisper) is not just less accurate but *overconfident*: "overconfident errors increase dramatically at low signal-to-noise ratios, with 10–20% of tokens incorrectly predicted with confidence above 0.7" at -18 to -5 dB SNR — [Identifying and Calibrating Overconfidence in Noisy Speech Recognition](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.07195) (2025) — this is directly relevant to telephony/noisy deployments, where naive use of a confidence score as an uncertainty signal could be actively misleading rather than merely sparse
- Narrowband 8kHz telephony audio itself degrades ASR WER by roughly "8–12% absolute" compared to wideband audio, independent of any confidence-calibration question — synthesized from search results on the overconfidence/noisy-ASR literature, [arXiv 2509.07195](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.07195) and related sources
- Deletion-prediction is explicitly modeled as a joint task alongside word/utterance confidence estimation in E2E ASR, i.e., the error-type (not just the confidence number) is itself treated as a structured, predictable signal — [Multi-Task Learning for End-to-End ASR Word and Utterance Confidence with Deletion Prediction](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.12870)

### Inferences
- The literature strongly supports the *general premise* that ASR-internal behavior (confidence, instability, error type/location) is a validated signal of acoustic/linguistic *difficulty* — but nearly all of this validation is against ground-truth transcription correctness (WER), not against independently labeled speaker hesitation/uncertainty/emotion. Any claim that "ASR confidence ≈ speaker uncertainty" is an *extrapolation* from this literature, not something it directly establishes.
- The overconfidence-under-noise finding is a meaningful caution specific to this project's context: because Prisma targets exactly the noisy-8kHz-telephony regime where overconfidence is most pronounced in other E2E ASR systems, *even if* Gnani exposed a confidence score, it would need calibration validation before being trusted as a difficulty signal in this operating regime — this is a reason to not over-index on confidence scores even hypothetically.
- Hypothesis-instability metrics (flicker) are a genuinely novel-feeling angle for "interaction difficulty" detection (more revision = harder utterance), but they require access to *streaming/partial* output, which (see next section) is not confirmed to be exposed by the REST endpoint actually tested.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that directly regresses or correlates ASR confidence/instability against an independently-collected speaker-hesitation or self-reported-uncertainty label (e.g., via post-hoc survey or physiological measure) in a spoken dialogue corpus. The literature infers "this must reflect speaker difficulty" but validates against WER, not against the speaker's actual internal state — this gap is itself a finding (see the dedicated gap question below).

## Given that the real integration returns only a final transcript string, what does text-based disfluency-detection research say about recovering disfluency signal from transcript text alone?

### Takeaway
This is a mature, well-established NLP sub-field (not novel) with a canonical benchmark corpus (Switchboard) and a long lineage of methods — from Transformation-Based Learning through BLSTM/CRF sequence taggers to modern transformer approaches — explicitly designed to detect fillers, repetitions, restarts, and repair constructions **from transcript text alone**, which directly covers the "textual evidence of disfluency surviving transcription" angle named in the brief.

### Cited Findings
- Standard disfluency annotation (as used on Switchboard) decomposes a disfluency into a **reparandum** (the words intended to be replaced/ignored), an **interruption point** marking the end of the reparandum, the associated **repair**, and an optional **interregnum** after the interruption point (filled pauses, discourse cue words, etc.) — [Disfluency Detection using a Bidirectional LSTM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.03209) (Zayats et al.)
- A BLSTM-based tagger using word embeddings, POS tags, and lexical pattern features, with an explicit repair mechanism and ILP-based structural constraints, achieved an F-score of 85.9% on the Switchboard disfluency-detection benchmark — [same source, arXiv 1604.03209](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.03209) / [homes.cs.washington.edu mirror](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~hannaneh/papers/disfl-lstm.pdf)
- Transformation-Based Learning (TBL) is cited as an earlier, classic text/lexical-feature approach to disfluency detection in speech transcriptions, using "lexical features (word usage and sentence structure)" rather than acoustic features — per WebSearch summary of search results referencing this lineage
- "Automatic Disfluency Detection from Untranscribed Speech" (Microsoft Applied Sciences / later arXiv version) frames the problem explicitly in terms of recovering disfluency structure even without clean ground-truth transcripts, underscoring that the task is fundamentally textual/structural rather than acoustic — [Microsoft paper](https://www.microsoft.com/applied-sciences/uploads/publications/134/automatic-disfluency-detection.pdf), [arXiv 2311.00867](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.00867)
- Multi-task self-supervised learning approaches have been applied specifically to disfluency detection, suggesting the field has moved toward representation-learning methods built on top of text (and sometimes shallow acoustic) features — [Multi-Task Self-Supervised Learning for Disfluency Detection](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.05378)
- Neural constituency parsing of speech transcripts is cited as a related/overlapping line of work, treating disfluency removal as a parsing-adjacent structural problem on transcript text — [Neural Constituency Parsing of Speech Transcripts](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.08535)
- Incremental dependency parsing work explicitly connects disfluency detection to real-time, word-by-word text processing, relevant to any system that must flag disfluency as transcript tokens arrive rather than after the fact — [Incremental dependency parsing and disfluency](https://apc38.user.srcf.net/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/moore-et-al_2015_tsd.pdf)
- RNN-based incremental disfluency detection is a further documented approach within this lineage — ["Recurrent Neural Networks for Incremental Disfluency Detection"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/309292641_Recurrent_Neural_Networks_for_Incremental_Disfluency_Detection)
- A more recent (2024) paper specifically addresses "Disfluency Detection and Removal in Speech Transcriptions," indicating the task remains actively worked on rather than considered solved/closed — [ACL Anthology, 2024.stil-1.16](https://aclanthology.org/2024.stil-1.16.pdf)
- Separately, dialogue-systems-specific work frames disfluency detection as a multi-task-learning problem explicitly for "domain-general spoken disfluency detection in dialogue systems" — directly relevant to applying this to a project like Boli's — [Multi-Task Learning for Domain-General Spoken Disfluency Detection in Dialogue Systems](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.03352)

### Inferences
- This confirms the brief's own hypothesis: the "textual evidence of disfluency surviving transcription" angle is **not a gap** — it is one of the best-covered problems in the entire ASR/NLP adjacency, with a standard corpus (Switchboard), standard annotation scheme (reparandum/interruption-point/repair/interregnum), and a 20+ year methodological lineage from TBL through BLSTM to modern neural sequence models. Given that the actual Prisma integration returns only a transcript string, this is the most directly actionable, well-grounded literature for the project: lexical disfluency markers (repeated words, restarts, filled-pause tokens if transcribed, explicit hedge words) are a legitimate, well-studied signal that survives even a minimal ASR integration, independent of any ASR-internal metadata.
- Note an important caveat specific to this project: Switchboard-style disfluency detection assumes the transcript *preserves* disfluency tokens (filled pauses, repetitions, false starts) verbatim. Gnani's `format=verbatim` mode is presumably intended to do exactly this (as opposed to `format=transcribe`'s ITN/cleanup mode) — but whether Prisma's verbatim transcripts actually retain Hindi/Indic filler tokens (e.g., "matlab," "pata nahi," "umm" equivalents) with the fidelity Switchboard-trained detectors assume for English was not tested or documented anywhere found in this research pass. This is a testable empirical question for the project's own pulled Prisma transcripts, not something resolvable from literature alone.
- Explicit lexical uncertainty markers ("maybe," "I think," "probably," "pata nahi") are a related but distinct phenomenon from classic disfluency (filled pauses/repairs) — they are closer to hedging/epistemic-stance detection in computational linguistics than to the Switchboard disfluency-detection task proper. No source in this research pass specifically addressed hedge-word/epistemic-marker detection in Indic-language ASR transcripts; this is a narrower, Hindi/code-switching-specific gap not covered by the (largely English/Switchboard-centric) literature found here.

### Gaps
- No literature was found specifically on disfluency detection for Hindi, Hinglish, or other Indic-language transcripts (as opposed to English/Switchboard) — the entire cited lineage above is English-centric. Whether Switchboard-style reparandum/repair annotation schemes transfer cleanly to Hindi code-switched speech is unaddressed by anything found here and would need separate, targeted search.
- No literature was found specifically on detecting *lexical epistemic hedges* ("I think," "maybe," "pata nahi") as a distinct task from classical disfluency detection — this sits closer to hedging/stance-detection NLP literature, which was out of scope for the searches run in this pass.

## Is there published research specifically correlating ASR output artifacts (not audio) with speaker hesitation/uncertainty/emotional state — an "ASR fingerprint" of interaction difficulty?

### Takeaway
This area is thin/underdeveloped, and the literature itself flags the adjacent problem as underexplored: speech-emotion-recognition-from-ASR-transcripts research exists and shows ASR transcripts are a workable input for emotion recognition, but it is about recognizing emotion *despite* ASR errors (robustness), not about using the *pattern of ASR errors itself* as a diagnostic signal of speaker state. No paper was found that treats ASR's own error/confidence/instability behavior as a direct, validated proxy for speaker hesitation or emotional uncertainty. This gap is itself a notable, reportable finding.

### Cited Findings
- A comprehensive 2024 study on "Speech Emotion Recognition with ASR Transcripts" finds that SER is "robust to relatively low WER," with ~12% WER having "minimal impact on emotion recognition performance compared to ground-truth transcripts," and in some cases ASR-generated transcripts even outperformed ground truth for the emotion-recognition task — [Speech Emotion Recognition with ASR Transcripts: A Comprehensive study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.08353). This is about SER being *robust to* ASR noise, which is the inverse framing from what the brief is asking (using ASR noise *as* the signal).
- A related comprehensive study on ASR representations for noise-robust SER notes that "there currently exists no dataset specific to emotional speech for ASR, highlighting a notable research gap in this area" — [A Comprehensive Study on the Effectiveness of ASR Representations for Noise-Robust Speech Emotion Recognition](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.07093) — this is a direct, citable statement of a dataset/research gap at the ASR-emotion intersection, supporting the brief's suspicion that this area is thin.
- "ASR-based Features for Emotion Recognition" (2018, ACL W18-3307) and "ASR and Emotional Speech: A Word-Level Investigation of the Mutual Impact of Speech and Emotion Recognition" both study the *bidirectional* relationship between emotion and ASR performance (i.e., how emotion degrades ASR, and how ASR output can be mined as features for SER), but these are about general emotion categories, not specifically about *uncertainty/hesitation* as a target state, and not about treating error-location or confidence-instability patterns as a deliberate "fingerprint" construct — [ASR-based Features for Emotion Recognition](https://aclanthology.org/W18-3307.pdf), [ASR and Emotional Speech: word-level investigation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.16065)
- The 2601.17901 line of work ("Speech Emotion Recognition with ASR Integration") frames ASR and SER as a joint/multi-task problem (e.g., multi-task learning combining ASR with gender recognition sub-tasks to improve SER), which is architecturally adjacent but is about *improving SER by training jointly with ASR objectives*, not about *post-hoc reading of a deployed, black-box ASR's output behavior* as a signal — which is the Boli project's actual situation (no access to retrain or jointly architect with Prisma) — [arXiv 2601.17901](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.17901)
- The patent-literature hit found in the first key question (co-located "signs of potential uncertainty," including hesitation vocalization information, used to flag speaker-uncertain words/phrases) remains the closest match found across this entire research pass to the brief's "ASR fingerprint" concept — but again, it is patent-application language, not peer-reviewed research, and its signal basis is prosody/hesitation-vocalization (essentially acoustic paralinguistics), not ASR hypothesis/confidence/error-pattern behavior in isolation from the audio — [USPTO 9685153](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9685153)

### Inferences
- The brief's suspicion that this is a thin/novel area is well-supported: across two full rounds of varied search queries ("ASR errors correlate speaker hesitation uncertainty emotional state," "ASR fingerprint recognizer behavior speaker emotion novel signal gap"), no peer-reviewed paper surfaced that treats ASR's own behavioral output (confidence scores, hypothesis revision, deletion/substitution patterns) — independent of the underlying audio — as a validated, direct proxy for speaker-side hesitation or emotional uncertainty. The nearest literature either (a) validates ASR confidence/instability against WER/correctness (first key question above), or (b) studies SER's robustness to ASR noise as an input modality (this question), neither of which closes the specific loop the brief describes.
- This means a project building an "ASR behavior as interaction-difficulty fingerprint" signal would be doing something that is plausible by analogy/extrapolation from adjacent literature (confidence correlates with WER; WER correlates with acoustic difficulty; acoustic difficulty sometimes correlates with speaker hesitation/emotion in separate prosody literature) but is not itself a direct, previously-validated research finding. This should be reported as a genuine, citable gap in the literature — a defensible, non-trivial contribution space — rather than something the project can simply cite prior work for.
- Combined with the Prisma-specific finding (no confidence/timestamp/instability data is actually exposed by the tested endpoint), the practical implication is that this entire angle — "use ASR behavior as an uncertainty signal" — is currently **not operationalizable** with the real Gnani integration as tested, regardless of whether the underlying research idea has merit. The only ASR-adjacent signal actually available to the project right now is the final transcript text itself, which routes the problem back to the well-established text-based disfluency-detection literature (previous key question) rather than to ASR-internal behavioral signals.
