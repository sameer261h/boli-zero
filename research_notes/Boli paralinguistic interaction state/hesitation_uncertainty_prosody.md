## Key Question 1: Pon-Barry (2008), "Prosodic Manifestations of Confidence and Uncertainty in Spoken Language"

### Takeaway
Pon-Barry (2008) is the foundational paper establishing that confidence/uncertainty in spoken language has a measurable prosodic signature in pitch (F0), energy/intensity (RMS), and timing features — and, importantly, that these cues appear not only across the whole utterance but are localizable to specific sub-regions (the uncertain word/phrase and its surrounding context). This is a single-study, corpus-specific finding (one elicited corpus, English, Harvard), not yet a large-scale replicated result, but it is the most-cited origin point of this research line and its feature set is reused by nearly all follow-on work.

### Cited Findings
- The paper is titled "Prosodic Manifestations of Confidence and Uncertainty in Spoken Language," authored by Heather Pon-Barry (Harvard School of Engineering and Applied Sciences), presented at Interspeech 2008 (Brisbane, Australia, Sept 22–26, 2008). — [ISCA Archive PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2008/ponbarry08_interspeech.pdf)
- The study extracted three families of prosodic features: (1) **pitch (F0)** — minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation, range, relative position in the utterance of the F0 min/max, and absolute slope; (2) **intensity (RMS energy)** — minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation, and relative positions of min/max; (3) **temporal features** — total silence duration, percent silence, total duration, speaking duration (total minus pauses), and speaking rate. — [Lacuna synthesis of the paper](https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/work/prosodic-manifestations-of-confidence-and-uncertainty-in-spoken-language/wrk_78fae7f3d9a50080d2a4b9062381ff9a); feature list corroborated by [WebSearch synthesis of the ISCA PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2008/ponbarry08_interspeech.pdf)
- Key finding: prosodic manifestations of confidence/uncertainty occur **both in the local region that triggers the uncertainty and in the surrounding context** of the utterance — i.e., some cues are strongest utterance-wide, others are strongest in the local target-word region or its immediate context. This localization result is the paper's most distinctive contribution and directly motivates the 2009 follow-up (Key Question 2). — [Lacuna synthesis](https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/work/prosodic-manifestations-of-confidence-and-uncertainty-in-spoken-language/wrk_78fae7f3d9a50080d2a4b9062381ff9a)
- The underlying data source is the **Harvard Uncertainty Speech Corpus**, a corpus specifically built/curated by Pon-Barry for this line of research (elicited spoken responses with annotated certainty levels). — [ponbarry.com corpus page](http://www.ponbarry.com/uncertaintycorpus/index.html)

### Inferences
- Because pitch range/slope and RMS-energy variability/position features recur across both the 2008 and 2009 Pon-Barry papers and are the same feature families used in GeMAPS/eGeMAPS (Key Question 5), this specific feature set (F0 descriptive statistics, intensity descriptive statistics, pause/speaking-rate temporal features) functions as a de facto standard toolkit for hesitation/confidence prosody work, not an idiosyncratic one-off.
- The localization finding (cues present in both the local triggering region and surrounding context) is the conceptual seed for treating uncertainty as a **continuous, span-level** phenomenon rather than a single utterance-level categorical label — directly relevant to Boli's "interaction state" framing instead of categorical emotion labels.

### Gaps
- Could not verify from available snippets: the exact corpus size (number of speakers/utterances), the specific classifier/model architecture used, or any quantitative accuracy/correlation statistics reported in the 2008 paper itself (the 2009 paper's 91% figure, verified below, belongs to the follow-up study, not this one). WebFetch to the ISCA PDF was blocked, so these numeric details could not be independently confirmed.
- Could not confirm from search snippets whether the corpus consists of naturalistic or acted/elicited uncertainty (task framing suggests elicited — subjects answering quiz-style questions — but this was not explicitly stated in retrieved snippets, so flagged as unconfirmed rather than assumed).
- No replication study by an independent lab was found in search results; this appears to be a single-study result from one lab/corpus. Should be labeled provisional until corroborated independently (it substantially is corroborated in direction, if not magnitude, by Kirkland et al. 2022's F0/rate findings and by Goupil et al. 2021's independent "common prosodic signature" study — see Key Question 6).

## Key Question 2: Pon-Barry & Shieber (2009), "Identifying Uncertain Words Within an Utterance via Prosodic Features"

### Takeaway
This Interspeech 2009 follow-up shows that uncertainty can be **localized to a specific word/phrase within an utterance**, not just judged holistically — using sub-utterance prosodic features (of the target word, its context, and the whole utterance combined) to pick the uncertain word out of a forced binary choice against a matched "control word," reaching 91% accuracy. The commonly cited "~91%" figure is verified, but it needs important context: it is accuracy on a binary (target-vs-control) forced-choice discrimination task, not open-vocabulary word-level tagging of continuous unconstrained speech, and the control word was deliberately matched for length/POS to make the comparison fair — so the number should not be read as "91% of individual words in arbitrary speech are correctly flagged as uncertain."

### Cited Findings
- Full citation: Pon-Barry, Heather and Stuart M. Shieber, "Identifying Uncertain Words within an Utterance via Prosodic Features," Interspeech 2009 (Brighton, UK, Sept 6–10, 2009). — [Harvard DASH record](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-972f-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)
- Research question: given an utterance already classified as uncertain, can prosodic features identify **which word or phrase** the speaker is uncertain about — i.e., push uncertainty detection from utterance-level to word/span-level. — [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-972f-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)
- Feature extraction was done at **three levels**: the whole utterance, the context surrounding the target word, and the target word itself — directly building on the localization finding from Pon-Barry 2008. — [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-972f-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)
- Task design: a binary forced choice between the actual target (uncertain) word and a "control word" — a word selected to be similar in length and part-of-speech to the target word, serving as a matched baseline/negative example. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/48547560_Identifying_Uncertain_Words_within_an_Utterance_via_Prosodic_Features)
- **Verified numeric result:** the best model (combining whole-utterance, context, and target-word prosodic features) achieved **91% accuracy** on this target-vs-control binary task, a **71% relative error reduction** over a baseline trained only on non-prosodic features, whose own accuracy was **67.44%**. — [WebSearch synthesis of paper](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037c-6ff6-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content); corroborated independently by [ResearchGate figure caption](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Correlations-with-perceived-level-of-certainty-per-fold-for-the-Combination-O-and-the_fig1_48547560)
- A related/companion Harvard output, "The Importance of Sub-Utterance Prosody in Predicting Level of Certainty," appears to be an extended-abstract or thesis-chapter version covering the same sub-utterance prosody methodology. — [Harvard DASH bitstream](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstreams/7312037c-6ff5-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/download)

### Inferences
- The 91% figure is real and traceable to the primary source via multiple converging secondary citations, so the secondary-source claim in the brief is **verified**, but it must be reported with the caveat that it reflects a constrained binary discrimination task (target word vs. a matched control word) rather than free-running word-level uncertainty tagging — reporting the bare "91%" without this context would overstate the generality of the result.
- 67.44% as the non-prosodic baseline suggests the task is far from trivial by text/lexical features alone (barely above chance-plus for a matched-pair task), which strengthens the claim that prosody carries substantial independent signal for localizing uncertainty beyond what word choice/content alone reveals.
- This paper is the most direct precedent for Boli's ambition to localize "interaction state" (hesitation/uncertainty) to a sub-utterance span rather than only tagging whole turns — it demonstrates feasibility (on a constrained task, on one corpus) 15+ years ago with fairly shallow, hand-engineered prosodic features (no deep learning needed).

### Gaps
- Could not find or verify the exact composition/size of the test corpus (number of target/control word pairs, number of speakers) from available snippets — would need the full PDF to confirm the sample size behind the 91% figure, which affects how much statistical confidence to place in it.
- Could not verify whether 91% generalizes beyond the binary target-vs-control setup to an open-set word-level uncertainty localization task (i.e., scanning all words in an utterance and flagging the uncertain one without a pre-selected control). This is a meaningful methodological gap for any claim that the result demonstrates general-purpose word-level uncertainty tagging.
- No independent replication of this specific 91%/67.44% pairing was found; it rests on one study using one corpus (Harvard Uncertainty Speech Corpus, English, apparently elicited rather than fully naturalistic speech).

## Key Question 3: Wottawa et al. (2020), "Towards Interactive Annotation for Hesitation in Conversational Speech" (LREC 2020)

### Takeaway
Wottawa, Tahon, Marin & Audibert establish a **continuous (non-categorical) hesitation annotation scheme** on French conversational speech (the NCCFr corpus), show that filled-pause count and vowel duration both increase with annotated hesitation degree, and train regression models to automate/extend the labeling — reaching an RMSE of 1.6 on their continuous scale. This directly supports treating hesitation as a graded signal (consistent with Boli's "interaction state" framing) rather than a binary/categorical label, and it is a concrete precedent for semi-automating what would otherwise be expensive manual annotation.

### Cited Findings
- Full citation: Jane Wottawa, Marie Tahon, Apolline Marin, Nicolas Audibert, "Towards Interactive Annotation for Hesitation in Conversational Speech," Proceedings of the 12th Language Resources and Evaluation Conference (LREC 2020), Marseille, France, May 2020, European Language Resources Association. Authors affiliated with LIUM (Laboratoire d'Informatique de l'Université du Mans) and LPP (Laboratoire de Phonétique et Phonologie). — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.190/)
- The goal is to **automatize the labeling of hesitant speech as a marker of expressed uncertainty**, motivated explicitly by the cost (time and human-resource expense) of manual annotation at scale. — [ACL Anthology record via WebSearch synthesis](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.190/)
- The NCCFr corpus was **manually annotated on a continuous scale from -3 to +3**, covering 5,834 annotated speech chunks — explicitly a continuous rather than categorical hesitation scoring scheme. — [WebSearch synthesis of ACL Anthology / HAL record](https://hal.science/hal-02505333v1)
- Core acoustic finding: **the number of filled pauses and vowel duration both increase with the annotated degree of hesitation** — i.e., these two measurable acoustic/disfluency features track graded hesitation intensity, not just its presence/absence. — [WebSearch synthesis](https://hal.science/hal-02505333v1)
- The authors trained regression models to automatically predict the continuous hesitation degree for unannotated chunks (to extend coverage beyond the manually labeled subset), reporting an **RMSE of 1.6** on their -3..+3 scale as "encouraging" automatic-prediction performance. — [WebSearch synthesis of paper](https://hal.science/hal-02505333v1)

### Inferences
- An RMSE of 1.6 on a 7-point-wide (-3 to +3) continuous scale is a non-trivial fraction of the scale's range (~23%), suggesting the automatic prediction is informative but not highly precise — consistent with the authors' own framing of the result as "encouraging" rather than "solved." This should be read as a promising proof-of-concept for semi-automated continuous hesitation scoring, not a production-ready detector.
- The annotation scheme itself (continuous scale + filled-pause-count + vowel-duration features) is conceptually reusable/adaptable for Boli: it demonstrates a lightweight, linguistically-motivated feature set (disfluency counts + vowel lengthening) can drive a continuous hesitation estimate without requiring full categorical emotion labeling, directly supporting the broader thesis that hesitation/uncertainty is better modeled as continuous "interaction state" than discrete emotion categories.
- This is French conversational (naturalistic, not elicited/acted) speech, which is a methodologically valuable complement to Pon-Barry's apparently elicited English corpus — it suggests the filled-pause/vowel-duration-hesitation relationship holds in spontaneous dialogue, not just task-elicited uncertainty, though this is still a single corpus/language and should not yet be treated as cross-linguistically confirmed.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm from available snippets whether the paper releases its annotation tool, codebase, or model weights publicly for outside reuse — search results describe the method and corpus but did not surface an explicit statement of tool/code availability (e.g., a GitHub link or toolkit name). This should be flagged as an open question for anyone wanting to directly reuse their "interactive annotation" tooling rather than just the conceptual scheme.
- Could not verify the exact inter-annotator agreement/reliability of the manual continuous-scale annotations (important for judging how reproducible/robust the underlying "ground truth" hesitation labels are) — not found in retrieved snippets.
- No information found on whether this annotation scheme or corpus has been picked up/reused by other research groups since 2020 (citation trail for direct scheme reuse was not confirmed in search results, beyond general awareness of the paper in disfluency-survey literature, e.g., "Fillers in Spoken Language Understanding" arXiv survey, which cites it as part of the broader filler/disfluency literature).

## Key Question 4: Kirkland et al. (2022), "Where's the uh, hesitation? The interplay between filled pause location, speech rate and fundamental frequency in perception of confidence" (Interspeech 2022)

### Takeaway
Using synthesized speech with independently controlled filled-pause location, speech rate, and F0, Kirkland, Lameris, Székely & Gustafson show these three features have an **additive effect on listener-perceived confidence**: the most confident-sounding synthetic utterances combine no filler + low F0 + high speech rate, and the least confident combine a mid-utterance filler + high F0 + low speech rate. Filled-pause presence/location has the strongest single effect, with F0 and rate acting as finer-grained modulators — and critically, this is a **perception study** (how human listeners judge confidence), not a classifier-accuracy study, directly answering the "perceived vs. machine-inferred" distinction in the research brief.

### Cited Findings
- Full citation: (Ambika) Kirkland, Harm Lameris, Éva Székely, Joakim Gustafson, "Where's the uh, hesitation? The interplay between filled pause location, speech rate and fundamental frequency in perception of confidence," Interspeech 2022, Incheon, Korea, DOI: 10.21437/Interspeech.2022-10973. — [ISCA Archive](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/kirkland22_interspeech.pdf); [ISCA Archive abstract page](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/kirkland22_interspeech.html)
- Methodology: the study uses **synthesized spontaneous speech** with controllable prosody, allowing filled-pause location, speech rate, and F0 to be manipulated independently and orthogonally — a method the authors present as a novel tool for studying speech perception of these variables in isolation (impossible to cleanly disentangle in natural recordings). — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/363646845_Where's_the_uh_hesitation_The_interplay_between_filled_pause_location_speech_rate_and_fundamental_frequency_in_perception_of_confidence)
- Key result: an **additive effect of all three features** on perceived confidence. Most-confident-sounding utterances = no filler + low F0 + high speech rate. Least-confident-sounding = medial (mid-utterance) filled pause + high F0 + low speech rate. — [WebSearch synthesis of ISCA PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/kirkland22_interspeech.pdf)
- Relative importance: **insertion/location of filled pauses had the strongest influence** on perceived confidence of the three factors, while pitch (F0) and speaking rate serve as finer-grained controls for calibrating the uncertainty impression. — [WebSearch synthesis](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/kirkland22_interspeech.pdf)
- The authors frame this method explicitly as enabling future "conversational systems which are more effectively able to engage in and respond to social behaviors" — i.e., this line of work is already explicitly aimed at voice-AI/dialogue-system applications, not purely linguistic description. — [WebSearch synthesis](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/kirkland22_interspeech.pdf)

### Inferences
- The finding that **low F0 = more confident, high F0 = less confident** is directionally consistent with the general prosody-of-emotion literature associating higher/more variable pitch with arousal/anxiety and lower, more controlled pitch with composure — this gives Kirkland et al.'s F0 finding some a priori plausibility rather than being an isolated surprising result.
- Because this is a controlled perception study (using synthesized stimuli to isolate variables) rather than a corpus-mining/classifier study, it answers a different but complementary question from Pon-Barry's work: Pon-Barry asks "what features correlate with a speaker's actual internal certainty state," while Kirkland et al. ask "what features make a listener judge an utterance as confident," regardless of the speaker's true internal state. For Boli's purposes, both matter — if the goal is to infer a human's actual uncertainty, Pon-Barry-style correlation studies are more relevant; if the goal is to control Boli's *own* synthesized speech to sound appropriately confident/hesitant, Kirkland et al. is directly actionable.
- The additive-effects framing (filler location > F0 ≈ rate, combinable) suggests a simple, interpretable scoring heuristic could approximate perceived confidence without deep modeling: filled-pause presence/position as the dominant binary/categorical signal, with F0 and rate as continuous modulating terms — potentially a cheap, explainable first-pass feature set for Boli.

### Gaps
- Could not verify exact effect sizes, statistical significance values, or the number of listener participants/stimuli from available snippets — WebFetch to the ISCA PDF was blocked, so only the qualitative directional findings could be confirmed, not the magnitude or significance of each factor's contribution.
- This study uses **synthesized** speech stimuli for perception testing, not naturalistic recordings — strong internal validity (clean causal manipulation) but an open question of how well the additive-effects pattern transfers to natural, uncontrolled human speech where these three features co-vary with many other cues simultaneously.
- No information found in search results on speaker/language diversity of the perception-test listener pool (e.g., native-English-only vs. cross-linguistic listeners), so cross-cultural generalizability of the specific "low F0 = confident" finding cannot be assessed from what was retrieved.

## Key Question 5: openSMILE and the GeMAPS/eGeMAPS Standardized Feature Sets

### Takeaway
openSMILE is a free, open-source, C/C++ acoustic feature-extraction toolkit (originally from TU Munich, now maintained by audEERING) built for exactly this kind of paralinguistic analysis; it is fast enough to run far faster than real-time on CPU, making it practically feasible to run cheaply on short audio clips. GeMAPS/eGeMAPS are its standardized, theory-driven feature sets (18 low-level descriptors / 88 features respectively) covering frequency, energy, spectral, and cepstral (eGeMAPS only) properties of speech, and among these, F0-related and loudness/energy-related features are the most established and well-validated correlates of **arousal** specifically — which is the dimension most relevant to hesitation/uncertainty/confidence framing (vs. valence, which performs much worse and is less relevant to this use case).

### Cited Findings
- openSMILE ("Munich Versatile and Fast Open-Source Audio Feature Extractor") is free/open-source, written in C/C++ with no third-party dependencies for core functionality, designed for both real-time streaming and offline batch feature extraction, and widely used in paralinguistic challenges and speech-emotion-recognition research. — [audeering.github.io documentation](https://audeering.github.io/opensmile/about.html); [audEERING research page](https://www.audeering.com/research/opensmile/)
- Performance/feasibility: openSMILE can extract a large feature set (reportedly up to ~27k features in its full configuration) with a **Real Time Factor (RTF) of 0.08** — i.e., roughly 12.5x faster than real-time on CPU — confirming it is computationally cheap enough to process short audio clips quickly without GPU acceleration. — [WebSearch synthesis of openSMILE documentation/tutorial](https://records.sigmm.org/2015/01/05/opensmile-the-munich-open-source-large-scale-multimedia-feature-extractor-a-tutorial-for-version-2-1/)
- **GeMAPS** (Geneva Minimalistic Acoustic Parameter Set) comprises a compact set of **18 low-level descriptors (LLDs)**: frequency-related — pitch/F0 (on a semitone scale from 27.5 Hz), jitter, formants 1–3 (center frequency) plus formant-1 bandwidth, shimmer, loudness (perceived-intensity estimate from an auditory spectrum); spectral-balance — harmonic difference H1–H2, harmonic difference H1–A3, alpha ratio, Hammarberg index, plus spectral flux and MFCC-related descriptors. — [Eyben et al. GeMAPS paper, via WebSearch synthesis](https://sail.usc.edu/publications/files/eyben-preprinttaffc-2015.pdf); cross-corroborated by [jmj3047 summary](https://jmj3047.github.io/2023/04/21/eGeMAPS/)
- **eGeMAPS** extends GeMAPS's base descriptors with additional (including MFCC) parameters to yield **88 total acoustic features**, explicitly designed for speech emotion recognition and, more broadly, for "automatic paralinguistic or clinical speech analysis." — [WebSearch synthesis combining Eyben et al. paper and secondary descriptions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9571288/)
- Florian Eyben (with Klaus Scherer, Björn Schuller and colleagues) is the key author of both the original openSMILE toolkit and the GeMAPS/eGeMAPS standardization; GeMAPS is described as the most frequently used configuration in the emotion-recognition literature, with validation results reported across six major affective speech databases. — [WebSearch synthesis](https://sail.usc.edu/publications/files/eyben-preprinttaffc-2015.pdf)
- Relevance to arousal vs. valence specifically: eGeMAPS performs well for **arousal** (reported around ~80% UAR generally, and above 93% UAR on some specific datasets) but is markedly weaker for **valence**, where "there are no strong discriminative speech features available to discriminate between positive speech and negative speech." Pitch is singled out as "one of the most reliable features" and is described as functioning as an index of arousal. — [WebSearch synthesis of multiple SER review sources](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9571288/)

### Inferences
- Because hesitation/uncertainty/confidence are arousal-adjacent (physiological activation / cognitive-effort-linked) states rather than valence-linked (positive/negative affect) states, the fact that eGeMAPS's F0 and loudness/energy descriptors are specifically strong for arousal (not valence) is a direct methodological argument for why this feature set is well-suited to Boli's "interaction state" goal — it is reaching for the right axis of variation (activation/effort) rather than the axis (positive/negative sentiment) where acoustic features are known to be weak.
- Given the RTF ≈ 0.08 figure, extracting GeMAPS/eGeMAPS (a much smaller 18–88 feature set vs. the full ~27k-feature configuration the RTF figure was measured on) from short clips (seconds to tens of seconds) should be extremely cheap — well within "run on every turn, on CPU, in a real-time voice pipeline" territory, though the exact RTF for the GeMAPS-only configuration specifically (smaller than the full config benchmarked) was not directly confirmed and is inferred to be at least as fast.
- The overlap between GeMAPS/eGeMAPS's 18/88 features and Pon-Barry's hand-picked feature list (F0 descriptive stats, intensity descriptive stats, pause/rate features) is substantial — F0 min/max/mean/SD/range/slope and RMS-energy stats are present in both — suggesting GeMAPS/eGeMAPS is a reasonable, standardized, off-the-shelf substitute for re-implementing Pon-Barry-style custom feature extraction from scratch.

### Gaps
- Could not verify from available snippets whether any published study has run GeMAPS/eGeMAPS specifically against hesitation/uncertainty labels (as opposed to general emotion-category or arousal/valence labels) — the arousal-specific validation found here is from the general SER literature, not a hesitation/uncertainty-specific validation. This is an inferential bridge (arousal-relevant features are plausibly uncertainty-relevant) rather than a directly confirmed result, and should be flagged as such in the synthesis.
- Could not confirm the exact current licensing terms (openSMILE has had license changes across versions — some sources note free for research but commercial licensing may differ for the latest major version) from retrieved snippets; "free/open-source" should be checked against the current openSMILE 3.x license terms before being stated as unconditionally true for commercial/production use in Boli.
- Could not find a direct RTF/latency benchmark for the GeMAPS or eGeMAPS configuration specifically (only the general/full-feature-set RTF of 0.08 was found) — the inference that the smaller config is at least as fast is reasonable but unconfirmed by a specific cited benchmark.

## Key Question 6: Generalizability — Universal Prosodic Correlates vs. Study-Population-Specific Findings

### Takeaway
There is a layered picture: the broad direction of several core cues (falling/low pitch and reduced pitch variability associated with confidence/certainty; longer pauses, slower rate, and filled pauses associated with uncertainty/hesitation) recurs across independent studies and is partially supported by a large-scale, explicitly cross-population perception study (Goupil et al., *Nature Communications* 2021) that found no in-group advantage and language-independence for the certainty/honesty prosodic signature. But most of the quantitative, fine-grained findings (the specific 91% figure, the exact additive-effect ordering in Kirkland et al., the RMSE=1.6 regression result) come from single studies on specific, narrow populations (English-speaking, often elicited/lab-recorded for Pon-Barry; French conversational for Wottawa et al.; synthesized-speech perception for Kirkland et al.) and have not been shown to replicate across languages/cultures/corpora within this literature itself.

### Cited Findings
- A broad secondary synthesis states: "doubtful utterances typically exhibit longer response latencies, more pauses, slower speech, rising terminal intonation, lower or less stable intensity, and greater fundamental-frequency variability" as a general characterization of uncertain speech across the literature. — [WebSearch synthesis, general literature summary](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2008/ponbarry08_interspeech.pdf)
- Goupil, Ponsot, Richardson, Reyes, Aucouturier et al., "Listeners' perceptions of the certainty and honesty of a speaker are associated with a common prosodic signature," *Nature Communications* (2021): using a **data-driven reverse-correlation method**, the authors found that listener judgments of both a speaker's certainty and honesty are driven by a **common prosodic signature** across pitch, duration, and loudness; **falling intonations were perceived as more certain/honest**; critically, the paper reports **no in-group advantage** in perceiving this reliability signature, and frames the signature as perceived "independently from individuals' conceptual knowledge and native language" — i.e., explicit evidence for a cross-linguistic/cross-cultural (not just cross-individual) component to certainty-prosody perception. — [Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-20649-4); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33558510/); [PMC full text](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7870677/) (note: a Publisher Correction was issued, [PubMed correction record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34580302/) / [PMC correction](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8476622/) — the correction's content/scope could not be confirmed from available snippets, so the underlying finding should be checked against the corrected version before being cited as final)
- A general cross-linguistic emotional-prosody synthesis notes: "certain acoustic patterns are largely consistent across languages, suggesting the existence of universal acoustic correlates of emotion, yet... each language's phonological and rhythmic structure constrains how these acoustic parameters are expressed and perceived" and that prosodic features "distinguish speech patterns within languages reasonably well and to a more limited extent across languages" — i.e., a within-language-strong, cross-language-weaker pattern of generalization for emotional/affective prosody broadly (not hesitation-specific, but the closest general finding available). — [WebSearch synthesis of cross-linguistic prosody literature]
- The population/elicitation context differs sharply across the three core studies in this report: Pon-Barry (2008/2009) uses the Harvard Uncertainty Speech Corpus (English, apparently task-elicited certainty judgments); Wottawa et al. (2020) uses the NCCFr corpus (French, naturalistic conversational speech); Kirkland et al. (2022) uses synthesized speech with experimentally controlled prosody (not natural human speech at all, for the stimulus side) and unspecified listener-population characteristics for the perception-rating side. — [synthesized across all primary-paper citations above]

### Inferences
- The directional "low/falling pitch + stable intensity + fast, fluent timing = confident; high/variable pitch + more pauses + filled pauses + slower or disfluent timing = uncertain" pattern is corroborated across at least three independent lines of evidence (Pon-Barry's corpus correlation study, Kirkland et al.'s controlled perception study, and Goupil et al.'s cross-population reverse-correlation study) despite differing methods, languages, and populations — this convergence is a reasonably strong basis for treating the *qualitative direction* of these core prosodic cues (F0 level/variability, pause/rate, filled-pause presence) as likely to generalize reasonably well, i.e., closer to "probably broadly robust" than "study-specific artifact."
- However, the *quantitative specifics* (exact accuracy numbers, exact RMSE, exact effect-size ordering) should be treated as population/corpus-specific and not assumed to transfer to a different language, speaker population, or recording condition (e.g., Boli's actual deployment context) without new validation — none of the quantitative headline numbers in this literature (91%, RMSE 1.6, the additive-effect ordering) have been shown in available sources to replicate outside their original study.
- The elicited-vs-naturalistic distinction matters: Pon-Barry's apparently task-elicited uncertainty may differ systematically from Wottawa et al.'s naturalistic conversational hesitation (e.g., elicited uncertainty might be more "performed"/exaggerated, naturalistic hesitation more subtle and intertwined with disfluency/filler patterns) — the fact that both nonetheless find F0/timing/pause-type cues suggests some robustness, but Boli's actual target domain (presumably naturalistic spontaneous speech in an interactive voice system) is architecturally closer to the Wottawa et al. setting than to Pon-Barry's, which should inform which study's specifics are weighted more heavily.

### Gaps
- Could not find any single study in this literature that directly tests cross-linguistic transfer of a trained hesitation/uncertainty/confidence prosody classifier (e.g., train on English, test on French, or vice versa) — the "universal vs. population-specific" question is answered here only indirectly, by noting qualitative convergence across separately-conducted single-language studies, not by a direct transfer experiment. This is a genuine open gap in the literature as surveyed.
- Could not confirm the scope/content of the Goupil et al. Nature Communications Publisher Correction (2021) — flagged above; this should be checked before citing the "no in-group advantage" finding as the paper's final, uncorrected claim.
- No information was found in search results on how these prosodic correlates might vary with speaker demographic factors (age, gender, L1 vs. L2 speaker status) within a given language/corpus — a relevant gap for any production system expected to work across a demographically diverse user base.
- Found only one (very recent, not yet detailed in retrieved snippets) directly relevant 2024–2026 follow-up trail: general references to vocal cues and uncertainty/prosody awareness inside LLM/voice-assistant research surveys (e.g., discussions of auditory awareness benchmarks for large audio-language models, and a described observation that "speech rate changes when individuals are unsure... either slowing down as they think or speeding up due to nervousness," suggesting rate effects may be more context-dependent/bidirectional than Kirkland et al.'s simple "slow rate = less confident" finding implies) — but none of these were confirmed as direct citation-trail follow-ups specifically building on Pon-Barry, Wottawa, or Kirkland by name, so this should be treated as thin, suggestive context rather than confirmed continuity of the research line into the 2023–2026 period.

## Overall Note on Reproducibility/Establishment Status (cross-cutting)

- **Well-established / broadly corroborated:** the general direction that F0 level/stability, intensity stability, pause frequency/duration, speaking rate, and filled-pause presence all carry confidence/uncertainty-relevant information — corroborated across Pon-Barry (2008/2009), Wottawa et al. (2020), Kirkland et al. (2022), and Goupil et al. (2021), using different methods, corpora, and (in Goupil et al.'s case) cross-population listener samples. The GeMAPS/eGeMAPS standardization of essentially this same feature family, and its specifically strong validated relationship to **arousal** (not valence) across "six major affective speech databases," is also well-established in the broader SER literature.
- **Single-study / not yet independently replicated:** the specific numeric results — Pon-Barry & Shieber's 91% target-vs-control word identification accuracy (67.44% baseline), Wottawa et al.'s RMSE 1.6 continuous-hesitation regression result, and Kirkland et al.'s precise additive-effect ordering (filler > F0 ≈ rate) — are each reported in exactly one paper apiece in the sources reviewed here, with no independent replication found. These should be presented in any downstream synthesis as "best available point estimates from the originating study," not as settled, cross-validated facts.
- **Computational feasibility:** established and favorable — openSMILE is free/open-source, CPU-only-capable, and benchmarked at RTF ≈ 0.08 (much faster than real-time) even for its largest feature configuration; GeMAPS (18 features) and eGeMAPS (88 features) are far smaller than that benchmarked configuration, so extracting either from short voice-AI clips on CPU is very likely cheap and practical, though a GeMAPS/eGeMAPS-specific latency benchmark was not directly located.
