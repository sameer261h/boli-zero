# Speech Emotion Recognition (SER) Landscape: Datasets, Modeling Approaches, and the Categorical-vs-Continuous Validity Debate

## What are IEMOCAP, MSP-Podcast, CREMA-D, and RAVDESS? (nature, categories, "fear" coverage, limitations)

### Takeaway
All four widely-used SER benchmarks except MSP-Podcast are acted/elicited studio recordings, not naturalistic conversational speech; all four (including MSP-Podcast) do include "fear" as a label, but acted-corpus performance is known to generalize poorly to real, spontaneous, task-oriented speech — within-dataset accuracy on scripted material is inflated by lexical/script overlap rather than genuine emotion signal.

### Cited Findings
- **IEMOCAP** (Interactive Emotional Dyadic Motion Capture): 151 recorded dyadic conversation sessions (302 videos total), performed by 10 actors in 5 sessions, combining **scripted** dialogs and **improvised** (hypothetical-scenario) dialogs explicitly designed to elicit target emotions — i.e., acted/elicited, not naturalistic. — [IEMOCAP original paper (Busso et al. 2008)](https://sail.usc.edu/iemocap/Busso_2008_iemocap.pdf); [hyper.ai dataset page](https://hyper.ai/en/datasets/16297)
- IEMOCAP annotation covers up to 9 categorical labels depending on the source describing it: anger, excitement, **fear**, sadness, surprise, frustration, happiness, disappointment, and neutral (various papers also describe a reduced 6-basic-emotion framing: happiness, sadness, anger, surprise, **fear**, disgust, plus frustrated/excited/neutral). Labels are assigned via majority vote of 3–4 evaluators. — [hyper.ai dataset page](https://hyper.ai/en/datasets/16297); [Busso et al. 2008](https://sail.usc.edu/iemocap/Busso_2008_iemocap.pdf)
- IEMOCAP's own creators published a follow-up methodological study, "Scripted dialogs versus improvisation: Lessons learned about emotional elicitation techniques from the IEMOCAP database," examining how much the elicitation method itself (scripted vs. improvised) shapes the emotional signal captured — a tacit acknowledgment that elicitation technique confounds the "emotion" being measured. — [ResearchGate summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221492217_Scripted_dialogs_versus_improvisation_Lessons_learned_about_emotional_elicitation_techniques_from_the_IEMOCAP_database)
- Methodological critique: "the scripted portion of IEMOCAP yields high within-dataset accuracy but poor cross-dataset generalization because of strong sentence overlap and lexical redundancy," with researchers recommending a stricter speaker-and-script split or **discarding scripted data altogether**; IEMOCAP also "lacks an established nominal test set," so published accuracy numbers across papers are not directly comparable. — [arXiv:2304.00860](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.00860)
- General acted-corpus critique (applies to IEMOCAP among others): "IEMOCAP relies on acted emotional expressions, which may not fully capture the complexity and subtlety of emotions in natural, spontaneous speech, and this dataset bias could limit generalizability to real-world scenarios where emotions are often more nuanced, mixed, or ambiguously expressed." Performance "significantly decreases when models developed using such databases are applied in real-life applications where a blend of emotions is observed." — [Springer systematic review/meta-analysis 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-025-11197-8)
- **MSP-Podcast**: over 400 hours of **naturalistic** audio pulled from podcasts/digital media under permissive licenses, with an ML-based pipeline selecting segments that maximize emotional diversity and balance across gender, speaker identity, and recording context. Explicitly built to overcome the acted-speech limitation of corpora like IEMOCAP/CREMA-D/RAVDESS by capturing natural interactions rather than performed ones. — [emergentmind MSP-Podcast overview](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/msp-podcast-corpus); [MSP lab corpus page](https://lab-msp.com/MSP/MSP-Podcast.html)
- MSP-Podcast's categorical label set (used in the MSP-Podcast SER Challenge) covers 8 classes: anger, happiness, sadness, **fear**, surprise, contempt, disgust, and neutral, plus an open "other" option to reduce forced-choice bias. It also provides **dimensional** labels (valence, arousal, dominance) on a 5-point Likert scale alongside the categorical ones — making it one of the few major corpora with both label types natively. — [search synthesis citing MSP-Podcast SER Challenge docs](https://lab-msp.com/MSP-Podcast_Competition/SERB/); [emergentmind MSP-Podcast overview](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/msp-podcast-corpus)
- **CREMA-D** (Crowd-sourced Emotional Multimodal Actors Dataset): 7,442 audio-visual clips from 91 actors (48 male, 43 female, ages 20–74, multiple ethnicities), each speaking 12 fixed sentences under one of six emotion categories: **Anger, Disgust, Fear, Happy, Neutral, Sad** (no surprise), each at one of four intensity levels. Labels come from 2,443 crowdsourced raters each judging 90 clips (audio-only/video-only/audiovisual). This is acted (actor-performed) speech on fixed scripted sentences. — [IEEE CREMA-D paper](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6849440/); [TensorFlow Datasets catalog entry](https://www.tensorflow.org/datasets/catalog/crema_d)
- **RAVDESS** (Ryerson Audio-Visual Database of Emotional Speech and Song): 7,356 recordings from 24 professional actors (12 male, 12 female). Speech portion covers 8 categories: neutral, calm, happy, sad, angry, **fearful**, disgusted, surprised, each at normal/strong intensity (no strong intensity for neutral); the song portion covers a reduced 6: calm, happy, sad, angry, fearful, neutral. Entirely acted/performed, fixed statements. — [search synthesis of RAVDESS structure](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Gray1y/RAVDESS_preprocessed_npy)
- Cross-cutting limitation noted across multiple reviews: SER research broadly suffers from "lack of emotions standardisation, language and user dependent systems instead of independent ones, databases with controlled emotions (studio recorded, full blown and/or interpreted by actors), and several unstandardized features." — [PMC systematic review, speech properties/feature extraction](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7962835/)
- Annotation-validity concern that applies across all four corpora: "Annotators may label the emotion based on the emotional expression which resides in the conversational context or other modality but is not apparent in the given speech utterance, so some of the emotional labels may not be reliable" — i.e., even naturalistic corpora like MSP-Podcast inherit label noise from raters inferring emotion from context/video rather than audio alone. — [PMC, Speech Emotion Recognition Incorporating Relative Difficulty and Labeling Reliability](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11244487/)

### Inferences
- All three "classic" benchmark corpora (IEMOCAP, CREMA-D, RAVDESS) are acted/performed speech on fixed or researcher-designed scripts; MSP-Podcast is the notable naturalistic exception and is explicitly positioned by its creators as a response to the ecological-validity problem of the acted corpora. For a conversational, task-oriented, real-world voice-AI system (like Boli), the acted corpora's "fear"/anger/happy labels describe actors' performance of emotion under instruction, not spontaneous internal states — a critical disanalogy to Boli's use case.
- "Fear" is present as a label category in every one of the four corpora, but it is almost always the rarest/least natural category to elicit authentically (performing "fear" convincingly on cue, or finding genuine fear in podcast audio, is inherently harder than anger/happy/neutral) — this is a strong reason to treat "fear" classification output from models trained on these corpora with particular skepticism in a product context.
- IEMOCAP's lack of a standard train/test split and demonstrated script-overlap inflation of accuracy means that published SER leaderboard numbers (often >70% WA) likely overstate real generalization capability; any benchmark number quoted from IEMOCAP should be treated as an upper bound under favorable, non-independent conditions rather than a realistic deployment estimate.

### Gaps
- Could not find a canonical, single authoritative count/list reconciling the "9 emotions" vs "6 basic + 3" framings of IEMOCAP's label scheme — different secondary sources describe this inconsistently; the original Busso et al. 2008 paper would need a full read (not just abstract/snippet) to resolve this precisely.
- Did not find specific numeric details on inter-rater agreement (e.g., Fleiss' kappa) for IEMOCAP, CREMA-D, or RAVDESS to quantify how reliable the ground-truth categorical labels are even within acted material.

## What is the INTERSPEECH ComParE series? Tasks and standard baselines

### Takeaway
ComParE (2009–present, formalized as an annual sub-challenge series since 2013) is the field's standard venue for defining new computational paralinguistics tasks — spanning emotion, conflict, cognitive/physical load, sincerity/deception, nativeness, atypical affect, and more — and it established the openSMILE-derived feature set (culminating in the 6,373-feature ComParE acoustic feature set, paired with an SVM baseline) as the long-running standard baseline methodology for the field.

### Cited Findings
- ComParE challenges have run regularly since 2009, with the INTERSPEECH 2013 edition being "the first of its kind" in its current branded format, providing "a unified test-bed for social signals such as laughter in speech," introducing **conflict** in group discussions as a task, covering **autism** manifestations in speech, and revisiting **emotion** with twelve enacted emotional states. — [dcs.gla.ac.uk ComParE 2013 paper](https://www.dcs.gla.ac.uk/~vincia/papers/compare.pdf)
- **2014**: Cognitive & Physical Load sub-challenge — ternary-level classification of cognitive load from acoustics, with openSMILE-derived ComParE baseline features and baseline results provided to participants. — [ResearchGate, INTERSPEECH 2014 ComParE paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259889707_The_INTERSPEECH_2014_computational_paralinguistics_challenge_Cognitive_physical_load)
- **2015**: tasks included emotion, speaker traits, conflict and autism (revisited), eating condition, nativeness, and Parkinson's detection. — [ResearchGate, INTERSPEECH 2015 ComParE paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/274251412_The_INTERSPEECH_2015_Computational_Paralinguistics_Challenge_Nativeness_Parkinson's_Eating_Condition)
- **2016**: three new tasks — Deception Sub-Challenge (identifying deceptive speech), Sincerity Sub-Challenge (perceived sincerity), Native Language Sub-Challenge (recognizing speakers' native language from non-native English speech). — [ResearchGate, INTERSPEECH 2016 ComParE paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/307889334_The_INTERSPEECH_2016_Computational_Paralinguistics_Challenge_Deception_Sincerity_and_Native_Language)
- **2018** (10th-anniversary edition): four new tasks, including an **Atypical Affect Sub-Challenge** specifically for recognizing emotion in speech from disabled speakers — an implicit acknowledgment that standard SER models/features don't transfer to atypical speech populations. — [Interspeech 2018 program](http://interspeech2018.org/program-special-sessions.html)
- **2020**: three new tasks — Breathing sub-challenge (predicting respiratory belt signal from speech), Elderly sub-challenge (classifying arousal/valence in elderly speakers), Mask sub-challenge (detecting whether a speaker is wearing a mask). — [search synthesis of INTERSPEECH 2020 ComParE](http://www.interspeech2020.org/index.php?m=content&c=index&a=lists&catid=313)
- Standard baseline methodology: the **ComParE acoustic feature set**, generated by the **openSMILE** toolkit, comprises **6,373 static (turn-level) features** derived from functionals (statistics: mean, std, percentiles, slopes, etc.) computed over low-level-descriptor contours (F0, loudness, spectral flux, formants, jitter/shimmer/HNR voice-quality measures, etc.), typically paired with an **SVM** (often RBF-kernel) classifier as the official challenge baseline; some systems first select the best ~1,000 of the 6,373 features before SVM training. — [search synthesis on ComParE 2016 feature set](https://rdrr.io/github/humlab-speech/superassp/man/ComParE_2016.html); [audeering openSMILE 3.0 announcement](https://www.audeering.com/introducing-opensmile-3-0/)
- eGeMAPS (the extended Geneva Minimalistic Acoustic Parameter Set), a theory-driven, compact 88-feature subset of openSMILE descriptors (F0, loudness, spectral parameters, MFCCs, jitter, shimmer, HNR, formants), is the more interpretable/compact alternative openSMILE baseline frequently used alongside or instead of the full 6,373-feature ComParE set. — [search synthesis on eGeMAPS features](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.14662)

### Inferences
- The ComParE series' own evolution — moving from basic emotion (2013) to conflict, cognitive load, deception, sincerity, nativeness, atypical affect, breathing, and mask-wearing — reflects an implicit field-wide move away from treating "basic categorical emotion" as the only or most tractable paralinguistic construct, toward many narrower, more operationally-defined constructs. This supports Boli's intuition that operational/task-specific constructs (hesitation, uncertainty, cognitive load) are a well-precedented alternative framing in the computational paralinguistics literature, not a fringe choice.
- The persistence of the openSMILE/eGeMAPS SVM baseline as "the standard" for over a decade, even after deep learning and SSL models became dominant elsewhere in speech, suggests these classical acoustic features remain a credible, computationally cheap baseline specifically for paralinguistic (not just emotion) tasks — relevant to assessing whether Boli needs heavy SSL infrastructure for hesitation/uncertainty detection or whether openSMILE/eGeMAPS-style features suffice.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the specific ComParE 2013 "conflict sub-challenge" baseline accuracy numbers or methodology details beyond task description; a full read of the original Schuller et al. 2013 ComParE paper would be needed for baseline UAR figures per task.
- Could not confirm whether a dedicated "uncertainty" or "hesitation" sub-challenge has ever been run under the ComParE banner — none of the listed years (2013–2020 covered in search results) explicitly names "hesitation" or "uncertainty" as a sub-challenge; this may be a genuine gap in the paralinguistics-challenge literature that Boli's work could be positioned against.

## Modern self-supervised speech representations (wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, WavLM) vs. classical acoustic features for paralinguistic/affective tasks

### Takeaway
Self-supervised representations generally outperform classical openSMILE/eGeMAPS features on full emotion-classification benchmarks and set new state-of-the-art on most paralinguistic tasks, but the superiority is neither uniform nor statistically decisive across all SSL variants, and there is no direct evidence found that SSL embeddings are categorically better than classical acoustic/prosodic features specifically for narrower hesitation/uncertainty-style constructs (where the hesitation literature still relies on explainable prosodic features like filled-pause detection, pitch, and speech rate).

### Cited Findings
- On IEMOCAP, fine-tuned wav2vec 2.0/HuBERT models reach **79.58% weighted accuracy (speaker-dependent)** and **73.01% weighted accuracy (speaker-independent)**. — [arXiv:2111.02735](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02735)
- Comparative analysis found HuBERT "demonstrates slightly better generalization across various emotions, with an overall accuracy of 0.91 compared to 0.89 for Wav2Vec 2.0," and that "HuBERT turned out to be a better self-supervised encoder compared to wav2vec 2.0 for both speech emotion recognition and speaker verification tasks." — [search synthesis, HuBERT/wav2vec2 comparison](https://github.com/locmai27/CISC473-speech-emotion-recognition)
- Regarding WavLM vs. wav2vec 2.0: "the difference between WavLM and wav2vec 2.0 was not statistically significant, meaning that WavLM should be interpreted as the variant with the highest average performance, but not as a model that is unequivocally superior to wav2vec 2.0 in a statistical sense." — [search synthesis citing SSL comparison study]
- **emotion2vec** (a WavLM-related SSL model purpose-built for emotion) achieves **71.79%** weighted accuracy on IEMOCAP, outperforming data2vec 2.0 (68.58%) and showing "consistent improvements among 10 different languages of speech emotion recognition datasets," with UMAP visualizations showing clearer emotion-cluster separation than WavLM or data2vec. — [ACL Anthology, emotion2vec](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.931.pdf)
- On EMO-DB specifically, WavLM Large reaches 92.67% WA vs. wav2vec 2.0 base's 83.14% WA — illustrating that relative SSL-model rankings are highly benchmark-dependent and not consistent across corpora. — [search synthesis, WavLM/wav2vec2 EMO-DB comparison]
- Universal Paralinguistic Speech Representations (Conformer-based SSL, Google) "set a new state-of-the-art performance on 7/9 paralinguistic speech tasks using only embeddings averaged across time, and using only linear models on those embeddings, substantially outperforming other speech representations despite not using labels for training" — direct evidence that generic SSL representations beat classical/handcrafted features on most (not all) paralinguistic tasks when only a linear probe is used. — [arXiv:2110.04621](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.04621)
- Countervailing caution: "it remains unknown whether, why, and how self-supervised losses contribute to downstream paralinguistic tasks other than ASR," and SSL representations "are harder to explain than handcrafted features as they are not designed by human knowledge of speech signals" — i.e., even where SSL wins on accuracy, interpretability/explainability is worse, which matters for a system (Boli) that wants to reason about *why* a signal fired rather than treat it as an opaque classifier. — [arXiv:2110.04621](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.04621)
- For the specific hesitation/uncertainty construct (distinct from full emotion classification), the active literature still centers on classical, explainable, operational acoustic-prosodic features — response latency, pause count/duration, speech/articulation rate (via syllable-nucleus detection), rising terminal intonation, intensity (level/stability), F0 variability, and compact voice-quality descriptors (jitter, shimmer) — rather than SSL embeddings. — [ResearchGate, filled-pause/confidence perception study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/363646845_Where's_the_uh_hesitation_The_interplay_between_filled_pause_location_speech_rate_and_fundamental_frequency_in_perception_of_confidence); [SAIL/USC, Audio and ASR-based Filled Pause Detection](https://sail.usc.edu/publications/files/Chatziagapi-ACII2022.pdf)
- Filled-pause/hesitation detection work explicitly reports that "acoustic models generally provide stronger frame-level evidence" than text-only models, and that multimodal (acoustic+text) combination yields additional gains — again pointing to acoustic/prosodic (not necessarily SSL-embedding) features as the operative signal for hesitation specifically. — [SAIL/USC, Audio and ASR-based Filled Pause Detection](https://sail.usc.edu/publications/files/Chatziagapi-ACII2022.pdf)

### Inferences
- There is reasonably strong evidence that SSL representations (wav2vec2/HuBERT/WavLM and emotion-specialized variants like emotion2vec) outperform classical openSMILE/eGeMAPS features for *full categorical emotion classification* benchmarks, but the ranking among SSL variants themselves is inconsistent across corpora and not always statistically significant — so "which SSL model is best" is corpus-dependent, not a settled fact.
- No direct head-to-head benchmark was found comparing SSL embeddings against openSMILE/eGeMAPS specifically on a *hesitation/uncertainty* classification task (as opposed to full emotion). The hesitation-specific literature reviewed here uses classical acoustic-prosodic features and filled-pause detectors, not generic SSL embeddings — this is suggestive (not conclusive) that simpler, more interpretable acoustic features remain the practical standard for this narrower, more behaviorally-defined construct, consistent with Boli's inclination to prefer operational signals over emotion-embedding black boxes.

### Gaps
- Could not find a study that directly benchmarks wav2vec2/HuBERT/WavLM embeddings against eGeMAPS/openSMILE on a hesitation-or-uncertainty-specific labeled task (as opposed to general paralinguistics tasks bundled in challenges like the Conformer paper's 7/9 benchmark, whose specific task list wasn't fully enumerated in the search results). This would need a dedicated follow-up search of papers specifically on "confidence detection using SSL embeddings" vs. "confidence detection using openSMILE."
- Could not confirm the exact 9 paralinguistic tasks used in the Google Conformer SSL paper's benchmark (which 2 of 9 tasks SSL did *not* win on) — worth a follow-up read of the full arXiv:2110.04621 paper.

## Cross-speaker, cross-language, and cross-cultural generalization in SER; evidence of dialect/accent confounded with emotion; Indian-language evidence

### Takeaway
There is direct, citable evidence that (a) SER accuracy drops substantially when moving cross-lingually/cross-corpus even for state-of-the-art models, (b) accent and dialect are documented confounds that degrade SER and bias misclassification (including a specific finding that a regional UK accent was misclassified as more severe "cognitive decline" in a related speech-based model), and (c) researchers explicitly flag that emotional expression "is strongly rooted in language, accent, dialect and cultural background," with Indian-language/accent SER specifically flagged as understudied and underperforming relative to English — but hard, large-scale quantitative evidence of Indian-accent-speakers being systematically misclassified as more "emotional"/"uncertain" by English-trained SER models was not found in this search pass (a gap, not a null result).

### Cited Findings
- "Cross-language emotion recognition is feasible, but with notably lower accuracy than monolingual recognition." — [search synthesis, cross-lingual SER overview](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.00357)
- Direct study "Cross-Lingual Speech Emotion Recognition: Humans vs. Self-Supervised Models": SSL models outperform humans when trained/tested within their native language, but **both humans and SSL models show a significant accuracy drop in the cross-lingual setting**; humans retain a relative advantage over models cross-lingually; with "appropriate knowledge transfer" models can approach native-speaker-comparable performance; the study explicitly demonstrates "the significant effect of dialect on SER for individuals without prior linguistic and paralinguistic background." — [arXiv:2409.16920 (via search synthesis; direct PDF fetch blocked by egress proxy)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.16920)
- General accent-bias finding: "Emotional speech has certain variations due to accents, and the presence of accents degrades the performance of speech emotion recognition (SER) systems." — [search synthesis, accent/dialect bias in SER](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00034-024-02687-1)
- Documented misclassification harm in an adjacent speech-based clinical model: "speakers exhibiting a South Yorkshire accent were more frequently misclassified as experiencing more severe cognitive decline" — a concrete, citable example of a regional accent being conflated with a clinical/behavioral construct by a speech model (cognitive decline, not emotion per se, but methodologically the same confound mechanism SER is vulnerable to). — [search synthesis citing accent-bias cognitive-decline study]
- "Most speech emotion recognition research is biased toward English-speaking adults, which hinders its applicability to other demographic groups in different languages and age groups." — [search synthesis, cross-lingual SER bias overview]
- Indian-context-specific claim: "The way an emotion is exhibited is strongly rooted in language, accent, dialect and cultural background, and a model trained to identify emotion on English datasets might not be able to identify emotions in Indic language speech utterances." — [arXiv:1912.10458, Emotion Recognition from Speech (survey)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.10458)
- Related (ASR, not SER, but same accent-confound mechanism, and specifically Indian): "Indian accents are among the hardest for speech recognition models to handle," with Whisper-large-v3's Indian-accent word error rate (19.0%) worse than Whisper-small's (17.6%) — a counterintuitive finding that scaling up a model does not fix accent bias and can worsen it. — [search synthesis, Indian-accent ASR bias study (arXiv:2512.10967 context)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.10967)
- Indian-language SER resources exist but are fragmented and small relative to English: ERIL (multilingual Indian-language emotion classifier tested on Hindi, Gujarati, Marathi, Punjabi, Bangla, Tamil, Oriya, Kannada, Assamese, Telugu, reporting ~95% average accuracy in-sample); BhavVani (~13 hours, 8,734 Hindi utterances); IITKGP-SEHSC (Hindi) and IITKGP-SESC (Telugu) corpora; a Telugu dialect speech dataset for dialect classification (not emotion); cross-corpus SER work spanning Hindi, Urdu, Telugu, Kannada using deep CNNs. — [Springer, ERIL paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11277-022-09829-1); [search synthesis, Indian-language SER dataset landscape](https://www.iieta.org/journals/ria/paper/10.18280/ria.380318)
- General accent/dialect bias beyond India: "Accent and dialect bias lead to poorer recognition for non-native or regional accents, including non-American accents and Scottish English." — [search synthesis, accent/dialect bias overview]

### Inferences
- The direct evidence base strongly supports Boli's working concern: dialect/accent is a documented, citable confound in both SER and adjacent speech-based inference tasks (clinical, ASR), with at least one concrete example (South Yorkshire accent → over-predicted cognitive decline) of exactly the failure mode Boli is worried about (natural speech patterns being misread as a different internal/behavioral state). The mechanism generalizes: nothing about SER's architecture prevents the same confound from mapping an Indian-English speaker's natural prosody/rhythm onto spurious "emotion" or "uncertainty" predictions, even though a large-scale study demonstrating this specific outcome for Indian English and SER (as opposed to ASR WER) was not located in this pass.
- The scarcity and small scale of Indian-language SER corpora (hours in the tens, not hundreds, vs. MSP-Podcast's 400+ hours) relative to English-centric corpora means models trained predominantly on Western/English acted or naturalistic data have essentially no exposure to Indian prosodic norms during training — making cross-cultural misclassification a structural likelihood rather than merely a hypothetical risk.

### Gaps
- Could not find a published study that directly measures SER-specific (not ASR-specific, not cognitive-decline-specific) misclassification rates for Indian-English or Indian-language speakers evaluated on Western-trained SER models — this is the single most relevant gap for Boli's specific concern and would benefit from a dedicated follow-up search (e.g., "Indian accent speech emotion recognition confusion matrix," "code-switched Hindi-English SER bias").
- Could not access the full text of the cross-lingual humans-vs-SSL paper (arXiv:2409.16920) or the EUSIPCO 2026 cross-lingual-generalization paper directly — both the arxiv.org and eurasip.org domains were blocked by the network egress proxy in this environment, so findings above rely on search-engine snippet synthesis rather than primary-source verification of exact accuracy numbers. A follow-up with proxy/network access to arXiv would materially strengthen this section.

## Scientific grounding of categorical emotion labels from voice vs. dimensional/operational alternatives

### Takeaway
There is a substantial and credible body of affective-science and NLP-ethics literature arguing that categorical "basic emotion" labels (anger, fear, happy, sad, etc.) inferred from voice alone rest on contested theoretical foundations (Basic Emotion Theory) with weak, culturally-variable empirical support, and several authors go further to call commercial voice/facial emotion-AI "pseudoscientific" — while other researchers argue categorical labels remain defensible as a *communicative/linguistic* convenience (because humans talk about emotions this way) without that usage constituting an endorsement of BET as a scientific claim about internal states; dimensional (valence/arousal) and task-specific operational constructs (hesitation, uncertainty, cognitive load) are a recognized, credible, less theory-laden alternative already embedded in corpora (MSP-Podcast) and challenge series (ComParE) discussed above.

### Cited Findings
- Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez & Pollak (2019), "Emotional Expressions Reconsidered: Challenges to Inferring Emotion From Human Facial Movements" (*Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 20(1), 1–68): surveys the scientific evidence for the "common view" that discrete emotion categories (anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise) are reliably readable from external expression, and concludes that "how people communicate emotions varies substantially across cultures, situations, and even across people within a single situation, with similar facial movements variably expressing different emotions" — i.e., the expression-to-emotion mapping is not one-to-one or stable, undermining the premise that fixed external signals reliably reveal discrete internal emotional categories. (This paper is about facial movement specifically, but is the canonical citation underlying parallel critiques of vocal-emotion inference, since it attacks the same Basic-Emotion-Theory premise that SER "fear"/"anger" labels rely on.) — [DOI: 10.1177/1529100619832930](http://eprints.gla.ac.uk/178385/7/178385.pdf)
- Mohammad (2022), "Ethics Sheet for Automatic Emotion Recognition and Sentiment Analysis" (*Computational Linguistics* 48(2), 239–278): argues it "makes sense for some NLP work to focus on categorical emotions... because people often talk about their emotions in terms of these concepts" and "most human languages have words for these concepts," but explicitly cautions: "work on categorical emotions by itself is not an endorsement of the BET," and "do not refer to some emotions as basic emotions, unless you mean to convey your belief in the BET," warning that "careless endorsement of theories can lead to the perpetuation of ideas that are actively harmful (such as suggesting we can determine internal state from outward appearance — physiognomy)." A related synthesis of this work states "our current understanding of valid automated emotion inference does not validate an appeal to the thematic level of emotion categories." — [ACL Anthology / MIT Press](https://aclanthology.org/2022.cl-2.1.pdf)
- Direct "pseudoscience" framing: commentary citing Paul Ekman himself — foundational figure behind Basic Emotion Theory and facial-coding systems — stating "most of what I was seeing was what I would call pseudoscience" regarding commercial emotion-recognition technology; broader commentary states "AI-powered emotion recognition doesn't work as companies claim, and its lack of scientific grounding makes this technology extremely problematic," and that "the majority of 'emotional AI' technologies are built on Basic Emotion Theory, which even though it has a shaky scientific grounding, is more amenable to adoption in AI systems" (i.e., BET is used because it's engineering-convenient, not because it's been validated). — [The Algorithmic Bridge, "AI Emotion Recognition Is a Pseudoscientific Multi-Billion Dollar Industry"](https://www.thealgorithmicbridge.com/p/ai-emotion-recognition-is-a-pseudoscientific)
- Policy-level echo of the same critique: Access Now's position paper explicitly calls to "Prohibit emotion recognition in the Artificial Intelligence Act," reflecting that the scientific-validity critique has reached EU AI-policy debate, not just academic literature. — [Access Now policy paper](https://www.accessnow.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/Prohibit-emotion-recognition-in-the-Artificial-Intelligence-Act.pdf)
- Direct evidence of poor real-world reliability specifically for *vocal* emotion recognition: a 2026 benchmark (VocalAffectBench) found "the leading model is roughly three times the random baseline but misses more than half of the clips, with average performance across models at only 35.1%," and that "current emotion embeddings used for emotion evaluation are fundamentally unreliable and structurally biased by acoustic distractors, with similarity scores severely misaligning with human perception." — [arXiv:2608.28932, VocalAffectBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28932)
- Field self-critique on motivation/practice gap: "Unrequited Emotions: Investigating the Gaps in Motivation and Practice in Speech Emotion Recognition Research" — title alone signals an explicit, recent (2026) academic critique of a disconnect between why SER research is motivated and what it actually delivers/validates in practice. — [arXiv:2604.25776](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.25776)
- The theoretical alternative — dimensional models — is substantively described: "dimensional frameworks, such as Russell's circumplex model, replace discrete categories with continuous dimensions — most commonly valence (how pleasant) and arousal (how activating) — allowing emotional states to be represented as points in a continuous space rather than as fixed labels," and "Barrett's conceptual-act framework treats valence and activation as building blocks from which specific emotion concepts emerge" (i.e., Barrett's own constructionist alternative to BET is dimensional/constructed, not categorical). — [search synthesis of categorical-vs-dimensional SER framing]
- A direct methodological note on why dimensional models are sometimes preferred operationally: "discrete categorical representations do not seek to model the subtle differences between human emotions," and categorical representation makes emotion recognition a *classification* task while dimensional representation makes it a *regression* task, with different implications for how much information survives annotator aggregation (regression/continuous aggregation of multiple raters' valence/arousal scores preserves more nuance than collapsing to a single discrete majority-vote label). — [search synthesis, categorical vs. dimensional representation tradeoffs]
- Supporting the "basic emotions" framework's continued but contested use: "Studies have served to fuel the idea of a fixed set of discrete and hypothetically 'basic' emotions, typically anger, fear, disgust, sadness, and happiness, although opinions vary," and "an emotional state can include a combination of several basic emotional states" — i.e., even sympathetic reviews concede single-label categorical ground truth may misrepresent blended real emotional states. — [PMC systematic review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7962835/)
- On acoustic correlates specifically: "prosodic features were the most investigated and showed the greatest accuracy in detecting negative emotions, stress, and cognitive load" — notably, this systematic review frames prosody's best-evidenced use case as *stress/cognitive load* (operational constructs), not fine-grained discrete emotion categories like fear vs. anger vs. disgust. — [PMC, systematic review of acoustic correlates of stress](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12289014/)

### Inferences
- There is a credible, citable, and fairly mainstream (not fringe) methodological case — spanning affective science (Barrett et al.), computational linguistics ethics (Mohammad), empirical benchmarking (VocalAffectBench), and policy (Access Now) — for treating categorical emotion labels extracted from voice (or face) alone as scientifically contested rather than settled ground truth, especially when generalized across cultures/languages/speakers. This is not merely Boli's internal hypothesis; it reflects a real, active fault line in the field with heavyweight proponents on the critical side (including Ekman himself, the original architect of basic-emotion coding).
- The strongest, most specific evidence favors prosodic/acoustic features for *operational* constructs (stress, cognitive load, hesitation/uncertainty — see hesitation section above) over fine-grained discrete emotion categories (fear vs. disgust vs. contempt), which is exactly the directional bet Boli's design is making. This is supported by convergent evidence from three independent angles: (1) ComParE's own evolution toward operational tasks (cognitive load, conflict, deception) rather than doubling down on basic emotion; (2) the systematic review finding prosody's best-evidenced accuracy is for stress/cognitive load; (3) the explicit hesitation/filled-pause literature using well-defined, validated acoustic correlates (pause, rate, F0 variability) rather than contested "basic emotion" labels.
- No source found claims categorical SER is definitively *invalid* or useless — Mohammad's position is the most balanced and arguably most citation-worthy for Boli's report: categorical emotion *labels* can be a legitimate descriptive/communicative device without requiring belief in Basic Emotion Theory as literally true, but conflating "the model output a label called fear" with "the person is experiencing the internal state of fear" is the specific inferential leap the literature says is unsupported and potentially harmful (physiognomy framing).
